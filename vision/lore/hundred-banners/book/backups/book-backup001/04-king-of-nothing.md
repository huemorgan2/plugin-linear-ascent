# Chapter 4 — The King of Nothing

Nix counted cups, because counting was the whole of the work and most of
the staying alive. Eleven cups on the board that morning: four chipped,
two mended with wire, one true silver with a swan on it that Gnarl called
the royal service, the crown treasure, the last civilized object in the
world. Nix had poured into all eleven since lamp-trim. The king had
thrown two, drunk one, and made the other eight into an audience.

"My lords," said Gnarl, to the cups. "My ladies. My esteemed and
colorful dead."

The dead were the banners. A hundred of them and more — Nix had counted
a hundred and six, then a hundred and nine, then given up; the king kept
sending guards out to steal fresh ones. They hung from poles, from the
muster-oaks, from the great Standard-Elm at the field's middle, from the
frame of the throne itself: colors of companies that no longer mustered,
kings that no longer reigned, causes carried up the tower and dropped.
In the dead air they did not fly. They hung, wept their dye into the
mud, and listened.

The throne was crates. Nix wished to be exact about that, being the one
who dusted it. Supply-crates of the old muster, stacked two high, lashed
round with banner-poles, a plank and a cushion atop, Gnarl on the
cushion, and the crown on Gnarl. The crown was the only part of it the
tower had made. It sat low on his brow, iron and thorn-brass, and it did
not come off — Nix had watched him try once, in the dark, three years
back, and had poured very carefully for a month after and said nothing,
one, two, never. From the crown the weld ran down under the king's
collar-bone and into the throne and into the field, and that was the
truth of the court: the throne was not Gnarl's seat. It was his
sentence.

"Cup," said Gnarl.

"Cup, majesty. Third of the morning, second of the good, last of the
jug." Nix poured. Fast hands, small pour, back away. He had held this
post six years by three rules: count everything, expect nothing, be
elsewhere when the cup flies.

Gnarl drank and looked out at his kingdom. The muster-meadow ran away
from the throne in trampled grass and barbed banner-thistle, past the
Long Camp's empty tents, rotted horse-lines, and cold cook-fires, to
the bluff at the far end where the stair-lift was cut into stone shaped
like a triumphal arch, and past the bluff the downs of the stolen realm
ran out to their own horizon. Ten kings' marks were carved on that
arch. Men had come to this field for a thousand years to
gather armies, and the tower had stolen it a breath before the last
gathering finished, and then — this was the joke, and Gnarl told it
every day — the tower had crowned a goblin to keep it.

"Do you know what a king is, cup-bearer?" said Gnarl.

"A king is a man the field agrees on, majesty," said Nix, because that
was the day's expected answer, one of nine, he had them numbered.

"A king," said Gnarl, "is a joke the tower tells, and the field is the
only one not laughing." He raised the swan cup to the banners.
"Patience, my dears. The house fills. The house always fills."

Out past the arch, on the gate road, the horns began.

⁂

Nix knew horns the way he knew cups. Goblin horns were short and rude
and said *run*. These were long, and they were brass, and they said
*form*. He had never heard them before, and he had heard them all his
life, in the marrow: somewhere below, a freed people was gathering, and
gathering was the one thing the tower forbade.

They came through the hedged mouth of the gate road in ranks that were
not good and did not need to be. Militia of the low floors — Nix picked
out eel-trappers, pressers with orchard-bills, miners with hammers on
their shoulders — and on the left of them, apart
from them, a company in boiled leather the color of ash, moving quiet,
killing everything, and everything they killed they killed twice.
Cullers. Even the warrens knew Cullers.

Gnarl stood up as far as the weld allowed, which was not far, and flung
out an arm to the field, and his voice went to the music-hall pitch he
saved for endings.

"Ladies and lords! The late house is IN!" He was laughing. Nix, thirty
paces back — never twenty-nine — saw that his eyes were not. "Honor-
guard! Give them the show!"

The honor-guard went. They hated him — every collar on the field hated
him, the joke crowned at their own expense, and Nix had hated him
longest and poured anyway — but the collars took them down the meadow
all the same, over-armored, under-fed, dying in the mud of a field
where Men once made kings. The fevered things of the meadow went too,
the Yoke driving all its cattle with one stick: wolves tangled in
dragging colors, courier-hounds on their dead routes, a parade-horse
screaming, riderless, huge. Where the militia's line met them it bent,
and held, and one voice held it — flat, carrying, unhurried, a
serjeant's voice with a captain under it.

"Wall. Wall. Step and *close*."

Nix stopped watching the battle then, because an arrow went through the
tent-fly above his head, and he did what six years of court had trained
into him: he dropped the cup and got behind something.

The something was a banner.

It lay where a guard had dropped it, off the pile of taken climbers'
colors Gnarl kept nearest the throne. It was grey. It
had a device Nix had dusted a hundred times and never once asked
about — a wall of squared stones, and above the wall an open hand — and
it was sewn heavy, double-heavy, war-heavy, and when Nix hauled it up
on its cracked pole and huddled in behind the cloth, an arrow hit it
and did not come through. He counted his blessings. He arrived at one.
He held onto it.

The line came up the meadow. The tents of the Long Camp went over. The
banner-poles went down like a hedge falling, a hundred dead causes
dropping into the mud, and Nix, peering around his wall of cloth,
watched the ash-grey company
reach the goblins who had thrown their weapons down at the throne-fence
and kill them where they knelt. Quick and orderly. A man with iron in
his beard walked behind the Cullers' line, not hurrying, pointing left,
pointing right, the way a farmer points at rows.

"Nothing collared leaves the field," the man said, not loudly, and
his company made it true. Nix stopped counting the dead — a thing he
had never once managed before — and it frightened him worse than the
arrows.

Then the woman with the serjeant's voice put her shoulder through the
throne-tent's lacing, and the court of the Kingsfield fell in behind
her, and the last act began.

⁂

Nix saw it from behind the grey banner, twelve paces from the throne,
which was too close and also the safest ground on the field, and both
of those were his whole life in one place, so he stayed.

She was of Men, past the middle of her life, grey woven through the
braid, a shield on her arm with the paint battered off it. Behind her
an elf with a bow half-drawn, watching everything and aiming at
nothing. At her collar-bone a splinter of crystal burned steady, and a
dry voice came off it that was not hers. *Welded to the throne,* it
said. *Root and crown. He cannot rise, and the working is a collar,
whatever shape they hammered it into.*

"Gnarl," said the woman.

"Majesty," said Gnarl, agreeably, correcting her. He had the swan cup
still, or again. His guard was dead or fled or kneeling, his banners
were down, and his sword — the great notched thing the tower had given
him, too big for any goblin to lift — stood planted in the mud, done
with. The crown sat askew. He did not
straighten it. "You've brought a crowd at last. Do you know how long
I've held this venue on promises?"

"It's over," she said. "Your garrison's broken. Whoever kneels, we
keep. Whoever runs, we let run." She said it loudly, for the field, and
Nix heard goblins put iron down behind him. "That leaves you."

"It always did," said Gnarl.

The elf came forward then, and out of his sleeve he took a skein of
pale cord, and even Nix, who knew no working grander than a steady
pour, felt his teeth ache at it. Silver script ran along the cord like
light along a wet hair.

"A Break-collar," the elf said. "Written small. Perhaps six hands
living can write one; my people paid for this the way you'd ransom a
town. Spoken once, it is spent, and the collar it is spoken over is
ended — crown, weld, root and all. We were told to spend it on
something that would matter. A king would matter."

The field had gone very quiet. The woman looked at Gnarl and her voice
came down off its wall. "No one else has to die a jailer," she said.
"You least of all. Yours were the first people taken. The tower crowned
you to mock them. Let us take the mockery off you, and be a goblin
among goblins, and live."

And Nix, twelve paces off, behind a dead company's banner, watched his
king listen to the only kind thing anyone had said to him in thirty
years, and watched him weigh it, and watched him put it down.

"A goblin among goblins," said Gnarl softly. "Madam, there are ten
thousand of those, and the tower has a use for every one." He turned
the swan cup in his fingers. "Take off the crown, and what remains?
Nothing remains. I have *been* nothing — I was nothing for years before
the tower found me, and it saw exactly what I was, and crowned me for
it. That is more than any of you ever offered." His voice climbed back
to the pitch for endings, and he spread his arms to the fallen banners
as to a full house. "I am the King of Nothing, and
nothing is a realm, my dears — it is the *only* realm the tower deals
in honestly. I'll not be pardoned out of my own punchline."

"You'll die for a joke," said the woman.

"Every king does," said Gnarl. "The good ones know it's theirs." He set
the swan cup down on the arm of the crate-throne, with care, the way
you set down the last civilized object in the world, and he took up the
great notched sword that no goblin could lift, and lifted it.

"Applause at the end," he said. "That is the whole of the contract."

She gave him a soldier's finish. One stroke, under the crown. Nix
counted it, because counting was how he wept: one.

The crown came off with him. It rolled in the mud and lay there, iron
and thorn-brass, no one's. Under the arch of the ten kings the stair-
lift's stone split its seam with a sound like a held breath let go, and
light came down the gap; and everywhere on the field, goblins felt the
Yoke go silent and stood in the wreck of their watch-fires, feeling the
whip lift for the first time in their lives. Most ran for the opened
lift, up, toward the next Warden's signal, because up was the only
direction the collar had ever taught them. A few threw the collars down
and walked away downfield, toward Roothollow, unsure how walking was
done when nobody drove you.

Nix stood behind the grey banner and did not run, one, and did not walk
away, two, and could not have said what the third thing was, only that
he was doing it.

⁂

The elf still held the skein of silver script. Half its light had gone
out of it — unsealed for the offer; once shown its work, a working like
that is spent whether it is spoken or not, and the cost stood in the
elf's face like a debt called early. He looked at the dead king, and
asked the woman a question with his eyebrows.

She was looking at Nix. At the banner, first — something had gone
through her when she saw the banner, a blade's worth of something — and
then at the goblin holding it up between himself and the world with
both hands and one knee.

"You," she said. "Cup-bearer."

"Majesty," said Nix, from habit, and could have bitten his tongue off.
"Captain. Serjeant. Whichever's biggest, that one, twice."

"Why are you still here?"

Nix considered the question. "Because it's the
first field I ever stood on where the man doing the counting wasn't
counting *me*," he said. "And because this wall stops arrows, and I've
been hiding behind worse walls all my life, and this one's better,
that's all, it's a good wall, I'd like to keep it."

The dry voice from her collar-bone spoke. *He held his post through the
battle. Forty-one climbers I have carried, and I have seen generals do
worse.*

The woman looked at the elf. The elf looked at the half-spent skein,
and shrugged with one shoulder — *it is already dying; let it die
working* — and knelt in the mud in front of a goblin cup-bearer as he
would have knelt for a king, and spoke the script off the cord.

It did not hurt. That was the part Nix told worst, ever afterward,
because there was nothing to tell: a weight he had carried so long he
had stopped calling it weight was, between one heartbeat and the next,
not. The collar opened like a hand unclenching. He caught it as it
fell, the way he'd catch a dropped cup — and then he understood what he
was holding, and he threw it down at the foot of the crate-throne,
hard, in front of the crown, for whatever was left of the court to see
what its cup-bearer thought of the service.

"That's my notice given," he said, three words where one would have
done, and his voice going all directions. "That's me resigned."

The woman put out her hand for the banner-pole, and Nix's stomach
dropped, one wall gone, none left — and she only set the pole upright,
squared it in his grip, and folded his fingers back around it.

"It's kept people behind it before," she said. "Carry it where I can
see it."

⁂

They did not climb that day. There was a field to clear, and dead of
four kinds to do four things with, and the muster-folk of Bannerline to
call up out of hiding among the rotting standards. Their keeper came
first — a muster-serjeant, Rung by name, with a color kept folded and
dry under her coat through the whole occupation — and at dusk-lamp she
climbed the Standard-Elm and raised it, a true color on the king's own
tree, and the field gave up its people, drummers and farriers and
banner-wrights walking out toward the elm as if the horns of a thousand
years had all sounded at once. The gathering the tower had frozen a
breath too soon stood on the Kingsfield and finished itself.

Nix counted them until he could not.

He kept the grey banner. No one claimed it; the woman — Ede, the
militia called her, and something older and harder when they thought
she couldn't hear — would not look at it long, and would not let anyone
take it from him either. Wall and open hand. He asked the elf whose it
had been, and got a fraction: "A company's." He asked the splinter of
crystal, and got: *Hers.*

In the morning the host went up through the arch of the ten kings, and
Nix went with it, the first of his people to climb that stair free
since the stair was built, holding a dead company's colors to hide
behind and hiding behind nothing, counting the steps — forty-one,
forty-two — because counting was how he sang. Above lay the counting
country, the giants' great ledger-halls, and the rumor was already
running down the column like spilled coin: the lifts up there stood
open, men of Roothollow had hung chains across them, and the chains
had a price on.

Nix had been climbing all his life in one collar or another. He knew
before anyone said it that the next floor would want paying.
