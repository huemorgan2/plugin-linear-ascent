# Epilogue — The Descent

Going down took longer than going up, which nobody had thought to expect,
and which made every kind of sense once you were in it. Climbing, you
fight a floor. Descending, you meet it.

Nix counted the floors down the way he had counted everything since the
Kingsfield — out loud, on his fingers, twice — and the count went slowly
because every freed floor wanted its hour. The waiting-folk of the
antechambers walked out of their hall for good and had to be shown, floor
by floor, that doors elsewhere simply opened when you pushed them. The
gardeners of Bloomside carried their woken planted down on litters, and
every gate-town on the way turned out to feed them. The masons stopped a
week on the Kingsway going down, not to topple anything — the toppling was
done — but to carve, small and low on one standing plinth, in a plain
hand, the names of everyone the avenue had ever lied about. Nix read them
until his eyes gave out. Then he had them read to him.

Somebody asked him, on the long stair below the Kingsway, whether going
down felt different from going up. Nix considered it for half a floor.
"Turns out you climb a tower the same way you leave one," he said. "One
true step, then the next." And went back to counting.

He walked, mostly, near the wagons. That was where his work was. The
parole of Berrin Or was a single sheet, written by the thirty and signed
by the thirty, and it said in effect that the man who had sold Shieldwall
would spend the rest of his life provisioning every floor he had ever
sold, forever, and that Nix held his rope. There was no rope. That was
the point of it, Ede had said, and Nix had taken a while to understand
and then had understood it better than anyone: the sentence was not that
Berrin be dragged from floor to floor. The sentence was that he go.

So the traitor of the age drove a supply-wagon down the freed tower with
a goblin sitting beside him counting sacks, and that was the justice of
the rising, and there were people on every floor who thought it soft
until they watched Berrin's face for a while, and then stopped thinking
that.

At the Frosthall they stayed nine days, because Jarl Hrimgar would not
hear of fewer.

The hall had been building all the year of the upper war — the hall-folk
of Hearthold had sworn, after the feast of the burning, that the rafters
would go back up before the war came down, and they had kept it. New
roof-trees, new benches, the old carvings re-cut where fire had eaten
them; and the whole vast place smelling of sap and mead and snow through
the open doors. The banners of the parliament hung again from the
rafters, all hundred of them now, sewn fresh by anyone whose floor had
one, and Nix stood under them a long time with his neck bent back,
counting, which by then the whole host understood was how Nix wept.

There were two doors into the Frosthall, as there had always been, and
the hall-folk had done a thing with them. Over the north door, cut deep
in the new lintel-beam, it said DUN HARROW HELD HERE. Over the south, in
the same hand, HALE HELD HERE. No titles on either, because Bruna
Hearthold, who had ordered the cutting, judged that the one man had
carried a banner and the other had carried a doctrine, and both had put
down what they carried and kept a door, and a door is a door.

The feast was a true feast. Hrimgar gave the toast standing, in the old
form, to the guest, the road, and the dead; the skalds sang Bruna's true
songs and then a new one, still rough at the joints, that Nix pretended
very loudly not to recognize himself in. Giants danced. Rhovan was
persuaded to sing, which he did the way elves do, one true verse and
then silence for an hour while everyone thought about it.

And at the high bench Ede Stonhal sat at Hrimgar's right hand and
laughed, actually laughed, twice — Nix counted — and the second time she
caught Nix counting and raised her cup half an inch to him across the
hall, and Nix raised his, and neither of them said Dun's name, and both
of them drank to it.

At the fortieth floor the wagons halted at the mouth of the Broodthrone,
and Rhovan of the Grey Rides straightened his coat like a man going to a
hanging he approved of, and went in to pay his debt.

Nix went along, officially as parole-keeper of the wagons, in truth
because nothing in the world would have kept him out. The great cavern
was strung with silk still — silk was what she was, there was no freeing
Vyx of Vyx — but the cocoons were long empty, the court long walked free,
and the tribute-webs hung with honest trade-goods now: Threadneedle
paid rates for websteel, and the rates were posted, and Voss himself had
countersigned them, which Vyx appeared to regard as the only compliment
the age had paid her.

The Matriarch received them on the throne-web, vast and grey-velvet and
folded, a teacup of warmth steaming between her forehands in the old
courtesy. The contract was recited — one true story, never told before,
annually, in perpetuity — and the clauses examined, and found binding,
and savored in the finding.

'Well, hostage,' said Vyx. 'What have you brought me this year?'

'The whole of it,' Rhovan said.

Eight eyes considered him. 'Define the whole.'

'From the first page,' said Rhovan. 'A farm rising into the dark, and a
child watching it go, whose name I will give you in its place in the
telling. A sealed ledger-page. A woman who trained other people's
recruits. Everything the Wake did and everything I hid from you the year
I sat your web and told you fractions. The feast. The shield on the
trophy wall. What the wyrm smelled. Who it was. What we did with him.
The Door, the throne, the light going home. All of it, in order, true,
and never told whole to anyone — not even to her. It will take some
nights.'

Vyx was still for a long moment, the way the web is still.

'You are overpaying,' she said at last, 'by some years.'

'I know my clauses,' Rhovan said. 'Call it interest.'

So the spider-queen of the Deep settled herself the way listeners have
settled since fires were new, and the elf sat cross-legged on the silk
where he had once sat as her hostage, and told her the book — this one,
all of it — and Nix stayed for the first three nights, wedged in a warm
gallery, hearing the war he had walked through come back at him as a
story, until he got to the part where he was in it and left, embarrassed
and gleaming, to see to his wagons.

He heard later that when the telling ended, days on, Vyx sat silent the
better part of an hour and then said the contract was discharged for the
year, and that the story wanted only one repair, which she would make
herself: it had been told to her as the tale of a rising, and it was
plainly, said the keeper of contracts, examining the thing from all
sides with a broker's eye, a tale about the keeping and breaking of
words.

At the thirtieth floor the black moat lay quiet around the great mound,
and the dead slept, and you crossed the water on a flat-bottomed ferry
poled by a Tollman in Voss's livery, and it cost a copper.

A posted board at the ferry-stage said so: ONE COPPER THE CROSSING.
FREIGHT BY WEIGHT. KEEPERS, COLLAR-BREAKERS, AND THE THIRTY CROSS FREE.
Nix read the last line four times. There were no chains anywhere on the
stage — Nix looked, thoroughly, on principle — and when a drover's family
came up short at the fare-box, the Tollman waved them aboard with the
weary air of a man under standing orders he had given up arguing with.

Voss was at the stage himself that day, weighing freight. He had grown
no rounder and no kinder coming down; he had simply, as Ede put it,
moved his whole ledger to the other side of the war and balanced it
there. Ferry-fares on twenty floors, at a copper a head, honestly
posted, honestly kept — 'A toll is a wall with a slot in it,' he told
Nix, unasked, dropping the family's uncollected coppers into the box
from his own purse and marking the sum in a book titled GOODWILL,
'whereas a fare is a road that pays for its own mending. I am in roads
now. Roads,' said the Tollmaster-General of the rising, with the
contentment of a man who had finally found a commodity no one could
liberate, 'go both ways.'

Nix paid his copper. Voss gave him change. Nix kept the change all the
way down to the first floor, in his cheek-pouch, like a pearl.

The twentieth floor still smelled of stone and old smoke, and would for
a lifetime. But the drum-towers were down, and the pens were gone — torn
out by the people they had held, the bars gone to Vega's forges and come
back as ploughshares and pit-props — and the mine was a mine again, and
Shieldwall the gate-town was raising new roofs under the great gallery's
lamps.

The wagons came down the switchback road in the morning, eleven of them,
grain and seed-grain, salt, iron stock, lamp-oil, and new bread. Nix
rode the first wagon. Berrin Or drove it.

He was grey now, and thinner than the man who had kept a granary and
everyone's confidence in Roothollow, and he had driven the whole
descent with the steadiness of a man who knows exactly how many floors
his sentence has and does not mean to miscount them. At the gallery
mouth, where the drums had been, Dara Shieldwall stood waiting with her
tally-slate, and behind her, not gathered, only failing to be anywhere
else, stood a fair number of the folk of the floor he had sold.

Nobody spoke. That was the shape the thing had found, delivery by
delivery, floor by floor: nobody spoke, and nobody helped, and nobody
stopped him. Berrin let down the tailboard and carried the sacks in
himself, one at a time, past Dara, into the granary the orcs had once
used for a gaol; and the smell of Roothollow bread went past the people
of Shieldwall in the arms of the man whose smell it had once betrayed,
and they watched it go by, and let it.

It took him until midday. Dara counted the sacks twice and signed the
slate, and looked at him once, straight, the way you look at weather.

'Same time next season,' she said.

'Yes,' Berrin said.

That was all. That was always all; and Nix, who held the parole and had
now watched twenty floors of it, understood better every time why the
thirty had chosen it over a rope. A hanged man is finished. A provisioner
is never finished. Berrin Or would spend the rest of his life doing,
floor after floor, season after season, forever, the exact thing he had
once pretended to be — and the difference between pretending and doing it
was to be his whole punishment, and would be quite enough.

He climbed back onto the empty wagon and took up the reins and waited,
eyes forward, for the word.

'Nineteen to go,' Nix said, and cracked his knuckles, and did not say it
unkindly. Nix said very little unkindly anymore. He had counted what it
cost.

They came down into the Fencerows at first lamp, if you could still call
it that — the Yoke's false daylight had died with the tower's will, and
Hobb Fennick's people had strung honest lanterns along the hedgerow lines,
so the valley went to a soft gold at the edges and the sky above it was
plain dark, miles deep, restful.

The hedges were laid and trimmed. The Mother Ditch had a mended
drove-bridge. There was a fair on Fairstone Green — an actual fair,
pitches and bunting and a fiddler, the first in a lifetime — and a dog
was working a flock along the water-meadow to a whistle, which stopped
Nix in the road entirely for a while.

Hobb met the wagons at Lamplit Steading with his coat on and his tally
in his hand, and he made them stand there, the whole train, while he did
the thing he had been waiting the age of the tower to do. He counted
them in. He walked the line with his stub of chalk, wagons, walkers,
drovers, one goblin, and he checked it against the count sent down from
the second floor, and he carried the two figures to the gate-post where
his lifetime of wrong sums was notched, and he set them side by side,
and looked at them a long time.

'They match,' said Hobb Fennick, to the gate-post, to the valley, to
every climber he had ever counted into the hedges and never counted out.
He said it the way other men say amen. 'Everyone who went in has come
out. The counts match.'

He chalked it on the post, and then, being Hobb, he checked it once
more.

Roothollow had not grown so much as opened. The gate-queue was gone;
the tower stood at the town's back the way a cliff stands over a shore,
filling the north of the world from horizon to horizon, its top beyond
the air — a fact of the country now, its dark doorway propped wide and
its first stair swept. People went up it to visit their kin on the near
floors, and came down again for market-day. The Stone of the Climb
stood where it had always stood, with its thirty names cut by Ede's own
hand in the bad years — and no one had scratched them out; instead
someone had cut, beneath the column, in newer letters: CAME HOME.

The market square was loud the morning the last wagons came in. And at
the head of the square, in the trampled ground between the aether-forge
and the horse trough where the recruits had always drilled, the recruits
were drilling.

There were more of them than there had ever been, and they were of more
kinds than Roothollow had ever seen — farm boys and kobolds, a young
giant, two of the waiting-folk's grandchildren stood very straight — and
the drill-mistress walking their line with her hands behind her back was
Ede Stonhal, and the assistant drill-mistress arguing with her about
footwork, to the open delight of the whole front rank, was Aldis Bright.
The general of the rising had been offered the summit, the throne-hall,
and the naming of the age. She had come home instead to train other
people's recruits — her old trade, made honest: she would climb with
them now, whenever the climbing was wanted, the whole way, every time.

The banner went up at midday, without a speech. Ede had refused every
proposal to plant it on the summit — the summit, she said, had had quite
enough flags — and so it was done here, in the market, where the whole
thing had started: a socket of good stone set in the trampled ground by
Vega's own hands, and the oldest banner in the world raised over the
drill-yard by Marn's one uncovered hand and Tam's two, the grey wall and
the crooked star hanging out full in the valley wind over recruits, and
market-stalls, and the forge, and the trough, and the ordinary priceless
racket of a town with nothing over it but sky.

Nix watched from the top of a grain-barrel, counting nothing at all for
once. His parole-sheet was folded in his cheek-pouch beside Voss's
copper; the wagons were unloaded; the age was over; supper was cooking
somewhere and he had been promised some of it.

The drill ended at dusk. The recruits racked their staves and went to
eat, loudly, the way recruits have always gone to eat, and the square
filled with lamplight and shout and the smell of bread that meant only
bread now. And because a drill-yard is a working place and not a shrine,
the last one off the ground — a farm girl with her hair in a soldier's
knot, born the year of the Warcamp, who would be tall enough for the
wall in a season or two — lifted the banner out of its socket at the
drill's end as she'd been taught, to take it in for the night; and then
supper called her too, the way it calls the young, and she stood the old
pole down for just a moment, carefully, in the one place in the square
where a thing may lean and not fall.

And that is where the story leaves it, if a story ever leaves anything:
the hundredth banner and the first one, the grey wall and the crooked
star, furled for the evening in a market square at the bottom of a
mountain that used to be a prison — leaning against the horse trough.
