# Chapter 8 — What the Freed Forgive

The drums had been silent a day and a night, and Ede had not yet learned
how to hear the quiet.

The Red Warcamp had been a mine before Skarn pitched his war in it, and
now the mine was coming back through the camp the way green comes back
through a burn. Lantern-light stood in the great galleries where drum-fire
had stood. The tents were down, or burning in orderly heaps, and the
looted dwarven tools were being sorted from the orcish scrap by giants who
wept while they worked, because they knew the makers' marks. Above it all
the cages hung from their gallery chains, doors open, swaying a little,
empty at last. Nobody would take them down yet. Vega had offered. The
freed had said no. They wanted to look at them open for a while.

Ede walked the cage-rows with the manifest Rhovan had cut from the
warcamp's counting-tent, and she made the only accounting that mattered.

A hundred and twelve had marched behind her into this floor, in the year
she did not name anymore. Thirty names she had cut into the Stone of the
Climb with her own hand, in Roothollow, in the winters after, one name a
night when the drink let her hold a chisel. The cages had given back
sixty-three. That left nineteen, and the manifest had them too, in a
clerk's neat fist, in a column headed *sent above*.

Sent above. Two words for nineteen people. She read the column three
times, as if the names would change their minds.

*"They kept good books,"* Sliver said against her palm, dry as old paper.
*"Evil generally does. It is the one habit I would have you learn from
it."*

"I'll keep the books," Ede said. "I won't keep them politely."

Below the cage-galleries the dead were being laid out in two grounds, and
the distance between the grounds was the whole war. In the first lay the
rising's own — militia of the low floors, miners of Lampfall and
Half-Light, four of Hale's Cullers, and not one of the freed, which Ede
counted the only clean thing the day had done. In the second ground lay
the garrison. Red Orc honor guard, big men made small by stillness. The
looters, who had died running. And the drum kobolds — a row of them, drums
still corded to their backs because nobody had the heart to cut the knots,
each body the size of a child's. They had been beaten forward in front of
the shield-line to keep the rhythm of an army that despised them, and they
had died keeping it.

The Wrongmade left no bodies at all. Where the warframe champion had
fought there was only a spill of parted iron and a scorch on the gallery
floor, going cold. The thing that had worn the iron was not dead — it had
been put out, evicted, dragged back down to the country it was poured
from, and the floor it had stood on was ground again, and nobody mourned
at that spot or ever would.

But a dead kobold is only a dead kobold. That was the arithmetic the
shardminds would not soften and Ede would not let herself forget: the
Natives were cured by killing, and the Wrongmade were evicted by it, and
the Pressed just died, in their collars, for a due nobody had ever paid
them. She stood at the second ground longer than the first.

Dun Harrow found her there, because Dun Harrow spent that whole first day
finding people who were standing too long in one place.

He came up the gallery with a crate of boots on his hip, his one arm doing
the work of two the way a river does the work of rain — no strain, just a
redistribution of the world. The left sleeve of his coat was pinned flat
at the shoulder, and the pin was a Shieldwall clasp, which was either the
saddest thing in the camp or the finest; with Dun it was always both.

"Boots," he said, by way of a greeting. "Skarn's quartermaster hoarded
them. Sixty pair, dwarven-made, hardly worn. The lads' feet rotted in
those cages five — " he caught himself, shrugged, " — in those cages a
long while. Good boots will do more for this company than a speech, so
I've took the liberty of cancelling your speech."

"I hadn't written one."

"I know. I've heard you speak." He said it so gravely that it took her a
breath to catch the shine in his eye, and by then he was already moving,
calling ahead of himself: "Boots! Dry boots! Form a line, you beautiful
skeletons, the war's begun paying wages!"

She watched him go. Eleven years he had carried her standard, and he had
lost the arm at the drum-hill carrying it, in the hour everything else was
lost, and five years of cages had taken his flesh and half his teeth and
none of him. Every night in the cages — the freed told it in pieces, none
of them able to tell it whole — Dun had sung the muster. Called the roll,
name by name, all hundred and twelve, so the dark could not have their
names whatever else it took, and the living had answered until they
couldn't, and when a voice stopped answering Dun answered for it, and sang
on.

He had not asked her a single question about the Warcamp. Not one. She
did not know what she had done to deserve that, and suspected the answer
was nothing, and that this was simply what Dun was for.

The trouble came at the fires, the second night, after the work ran out
and before the sleep came.

The freed camped apart. That was natural; Vega had said so in her slow
way, and Nix had said so in his fast one — *"you don't come out of a cage
and straight into a crowd, boss, a crowd is just a cage that moves"* — and
Ede had agreed and hated it. Their fires burned in the old mine's east
gallery, and the rising's burned in the west, and Ede walked into the east
with a kettle of stew and her hood down so everyone could see her face,
because going where you are not wanted with your face showing was the only
kind of courage she had left over that day.

Serle Danner stood up across the fire before the kettle touched the
ground.

She remembered him as a corporal, quick and proud, the best hedge-fighter
in the second file. The cages had made him a long shadow of that man, but
the quickness was still there, and the pride had gone to an edge.

"I'll ask it plain," Serle said, "since no one else will ask it at all.
The orcs knew our route. They knew the watch-words. They knew the hour we
changed the pickets — they were *inside* the change. I've had five years
to think about how many people knew those three things, Captain, and I
keep counting the same small number, and every count ends with the one of
us who walked back down that stair alive."

The gallery went quiet as the inside of a bell.

"Say it all," Ede said.

"You sold us." His voice cracked in the middle of it, which made it worse,
because it meant he did not fully believe it and had to live with it
anyway. "Or tell me who did."

Half the fire looked away from him in shame. The other half looked at
her, and did not look away — five years in the dark with Serle's same
count, and the same answer, because it was the only answer the dark sold.

She had rehearsed nothing. Everything she might say she had already said
to herself, drunk and sober, for five years, and none of it had ever
acquitted her, because the charge that mattered was not treason. The
charge was that she had led them in, and come out.

"I didn't sell you," she said. "I can't prove that tonight. Proof is
above us somewhere, and I mean to climb until I stand on it. What I can
tell you tonight is only what you already know: I came back. It took me
five years and it is worth exactly what it is worth."

"It's worth *nothing* — " Serle began, and did not finish, because Dun
Harrow was walking into the firelight, and the thing across his shoulder
was the banner.

Nix had carried it up from the Kingsfield, that shabby glorious meadow of
stolen flags where he had first snatched it up to hide behind — a goblin's
shield of good wool and better ghosts. He had surrendered it that
afternoon without being asked, the way you give back something you were
keeping for someone. *"It never fit me,"* he'd said. *"Too tall. Wrong
number of stones in the wall. Also it's yours."* And Dun had taken it in his
one hand and said nothing at all, and gone away with his face very still,
and now here he was.

He stopped at the fire's edge, where everyone could see, and he did not
raise his voice, because he had never in eleven years needed to.

"This came off the drum-hill," Dun said. "It's been in a goblin king's
trophy-meadow five years, and it was minded there better than we were
minded here, and now it's home. So. There's a question at this fire about
who sold us. It's a fair question. Here's mine back: the one who sold us
— did that man ever, once, in five years, come back for us?"

Nobody spoke.

"No," Dun said. "He got his price. We were spent goods. Whoever he is,
wherever he sleeps, we are the one debt he'll never come to collect,
because we cost him nothing and we were worth it." He turned to Ede then,
and held the banner out across the fire, the pole steady in his single
fist, the sewn wall and its crooked star swaying in the heat-shimmer.
"She came back. Walked up
the worst stair in the world to do her collecting. That sums it, for me.
I'd plant this for you, Captain, but the floor's dwarven granite and I'm
short a hand, so you'll have to hold it yourself, and God help you, so
will everyone else."

She took it. There was nothing else to do. The wool was heavier than she
remembered, and then it was lighter.

Dun stepped back, and lifted his chin, and began — quietly, in the tune
worn smooth by five years of dark — to sing the muster. "Aldric of the
first file." And from the fire: "Standing." "Bett Aldwyn." "Standing."
"Cole Danner." Silence — and Dun sang the answer himself, unhurried, the
way he had five hundred nights: "With us." On down the roll, all hundred
and twelve, the living answering and Dun answering for the dead and for
the nineteen sent above, and Serle Danner sat down somewhere in the middle
of it with his face in his hands, not forgiving her, not yet, but weeping
at last on the right side of the fire.

What the freed forgive, Ede thought, holding the banner while the names
went by, is not the ones who wronged them. That comes late or never, and
it is theirs to give. What they forgive first, if you are lucky, is the
one who lived.

Mag Weller came to her past midnight, when the fires were coals.

She had been the company's baker, before — one of the followers who had
walked in with the baggage and been swept up in the sale with everyone
else — and the cages had spared her hands nothing. Skarn's orcs had put
her to pouring at the Warlord's councils, and so for five years she had
stood in the smoke of the enemy's tent with a jug, being furniture, and
hearing everything.

Rhovan was at Ede's fire when she came, folded in his grey way into the
shadow of a gallery post, and Mag looked at him a long moment and decided,
visibly, that a woman who had poured for orcs could talk in front of an
elf.

"There was a courier," Mag said. "Before the drum-hill. Twice he came.
Hooded, gloved, and he never ate at the fire and never drank what I
poured, and I'll tell you for nothing, Captain — a man who won't eat at a
fire is hiding his face or his manners, and this one had manners." Her
scarred hands worked in her lap as if kneading. "He brought Skarn a paper.
I couldn't read it upside down, I only saw it had our shape on it — the
route has a shape, you know it when you've walked it. And when he leaned
past me to lay it down, I smelled him."

The coals ticked. Somewhere off in the east gallery a voice was still
humming Dun's muster-tune, too low for words.

"Smoke and orc-iron, that's every tent on this floor. But under it —"
Mag's face folded up. "Ash-barley and honeyroot. The sweet dark loaf. I
baked at the gate ovens in Roothollow nineteen years before ever I
followed this company, and you don't forget the smell of a bread you've
baked, it gets into a body's clothes and won't be washed out; we used to
laugh about it, we all smelled of it, the whole street. He smelled of
Roothollow bread, Captain. Fresh. Not a week old." She looked up.
"Whoever carried us to the orcs had stood in our own market inside that
same week. He came from home."

For a while nobody said anything at all.

"The toll-ledger," Rhovan said at last, to the fire, in the tone he used
for handing over a fraction and keeping the rest. "The payment that went
up the tower, dated the week of the sale. I told you money moved. I did
not tell you the receipt was countersigned, because the countersign is a
mark, not a name. The mark is a Roothollow factor's mark." A pause,
measured to the coal-tick. "I do not know whose. That is the truth, and I
would know if it were not."

Home. Ede sat with the word like a stone in the mouth. She had built the
Warcamp's ghosts a hundred bodies over five years — orc spies, tower
clerks, Wake men, once in a black month even Hale — and every body she had
built for it had lived conveniently far away, up the tower, in the
enemy's country. Not once had she built it a body that queued at the gate
ovens of a morning, and watched her drill recruits in the market square,
and said good morning.

Berrin would take this hardest of anyone, she thought, and the thought
arrived with its own ache. He had fed half the town from those ovens'
street — stood godfather to the bakers' children, forgiven their debts in
bad winters, carted the sweet dark loaves up the gate roads at his own
cost the day the climb reopened. Home was his religion. She would have to
write to him, and she did not know how a person writes *it was one of
us.*

*"So,"* Sliver said, into her palm, very softly for a thing with no
softness in it. *"The wound is not up the tower. You have been climbing
toward it and carrying it the whole while. I have known forty-one
climbers, Ede Stonhal, and I will tell you what the strong ones knew: home
is the wound. Climb with it open. It bleeds less in the wind."*

In the morning the freed mustered under the banner — sixty-three of them
in dwarven boots, Vega having mended the old wall-and-star's torn hoist
with silver forge-wire, one-handed Dun directing the stitching like a man
conducting music — and when Ede asked, formally, who among them would go
down to Roothollow and rest, not one soul stepped out of the line, and
Serle Danner stood at the end of it with his jaw set, daring her to send
him anywhere but up.

Above them waited the Burnt Common, a heath the fire had taken the same
season the tower took it, green pushing through black char, a country
stopped halfway through healing — and above that, eighty floors of other
people's cages. Somewhere below, in the streets of home, walked a man who
smelled of sweet dark bread, counting his money.

Ede put her hand to the lift-gate, and the freed came up behind her, and
they climbed.
