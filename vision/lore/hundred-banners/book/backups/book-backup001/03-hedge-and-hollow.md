# Chapter 3 — Hedge and Hollow

The farmer was waiting at the lift-head with a tally-stick, and Rhovan
liked him before he spoke, which in Rhovan's trade was a fault and he
noted it as one.

"Thirteen in," the old man said, cutting a notch. Not a greeting: an
entry. He was small and weathered-grey, in a hedger's coat mended with
four kinds of thread, and behind him the Fencerows ran to the white
horizon — hedged fields in their old straight lines under a false
light that never set, hay half-cut and left to grey a lifetime ago,
grass gone pale as paper, and no shadow on the whole floor that had
ever moved. "Hobb Fennick. I count what goes into the hedges, and I count
what comes out. The counts don't match, and haven't in forty years, and
you'll forgive me for saying I don't expect you thirteen to mend the
arithmetic."

"We're not here to mend it," said Ede. "We're here to change what's
being counted."

Hobb looked at her a long breath, then at the splinter lit at her
throat, and cut a second notch that Rhovan could see no reason for, and
led them in along the fence-line toward Lamplit Steading with its barred
doors and its rationed lamps.

Rhovan walked last, where he could see all of it, and kept his book.

It was a plain grey book, oilcloth-bound, and it was his employer as
truly as any spymaster on the Grey Rides. The Wake liked its wars
auditable. Three columns, ruled by his own hand at the top of every
page, in the tongue of the low floors so that anyone finding it would
read piety instead of accountancy: *Cured. Evicted. Dead.* A kill of a
Native was a cure — the fever cut out of the land's own beast. A kill of
a Wrongmade was an eviction — the King's craft turned out of ground that
was never his. And a kill of one of the Pressed was a death, plainly, a
death that should not have been needed, and the Wake's doctrine held
that a rising which could not keep its third column short deserved to
lose. Rhovan had kept the three columns through two wars and one long
peace, and had learned more about every company he'd ever traveled with
from the shape of its entries than from anything its captains said.

He was here to keep a fourth account beside the three, though it was
written nowhere: the woman. What she looked at first. What she counted.
What her hands did before her orders caught up with them. The people he
answered to had bought themselves a spine for their army, sight unseen,
on the strength of a cut page and an old story. Rhovan's work was to
learn what they had actually paid for.

The hedges taught him quickly.

⁂

The first fight came before the steading did — a rat-hunt, the low war
in miniature. Hedgerow rats, dog-big, teeth first, boiling out of a
bramble gap at the smell of the bread in the packs. It should have been
chaos, thirteen strangers in a lane. It was drill. Ede's voice went out
flat and unhurried — *lane fights narrow, shields make the wall, spears
work over* — and the recruits folded around her words like a gate
swinging shut. Brom's shield was the wall. The twins' spears did the
working over. It lasted twenty heartbeats and cost one bitten boot.

The wolf was different, and the wolf was the lesson.

It came at them where the third hedge gapped, a grey horror the size of
a pony, ribbed and fever-gaunt, all its hunger pointed the wrong way
out. Ede put it down herself, one spear-thrust, stepping *toward* it as
its charge broke around Brom's shield — and then the recruits saw what
every climber sees once and no one believes secondhand. The dead bulk
did not lie there. It dropped away — sloughed, like a coat let fall,
like a fist unclenching — and what got up out of it was a wolf. A plain
grey wolf, lean and shy and right-sized, that looked at fourteen humans
with an animal's honest arithmetic and flowed away through the gap in
the hedge, and was gone toward the wild edge of the floor.

"What we kill was never the beast," Ede said, into their silence. "It's
the thing the tower set riding it. Steel's the only exorcism on offer up
here. Say it back."

They said it back. Rhovan wrote *cured, one wolf* in the first column,
and underneath it, in the private shorthand of the fourth account: *she
stepped toward it.*

The floor gave them one more mercy and one more test before the Warden.
The mercy was a sheepdog — the steading's last pack ran the fields in a
silent ring, fevered, hunting the very fences they'd been whelped to
keep, and when the ring closed on the party at the Mother Ditch it went
the way of the wolf: cured shapes shaking off the wrongness, scattering.
But one of them, a black-and-white bitch with one wall eye, stopped at
Fell's low whistle. Turned her head. Remembered, visibly, in front of
everyone, the whole of a stolen life — the whistle, the work, the name.
She came in to heel shaking like a leaf, and Hobb Fennick knelt down in
the pale grass and took her face in his two hands and said a name Rhovan
didn't catch, and the count of the floor changed by one in a way no
tally-stick had a notch for.

The test stood guard at the Burnt Steading, and it was small, and it
carried a longsword too heavy for it by half.

A goblin. A straggler of the garrison, left behind sick when its
war-band climbed on years ago — collared, footsore, starved down to
wire, holding a door into a roofless black-timbered ruin that no one
had lived in for a lifetime. Nobody had told it the post was abandoned.
Or somebody had, and it had decided that a post was a post. It set its
feet in the doorway as thirteen armed climbers came up the lane, and
got the big blade's point more or less off the ground, and stood.

"Hold," Ede said, and the line held, and Rhovan watched her walk out of
it alone, empty-handed, into the space where the goblin's reach would
have been if the goblin could have swung.

"The collar, not the body," she said to it. "That's the only thing on
this floor we want broken. Put the iron down and it's done. No one here
is owed your blood, and you're not paid enough to owe them yours.
You're not paid at all."

The goblin looked at her for the space of ten heartbeats — Rhovan
counted; counting was how he kept his hands still — and then the
longsword went down in the weeds, and its claws went to its own throat,
and the collar came off over its ears, and it dropped the thing in the
lane-mud where anyone could tread it, and it did not run. It stood
there, half-naked and shaking, unpaid, unowned, guarding nothing, free.

"I'll count him," Hobb Fennick said, in the tone of a man ruling a
line into a ledger kept for forty years. "There's fences want laying.
He'll eat what we eat."

Rhovan stood a while over his book at the day's end. Three columns, and
none of them fit. Cured was for beasts, evicted for craft, dead for
Pressed — there was no heading in the Wake's whole doctrine for *stood
down and stayed*. He ruled a fourth column narrow along the margin and
left the header blank, and in the fourth account, the unwritten one, he
recorded that the woman had walked out of her own shield-wall with
empty hands, and that his employers had no idea what they had bought.

Brackjaw died the next morning in the meadow before the chalk bluff,
and died exactly by the counts she'd taught in the yard: it circled
once — always once — servo-jaws ticking, wolf-and-plate, the meadow's
own grey hunter welded into a mockery of itself; and when the head went
down and it came straight, the line was not in front of it. Brom's
shield turned its shoulder, Rhovan's shaft found the servo-joint from
the flank, and Ede went in over the wreck of its charge and finished
it. The false light overhead dimmed by a hair, the whole sky of it at
once, like a held breath let out. Away across the fields, the doors of Lamplit
Steading began, one by one, to unbar.

There were names on the Stone of the Climb, Rhovan knew, of climbers
who had broken this same floor before — two generations of them. The
tower had re-welded its jailer each time within a few winters, a new
wolf under the old name, Brackjaw the third of that name, and the fever
had crept back over the hedges like damp into a house no one keeps. The
lifts a Warden's death opened stayed open; the *peace* did not, not
unless somebody held it. That was the whole argument of the people
Rhovan worked for, written in one meadow: floors do not stay freed.
They are *held* freed, or they are lost again — and no lone company,
however good its counts, holds anything. For that you need the thing
the tower was built to prevent.

Hobb notched them out at the bluff: "Thirteen up. First time in forty
years the counts came out ahead." He said it accusingly, as if they'd
broken something he'd grown fond of, and stood at the lift-head with
the wall-eyed bitch at his knee, watching them rise, until the chalk
and the hedges and the old man and the dog were all one pale country
below, and then were the underside of one.

⁂

Floor two was the inside of a mountain that no longer had an outside.

The Rustwater Adit: a slab of the giants' deep-forge realm cut off at
the mine-mouth, ore-carts loaded on rails that ran off the floor's edge
into open air, bats hung thick in the entrance vaults, and everything
not iron furred orange with rust. The mine still wept — iron-water
running down the cut faces, sweet-smelling and wrong — and the dark
past the third lamp was the first true dark most of the recruits had
ever stood in.

The keeper met them under the headframe, a giant woman with a dead
safety-lamp on her belt, who filled the gallery she stood in. Bergda
Ironkettle, pit-foreman of a pit with no bottom left. She dipped two
fingers in the runnel by the rail and tasted them, unhurried, while the
party stood dripping in their pooled lamplight.

"Sweet today," she said. "It lies. There are kobolds cutting my seams
in the dark, poor collared vermin, and an orc set over them with a
whip, and the Warden hunts the flooded drift where the water hides its
feet. You'll want the rails. Walk between them, not on them. Rails
carry sound the way ledgers carry debt: farther than you'd credit, and
always to somebody."

The ambush came at the Cart-End all the same, where the loaded carts
stood halted at the world's edge — kobold diggers rising out of the
side-cuts with mattocks and eyes like dulled coins, driven from behind
by a Red Orc outrider who bawled them forward in the tower's tongue and
kept himself behind the line of them the whole while. It was ugly, and
close, and won the way lane-fights are won, with the shield-wall and
the flat voice and the spears working over. The orc died last, at the
lip of the drop, contemptuous to the end of the vermin he'd spent.
Rhovan's third column took its first entries of the climb, and he wrote
them small and hated the necessity: *dead, kobolds, five. Dead, Red Orc
outrider, one.* Deaths that should not have been needed. The bill of
the low war, presented at the usual counter.

And then the last kobold put its mattock down.

It came out of the side-cut after the fight was already over — half
blind, its lamp long dead, following the sound of breathing. It saw the
lamplight and the bodies and the wall of shields, and it did the thing
that every recruit in the party had been taught at the trough-side in
Roothollow, the sign the whole mercy of the climb turned upon: it laid
the mattock on the stone, and knelt, and raised both hands to its own
collar.

Ede killed it.

Rhovan did not see her decide, and that was the whole of what he saw:
there was no deciding in it. The kobold's hands went to its collar and
her blade was already through it — one motion, old and oiled and
perfect, a stroke from another war, from a mine-dark five years deep
where things that knelt in front of Shieldwall had killed the ones who
believed the kneeling. The sound the body made was small. The sound the
water made, dripping somewhere off in the galleries, went on exactly as
before, and was suddenly the loudest thing on the floor.

Nobody spoke. Brom looked away, once, and made himself look back. The
splinter at Ede's throat burned steady and said nothing at all, which
was its own kind of entry.

Ede stood over the body with the blade still in her hand, and Rhovan
watched her look at it — at the mattock lying flat on the stone, at
the raised hands now not raised — and understood that she had arrived
in the same heartbeat everyone else had, one motion too late, at the
knowledge of what her hands had done before her orders could catch
them.

He took out the grey book. He was aware of her eyes coming up as the
oilcloth cracked, aware of the recruits watching him do it, aware that
there were words that could be said — *warcamp reflexes, five years,
no one blames* — and that saying any of them was not his work. His
work was the columns. He set the book on his knee and wrote, in the
third column, in a fair hand, without hurry and without a word:

*Dead. One kobold, collared. Mattock grounded. Hands at the throat.*

The pen-scratch was the only sound in the gallery. When he closed the
book she was still looking at him, and he met it, and gave her nothing,
because nothing was the truest thing he had. It went into the fourth
account too, the unwritten one, and what he wrote there was: *she will
carry this better than absolution would let her. See that she is never
absolved cheaply.* The people he worked for would read a different
report. They would get the fraction they had paid for.

Rustmaw they took in the flooded drift that evening, by Bergda's counsel
and the counts: a rust hound welded under an ore-crusher's jaw, dragging
lengths of rail behind it like a penance, hunting where the black water
hid its footing. Pike found the dry spine of the old sump-causeway with
a plumb-line and a delver's patience; they baited the charge along it;
Brom met the crusher-jaw with the boss of his shield and the whole floor
rang like a struck bell; and Ede finished it in the water, and stood
after, breathing, while the orange stain of the drift settled around her
knees.

That night, walking the watch below the Headframe, Rhovan passed the
banked fire where the captain sat alone and heard the splinter
speaking, low, in the dark over her collar-bone, and did not stop
walking, and heard it anyway.

*I have carried one-and-forty climbers,* it said, *and every one of
them was certain of something that killed somebody. A sword is quick,
Ede Stonhal. It answers today's question and deafens you to
tomorrow's. Spend your certainty slowly. It is the only coin in this
tower that does not come back.*

If she answered, the fire kept it. He wrote nothing in any column.
There are entries a book cannot hold.

The Headframe's great wheel turned that night for the first time in
forty years, raising crews out of galleries the tower had written off,
and the lamps of Lampfall came up all together — and if any soul in the
party thought the low floors would now simply open before them, mercy
by mercy, cure by cure, the third floor was waiting to correct them.

⁂

The Drowned Pasture lay under its own unmoving light like a dropped
mirror: a water-meadow taken mid-flood and never allowed to drain, grey
water sheeted to the horizon, hay-ricks standing out of it as rotting
islands, willows trailing in their own reflections, and somewhere a
sluice still turning, patient as a clerk, keeping the wrong things wet.

Old Nan Sedge poled out of the reeds to meet them standing up in a punt,
a dry stick of a woman who had not, by her own accounting, been dry in a
year. "The weir still works," she told them, by way of a keeper's
greeting. "That's the joke of it. The weir works, and everything it kept
dry is under. You'll walk the Sunken Drove if you've sense — the old
road runs a hand's breadth under the flood, straight across, and the
beasts won't cross it. Single file. Where the water goes white over your
boots, that's the bridge, and you'll want to be nowhere else."

The drove crossing was the longest half-league of the climb so far:
thirteen climbers walking on water they could not see through, on a road
they had to trust was there, while things moved in the reed-beds to
either side and the white glare came off the flat grey sheet like a
blade held to the eyes. A fence-wire eel took Lark's calf open at the
midpoint — a barbed length of the drowned fences come alive, wire and
eel grown into one long wrongness — and Fell killed it on her sister's
leg without breaking stride, and it shed its wire as it died and slid
away into the deep channel, cured, only an eel, while Lark bled and
laughed and swore in marsh-cant all the rest of the way across.

The drowned lantern came at dusk, and it came for the wounded.

A cold light, drifting over the water, sweet and steady, making for the
party's island camp like a ferryman's lamp coming out to the rescue —
and every line of it wrong to anyone who looked slantwise. Rhovan
looked slantwise for a living. A trapper's lamp lost in the flood, the
keeper had warned them, its dead flame soaked in spilled aether until
the light learned to walk and to *call*; punts followed it onto the
black cutting-reed and were found, later, lightened. He let it come on
until it stopped pretending to drift, and then he put one shaft through
the heart of the glow, and it did not die, because it had never lived:
it guttered, wick-fashion, and came apart, and drained downward through
the dark water like something being poured back where it was drawn
from. *Evicted, one lantern,* said the second column. The oldest
climbers' word for it was the right one. You did not kill the Wrongmade.
You turned it out of the house.

Sedgeback they broke at the mill-pool at dawn — a mire boar under
weir-iron, green bronze plates shedding arrows like a roof sheds rain,
charging through the shallows in a bow-wave that hid its feet. They
denied it the flood: baited its charge up onto the drove-causeway where
the water ran only boot-deep and every footfall showed, and Brom
turned it, and the twins hamstrung it, and Ede put it down clean. By
the time the party had breath again, Nan Sedge had the drain-cock's
wheel groaning, and the pasture had begun — floor by inch, as she put
it — to remember it was a pasture. Fence-lines breaking the surface.
Drowned hay steaming under the false light. An ancient tortoise, cured
days since by someone's passing spear and too old to bother being
afraid, sunning itself on the emerging drove like the floor's own
seal of survivorship.

That was when the soldiers came up the lift behind them.

They came in two grey files, forty strong, up through the weir-arch
from the floor below — which was open, of course; every lift the party
had won stayed won, and Rhovan had walked past that fact three times
without seeing what it meant coming up behind them. They wore
slate-grey, and each carried a knotted cord at the shoulder, tallied
with notches like Hobb Fennick's stick, and they moved with the
unhurried thoroughness of men who had done this many times on many
floors and expected to do it many more. Where they passed the reed-beds
they worked in pairs, spear and net, and what they killed they killed
entirely, and counted.

At their head walked a big man gone iron-grey, older than Ede by
twenty years, with a serjeant's plain harness and a face like the north
side of the Stone, and Rhovan knew his voice before he ever heard it —
knew it the first breath the man opened his mouth — because he had been
listening to a copy of it for weeks across a Roothollow drill-yard.

"Stonhal," the man said.

"Serjeant Hale." Her spine had changed before the name was out of her.
Rhovan filed the sight of it: the whole shape of her stood to a lesson
learned thirty years down, deeper than the Warcamp, deeper than the
drink. The man who had taught her to fight. The voice her voice was
built on.

"You put three jailers down in nine days with children and a poacher."
Hale's eyes went along the party once, pricing it — lingering a
half-breath on Rhovan, poacher, which Rhovan chose to find fair — and
came back to Ede. "That's good work spoiled by luck, and luck's not
doctrine. This is a writ of the militia charter." He didn't produce it;
men like Hale are their own paper. "The Cullers hold wardship of the
low floors, one through nineteen, and everything armed that walks them
walks under my count. There's a muster forming at the Beacon Field for
the Kingsfield above it, and your lot are announced to it. You'll have
escort."

"Escort," Ede said.

"Escort," said Hale, in the voice that had invented the flatness hers
copied, and turned to give his files their order of march, the matter
being — visibly, entirely — settled.

So the party that had walked onto floor one counting thirteen walked
off floor three counted, by somebody else, among fifty-three, and the
difference between those two arithmetics was the whole of what Rhovan
put in the grey book that night. He sat late over it by the drying
fence-lines, listening to the Cullers' pickets call their tallies
around the camp like clock-bells, and ruled a fresh page, and headed
it, in the plain tongue of the low floors where anyone might read it:

*The low war has an owner.*

And underneath, in the fourth account, the one with no book: *He taught
her. She stood to him like a recruit. Above us there's a meadow drowned
in a hundred stolen banners and a king the tower crowned as a joke, and
this man means to storm it by doctrine, kill-all, cull and count. What
she does in that meadow will tell my employers what they bought. What
he does there will tell me what she was, before she was ours.*

The false light of the Drowned Pasture never dimmed for night. But at
the weir-arch, where the freed lift ran its chain up out of sight, the
clearest air showed a faint dark grain far overhead — the underside of
the next country, a grain and no more, at the top of miles of sky — and
what hung four floors above that no eye could reach; it came down the
lifts as rumor instead, a meadow drowned in color that Nan Sedge swore
was banners.

A hundred of them. All stolen. All waiting.
