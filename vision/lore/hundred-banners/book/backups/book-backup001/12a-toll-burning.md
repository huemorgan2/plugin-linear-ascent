# Chapter 14 — The Toll-Burning

A host of three thousand does not climb a stair. It queues for one.

Two days after the Kingsbarrow folded itself shut, the rising was
still filing up through the lych-face lift by fifties, wagons broken
down and carried in pieces, and Rhovan of the Grey Rides stood the
rear watch on the last green ridge of floor thirty and did what he
was for: he counted other people's fires.

Except that the fires he was counting were not campfires, and they
were not on this floor.

South and west, out over the barrow country and far beyond it, where
the gate road ran away toward a horizon that was simply distance —
no far wall, no rim, only the land curving off under its own grey
sky — smoke stood up in the still air. One column, two days old,
gone brown at the top. A second, blacker, younger, a day's ride past
it. And that morning, so far off it was less a sight than a
suspicion, a third. News on this tier moved at the speed of a horse,
and the smoke was the news outrunning its own riders: something was
working its way down the freed floors, gate by gate, burning as it
went.

The first rider had come up the lych road the night the mound
closed, with the word that had gone into the reports as
*Herald-fire*: Voss's toll-houses burned at three gates in the
night. The second rider made it five gates. The third made it
eight, and brought a square of paper, and the paper was why Ede had
sent for him.

She received him in the watch-house at the causeway's end, Mereth
Wakesend's lamp still burning in it, the crown of the Barrow King
sitting on the table between them wrapped in sacking, like
something confiscated.

"Eight toll-houses on four floors," Ede said. "In six nights. The
riders say nobody's seen who does it. I want someone who reads ash
the way Voss reads margins. Go down, look, come back. The host
won't wait — we're through the lych-face and climbing. Catch us
up."

"And if what I read wants acting on?"

"Then you'll tell me what you read," said Ede, "and I'll act on
it." She had been two years learning how he parceled truth, and it
had made her exact. He took the paper she slid across the table —
the square the third rider had carried, singed at one corner.
"That was on the post at the Fairstead gate. There's one like it
in every pile of ash so far. Read it on the road."

He read it by the watch-house lamp before ever he took the road,
because paper spoke to him louder than smoke.

It was a fair clerk's hand, ruled, unhurried. No seal. No signature.

*Item: the gate-companies of Roothollow, having extended their
custom and their credit to the rebellion, are found in default of
the King's peace.*

*Item: their charter is void. Their credit is called in. Their
houses are closed, as witnessed.*

*Item: the silver is left. The King has no need of your silver.
You are invited to look at it, and to consider what it kept.*

*Settlement of account.*

Rhovan stood very still for the time a lamp takes to gutter once.

Settlement of account. He had read that phrase before, in a
strong-room a mile inside a stolen mountain, under an entry that
had bored him and then unmade his month: money moved up the tower,
no goods against it, the week Ede's company was sold. The
remittance-book's grammar. Whoever clerked for the tower's high
offices, they wrote a burned gate and a bought company in the same
ruled hand — and this time had wanted it read. A ledger whispers.
This was a ledger raising its voice.

He folded the paper into his coat, next to the older page sewn in
its lining, and went down.

It took him three days to reach the Fairstead gate, on a borrowed
barrow pony, down gate roads he had walked up a season before, and
the going down was a lesson in what the rising had been building.
The toll-roads were arteries now. Grain went up them, and
boot-leather, and arrow-shafts; the mail went up them, and freed
families went down them, going home to floors their grandmothers
had been stolen onto. At every gate the factors' chalk-boards
posted rates, and the rates were honest since the Tithe Barrows,
and Rhovan passed queues at open chains and thought, not for the
first time, that the tower had never once managed to build a thing
this dangerous.

The Heralds had understood it too. That was the whole of what the
ashes said.

The Fairstead toll-house had been a good stone building with a
slate roof, and it was a black shell with its lintel cracked when
Rhovan rode up to it through the fair-country dusk. The Quiet had
got there first — the fair-folk of the floor, who had survived
reprisal before and knew its etiquette — and they had done what
they knew: taken the bunting off their own doors and shuttered
their lamps and left the ruin scrupulously alone, in case looking
at it too long counted as taking a side.

He read the burn the way he had been taught to read rooms.

They had come at night, on foot, without lamps. They had not
hurried; the char-lines said the fire was laid, not thrown —
ledgers first, stacked on the counting-table and lit like a hearth,
the building fired from the paper outward, so that the books were
cinders before the roof ever caught. Coin-boxes dragged into the
yard, opened, and left. Silver fused into slabs by the heat, in ash
where any hand could take it, and no hand had. Six nights, eight
gates, and not one coin missing anywhere — which was the true
message, and it was addressed to every carter and factor and
gate-wife on twelve floors, and it said: *this is not robbery.
Robbery a man can price, and carry, and rebuild from.*

The keeper of the toll-house had been a factor named Immer, a
Warcamp survivor who wore Voss's livery over the scars, and Immer
had done the one thing the fire's authors had no line ruled for:
he had gone back in for the current-book, the season's accounts,
every debt owed to and by four floors of small traders. He had got
it out, mostly. He sat in the yard with his burned hands wrapped
in fair-cloth, the book in his lap under the bandages, and gave
Rhovan his testimony in a voice flattened by two days of use.

Hooded men. Nine or ten, come out of the dark on the road's blind
side, the night watch face-down in the grass with their thumbs
bound before a dog barked. No blades shown. No word said above a
murmur. They had worked, Immer said, like inventory-men — one
counting the ledgers into the fire, aloud, title by title, so
that a man lying in the grass could hear his own life's work
enumerated as it burned. And when the roof fell in, they nailed
their paper to the post and walked into the dark in no hurry at
all, southward, downward, toward the next gate on their list.

"They knew the relay schedule," Immer said. "They fired the beacon
gates first — the three that carry the up-mail. Word crawled. Gate
couldn't warn gate." His mouth worked. "The tower kept better
books on my toll-road than I did."

Rhovan looked at the melted silver in the ash, and at the coin
column nailed up the gate-post — Voss's proud tariff, a head-price
for a man, a price for a mule — where the heat had run the nailed
coins into one long bright smear down the wood, like a candle's
year of weeping done in an hour. He made his tally in the currency
he dealt in: eight gates, chosen not for their riches but for
their freight; the mail-gates first; the
grammar of the high tower's own receiver on the bill. The fire had
read a manifest. Somebody's manifest.

He wrote the Wake that night from the fair-country verge, in the
flat cipher of facts: gates burned, coin left, credit dead,
carters refusing the up-roads, the rising's supply line bleeding
out not from any wound but from fright. It was accurate to the
last figure, and it was a fraction. He did not write the sentence
he could not source: *the tower has stopped sending soldiers down,
because it has found the thing that works.*

He caught the host at the Grey Rides' first camps, eleven days
later, and found the war-council already convened around a trestle
in the bridleway dusk — because the war had come up the lift behind
him, in the shape of a heavy man walking.

Gilder Voss, Tollmaster-General of the gate-companies of
Roothollow, came up the last mile of the gate road on foot, at the
head of what remained of his southern factorage: six clerks, two
guards who had not been paid and had come anyway, and a mule that
had given out a floor below and been left, its load shifted to the
Tollmaster's own back. He carried two strong-boxes in a porter's
frame, the straps cutting welts you could see from the ridge, and
he would not give them up — not to the pickets, not to his clerks,
not to the three soldiers who offered and were priced with a look
and found wanting. Under one arm he carried his ledgers, or what
the fire had left of them, singed to the spine at the corners, and
those he held the way other men had carried their dead down from
the Warcamp.

Rhovan watched him come and understood he was seeing a man carry
his entire remaining self uphill.

They gave him the trestle's end, and beer he did not drink, and
the council looked at him — Ede at the table's head with the day's
climb still on her, Dun Harrow at his post behind her shoulder,
banner grounded; Sef Wakewater and his vigil-book; Hale silent at
the foot; Berrin, who had come up with the evening's bread and
stayed, because supply would be the whole matter; Nix on a barrel,
counting the strong-boxes at a glance. Voss set the frame down off
his back with the care of a man lowering a coffin, and
straightened, and winced, and looked around the trestle with his
small shrewd unbeautiful eyes, and said:

"Before anyone begins. I am not here because I have seen a light."

"Nobody supposed it," said Ede.

"They burned eleven houses," Voss said. "By now, likely thirteen.
They left the silver. Do you understand what that is, captain? I
have been robbed by professionals on four floors and I have robbed
a few myself, in the way of business, and there is not a thief in
the world who leaves the silver. Silver they left. Paper they
burned. Forty years of paper — bonds, remittances, standing
credit, the debts of every carter between your yard and my gates.
A man's coin is his purse, captain, but a man's *paper* is his
face, and the King has burned my face off the tier." His voice
stayed level the whole way through it, which was somehow worse
than grief. "And nailed up a bill informing the world that only
one house may keep accounts under this sky. His."

"And so?" Ede said.

"And so I have re-read the market." Voss put one hand flat on the
strong-boxes. "I do not love your rising. It is loud, it is
sentimental, and its arithmetic would shame a child. But it is
now, as of six nights past, the only market left standing. A
world where one hand writes all the paper is not a world a man of
business inhabits; it is a world he decorates. I decline it. So."
He pushed the boxes, both of them, the width of the trestle. "The
southern factorage's reserve. It buys grain where fright has
stopped credit. It pays carters in coin at the gate until the
roads believe in paper again — my clerks will draw the schedule;
they are the only men on this tier who know which debts still
stand, because I went into the fire for the proof of it." A short
pause, the first. "Immer went into the fire. I employ men who go
into fires. That is the whole of my character, captain, and I'd
thank the council to expect nothing better of it."

One of the keepers said what half the camp would say by morning:
"And when the King's clerks come to buy you back?"

"Buy me *back*?" For one heartbeat, Voss looked honestly
affronted — the professional insulted on the one point of his
pride. "Sir, I have just been shown the King's terms of purchase.
He paid eleven houses to teach the freed floors that my custom is
death to take. There is no percentage under him. There is no
*under him* at all; that is what a hundred floors means, I grasp
it at last, it only wanted putting in figures I could read." He
turned back to Ede. "I am not converted. Let the criers say
otherwise and I will sue the criers. I am *invested*. Every coin
in those boxes is a wager that you reach the top, and I mean to
stand at the settling of it holding the world's entire paper in
my two hands. You will find me insufferable. You will find me at
my post."

The silence had the particular texture of a council working out
whether it was allowed to take what it needed.

Ede looked at the strong-boxes a long moment, and Rhovan, who had
spent two years learning her face, watched her arrive at the only
sane filing there was for Gilder Voss — useful, and never to be
turned your back on — and then reach out, with her eyes open, and
turn it into policy.

"No titles," she said. "You'll want one; you'll not have it; paid
on delivery, if we live. Your clerks keep their books under
Berrin's countersign, as at the Barrows. The boxes travel under
Shieldwall guard, sealed, audited when the council says — and you
will be watched, Tollmaster, all the way to the top, by people who
remember your chains at open gates."

"Watched," said Voss, with his first and only smile of the
evening, sitting down at last to the beer, "is another word for
*guarded*. I accept."

It was Berrin who closed it, because it was Berrin who closed
things: he put bread in front of the Tollmaster, and sat down
across from him with his lists, warm as an oven-door, and said,
"Now then. Show me what's left of your paper, Gilder, and we'll
draw up what the fire's still owed" — and the two best
ledger-minds in the freed world bent over the singed remains
together in the lamplight, rebuilding the tier's credit out of a
quartermaster's memory, and everyone at that trestle slept easier
for the sight of it.

⁂

Rhovan wrote his report to the Wake two nights on, in cipher, from
a bridleway verge: the burnings enumerated, the bill transcribed,
the Tollmaster arrived, the reserve pledged, terms as stated.
Accurate to the last figure. A fraction.

He left out the coins run down the gate-post like candle-wax. He
left out that the mail-gates had burned first, in an order that
wanted a manifest, and that the bill's ruled hand had the same
grammar as a receipt he carried sewn into his coat. Those he kept,
the way he kept everything that mattered, for the day telling it
would arm somebody instead of gutting them.

And he left out the thing he had watched at the trestle, because
the Wake's ciphers had no characters for it: the rising taking a
man's greed into its line of battle with its eyes open — no oath
sworn, no heart changed, a wall and a purse agreeing to stand in
the same shield-ring because the same enemy had priced them both
for burning. It was the most cynical alliance the climb had yet
signed, and Rhovan had been three hundred years in the trade of
cynical arrangements, and he could not stop noticing that this
one had been made in daylight, at a public table, in front of the
bread.

The tower bought men in the dark and burned what it could not
buy. The rising put its one thief on the books, and posted the
audit.

Somewhere below, the last smoke of Voss's old empire stood up into
a country-wide sky, and above, at the head of the grey bridleways,
the wagons were already rolling under new paper. Rhovan sanded the
page, and pocketed the fraction, and climbed.
