# Chapter 10 — The Quiet

They learned the shape of winning on the Burnt Common, and it fit them so
well it should have frightened her sooner.

Floor twenty-one was a heath the fire had taken the same season the tower
did — a black country of standing char and gorse-skeletons, with new green
bracken pushing through the ash and stopping, held forever at knee-high,
at the moment after the burn. The Warden was a boar in a shell of
ember-iron that glowed as it fought, and it died the way the lesser
Wardens died, which is to say usefully: Vega's crews dropped a fire-break
across its charge, the militia held, and when the welded shell came apart
there was nothing inside to bury — only the King's scrap of will draining
down and out of the world, evicted, and a floor's worth of quiet falling
like the first rain after a drought.

Then the doors of Ashfold opened, and the pattern played out as it had
played on every floor since the Warcamp. The commoners came out blinking.
The keeper — Goodwife Ash, a common-reeve with a face like a kindly
hatchet — wept exactly once, briefly, as at the end of a long count, and
then put the whole town to work. The parish seed-store came up out of the
glazed sett where it had waited out the burn, and was sown across the
cooled black before the host had finished burying its dead. And when the
rising moved for the stair, the floor rose with it: forty foragers and
fire-wardens of Ashfold in the column, carrying the char-country's hard
plain spell for putting fires out, because — Ash said — the floors above
would be burning, one way or the other, and somebody sensible ought to
come.

That was the shape: the Warden falls, the doors open, the floor rises.
Twenty-two gave them the Whisperwood, an elf-wood that repeated what it
heard, where the host marched between trees that murmured its own words
back a day late — she heard Serle Danner's *you sold us* drift out of the
gall-oaks twice, and once her own voice answering — and where the only
thing that had to be killed with grief was a quoted shade, a knot of dead
signal wearing a dead elf's sentences, which came apart under Rhovan's
arrows still reciting and was evicted, and the sentences went with it.
Twenty-three gave them the Anvil Commons and the smiths of Hammerhome,
and cost them what the Pressed floors always cost: the Red Orc armorers
held the forge-line for a tower that had chained them to it, and three
threw down the lash and lived, and eleven did not, and Ede had Rhovan
enter the eleven in the roll in their own column, the one with no heading,
because there was no word for a death that should not have been needed
and she refused to borrow one.

Twelve hundred mouths climbed off the Anvil Commons. The host had
outgrown the word *party* somewhere below the Warcamp and was outgrowing
the word *company* now. It ate like a town, argued like a family, and
won like a machine, and Ede stood at its head watching the pattern hold,
and let herself begin to believe the pattern was a law.

The Broodwarrens taught her something better, and the Gallows Fair taught
her something true.

⁂

Nobody planned the Broodwarrens. That was the wonder of it.

Floor twenty-four was silk from floor to roof — a Deep-town swallowed by
its own neighbors, caverns packed with sheets and funnels and hanging
egg-sacs like grey fruit, where lamplight died six feet out and the
stair-lift stood cocooned in the deepest chamber. The vanguard's plan was
fire and sever-work, gallery by gallery, and the plan's price was written
on every face that had seen the silk.

Then Wend Silkstead's people, barricaded five years in their sealed vault
with a lamp and a knife, tapped out through the door the old truce-signs
their town had kept with the brood since before the Theft — and Nix asked
what they were, and learned them in an afternoon, and walked into the
Nursery with a lamp and no blade at all.

Ede stood at the gallery mouth for the length of that walk with her heart
in her teeth. She could hear him talking the whole way — fast, ridiculous,
fearless, a cup-bearer's patter poured out into the dark that had eaten a
town: *"— big mothers, listen, I know the signs, one knock for guest, two
for trade, I'm knocking, hear me knocking? — nobody's burning anything,
I told them, I said she'll want to hear us out, I said she's got ten
thousand children, you don't fight a lady with ten thousand children, you
ask her what they eat —"*

And the brood-midwives came down the web-lanes, huge and patient, and
listened.

What the fever could not unmake, it turned out, was older than the fever.
The truce ran deeper than the Yoke's whole voice. By dusk the warren had
yielded entire — not broken, *yielded*, the first floor in the tower's
history to come over on a goblin's word — and the brood itself did what no
blade could have done cheaply: the midwives took their own Warden, the
great silk-engine Cradlesilk that wore their shape as a mockery, and
bound it in a cathedral of cable no thrashing could part. Vega walked in
alone, laid her hands on the welded core, and drew the tower's rivets the
way a surgeon draws a splinter, and the thing that had ridden the warren
five years was evicted without one arrow loosed in anger — it came apart
and drained down and was gone, and the silk went slack across three miles
of cavern all at once, like a held breath let go.

The wrapped came free. Some of them living. Silkstead's survivors walked
streets they had not seen in five years, and the first freed brood-mother
spun silksteel cable for the host's baggage-train as calmly as if the
whole world had merely been a bad season. Nix came out of the Nursery
shaking so hard he had to sit down on a coil of rope, and counted his own
fingers twice — *"ten, good, they felt like six in there"* — and the host
cheered him until the silk trembled.

That night Ede allowed herself the thought in full, deliberately, to see
how it tasted: *perhaps the whole tower can be talked down. Perhaps the
pattern bends all the way up.*

The next floor was the Gallows Fair.

⁂

They heard it before they saw it: music. Thin, wheezing, cheerful music,
box-tunes ground out by broken organs for no one, drifting down the gate
road with the smell of old sugar.

Floor twenty-five was a travelling-fair caught mid-season and abandoned —
booth-rows gone grey, a big wheel frozen against the white sky, bunting
strung everywhere and rotted to rags, the menagerie cages standing open.
A carousel turned at the heart of it, slowly, riderless, and had been
turning for five years. It was a country built for crowds and joy, held
at the moment after everyone fled, and it was the wrongest place Ede had
stood in twenty-five floors, because everything in it was still trying to
be delightful.

The keeper met them on the road a mile short of his own town, alone,
carrying not a white flag but a grey one — a strip of the dead bunting on
a tent-pole, which they would learn was the same thing, in fair-country.

"Pol Fairstead," he said. "Ringwright. I keep the true accounts and the
cage-keys, and I've come out this far so you'd hear me before you're heard
in there. We know who you are. We know what you've done below. And I'm
asking you — the whole fair is asking you, every wagon of it —" he took a
breath like a man lifting a weight, "— pass us by."

Ede had been offered surrender, defiance, worship, and toll. Nobody had
yet asked her to not exist.

They gave him supper at the head of the column and he told it plainly,
the way keepers tell things, and the telling was worse than any Warden.
His grandmother had cheered a rising once. Everyone Pol's age had grown
up on the story — climbers on the low stairs, banners, a season of
believing — and then the climbers were dead somewhere above, and the
tower's answer had come down through the floors that cheered, methodical
as rent-day. The fair remembered what the answer had been. He did not
describe it. His hands described it, going still in his lap.

"You'll go up," Pol said. "Maybe you'll win. I'll pray so. But if you
don't — and nobody yet has — the tower will come asking who lit lamps for
you, and it keeps better books than God. So kill our Warden if you must,
I know you need the stair. But do it quiet, take nothing, muster no one,
and let me keep my people hid. Don't light the wheel. A crowd is the one
thing the fair can't survive."

Rhovan said quietly that the Wake had a name for such floors, the ones
that begged the war to look away. They
were called the Quiet, and there were more of them above than anyone at
this fire would like to know.

The council that night was the worst since the Warcamp, and it was the
worst because nobody in it was wrong.

Ede argued for the fear. She was surprised by her own vehemence. She
stood under the wheel's frozen shadow and argued that fear earned over
five years was not ignorance to be corrected but wisdom they hadn't paid
for, that a floor was not freed if its people
were dragged blinking into a war they had begged to be spared, that the
rising could kill the Warden, free the fever, and leave the fair its
silence — a gift with no blade behind it.

And she lost, to the best people she knew.

Goodwife Ash said, gently, terribly: nobody had asked Ashfold whether it
wanted the fire put out, and thank the stone for that, because fear votes
against its own rescue every time; that is what fear is *for*. Serle
Danner stood up with his jaw working and said the cages had also been
quiet, that he had personally spent five years being passed by, and that
he would not march one floor higher under a banner that had learned to do
it politely. Vega, slowest and kindest and most immovable, said that a
broken thing left unmended does not stay broken, it breaks other things,
and that she had never yet met the fear that was healed by being obeyed.
And the quartermasters said, simply, bread: twelve hundred mouths, winter
somewhere above, and the fair's stores standing shuttered.

The host voted with its hunger and its scars. The fair would be freed —
fully, loudly, mustered and provisioned and its wheel lit, like every
floor before it. When Ede carried the answer out to Pol, he did not
argue. He thanked her, with a courtesy that was worse than a curse, for
coming to tell him herself. Then he went back to his wagons, and hid his
people, and hid his keys, and told the rising nothing at all about the
ring it was about to walk into.

She would have done the same, she thought. That was the thing about fear
they had all been too well-fed to remember. It doesn't argue. It just
stops helping you.

⁂

The assault went in at first light, and everything that went wrong went
wrong because the fair fought them blind.

No keys, so the menagerie undercroft stood unsecured at their backs, and
the carousel wolves came up out of it through the booth-rows. No
ring-lore, so nobody had told the Ashfold foragers about the tunes — and a
whole file of them walked off the flank toward the carousel, smiling,
helmets in their hands, following a music-box compulsion straight into
the ring, and had to be dragged back one by one with wax in every ear the
host could stop up. And the Warden — Hookmaster, a fair-cat welded into a
rig of grabbing-hooks off the strongman games — worked the fight like the
fight was a fixed stall, snatching soldiers off their feet and reeling
them through the wreckage, always where the carousel's turn had already
sickened their footing.

They broke it. Of course they broke it; twelve hundred veterans of
twenty-four floors, against one floor's worth of wrongness — the games
fell still, the carousel stopped for the first time in five years, the
tunes died mid-phrase, and the fever lifted off the fair like heat off a
griddle. The cost inside the battle was seven dead, which the host had
learned to call light.

The cost outside the battle was the village.

Nobody ever pieced together whose lamp it was — a dropped one, a thrown
one, it changed nothing. Fire took the toffee-nettle, ran up the
bunting-bindweed along the guy-ropes, and walked across the tent-tops
into the wagon-rows west of the ring, where the fair-folk were hiding
from their own liberation, in deep cover, in silence, exactly as they had
survived everything for five years — and silence is the one thing you
cannot do when the fire comes. The rising's soldiers pulled wagons apart
with their hands. Vega stood in the burning row and lifted a cook-van off
a family whole. The Ashfold fire-wardens spent their new spell until they
dropped. It wasn't enough, and it was never going to be, because the
people in the wagons had spent five years learning not to cry out, and
they didn't.

Thirty-one of Fairstead dead, and not one of them in the fight.

Ede stood in the black lane afterward while the lists were made. Pol
Fairstead walked the row behind her, not looking at her, and she did not
defend herself, then or ever. There was nothing to defend. Every argument
at the council had been sound, and the wagons had burned anyway, and both
of those things were true at once and would go on being true all the way
up the tower.

At dusk, Pol lit the Big Wheel.

Not for a crowd. The fair-folk stood in the ash of their own street and
watched the great ring of lamps kindle against the dusk, and Pol
worked the crank himself, one-armed with weariness, and when someone
asked him softly why now, he said: "Step right up," to nobody at all, in
a barker's voice with nothing left in it, and Ede understood that it was
a funeral, the only kind a fair knows how to hold — every light burning,
for thirty-one people who had hidden from the light, and been right to.

*"Count what you have learned,"* Sliver said in her palm, that night,
when she could not sleep. *"You knew the tower was your enemy. You have
met the second one now. It does not hold a floor. It holds the people,
and it was here before Vharuk, and it will not die at the summit."*

"Fear," Ede said.

*"Theirs," Sliver said. "And yours. You feared to be the kind of general
who overrules a frightened town, so you argued and washed your hands in
losing. The freed feared the quiet, so they shouted it down. Nobody in
that council served the fair. Every soul in it was paying an old debt to
themselves."* A pause, dry as chalk. *"I have carried forty-one climbers.
The ones who frightened me had stopped being frightened. Stay as you
are."*

In the morning the fair rose anyway — that was the bitter wonder of it;
eleven fair-folk mustered under the banners, tumblers and sharps and one
grim rigger whose wagon had burned, because, the rigger said, hiding
hadn't worked, so.

Pol Fairstead did not muster. He came to the column's head instead,
ring-keys on his belt, grey flag furled, to see the army off his
floor, and what he said to Ede at the gate went up the tower with
her. "You'll break more Wardens, captain; I don't doubt it now. Break
this too, then. The tower is only the stone. The prison is what we
learned to want. You can break stone." He looked back down the burned
street, where the great wheel still burned its funeral lamps and the
fair's children were out at last in the ash. "Now break the wanting."

Behind him, thin and clear over the ash-lane, the children were
already skipping rope to an old counting-rhyme of the Men floors —
*run from the horn and the horn runs after; walk to the horn and the
horn falls silent* — and Ede heard it without knowing she heard it,
the way you bank a coal against a colder year.

The host climbed toward the Slag Barrens with its
first unwilling floor behind it and the wheel-light on its back, and at
the rear of the column Ede watched the Cullers' grey pennons peel away
and drop down the freed lifts, Hale's companies taking up the rear-gate
watch across the floors the host had won — the Whisperwood, the Anvil
Commons, the silk-quiet Broodwarrens sleeping on their yielded word.

She felt nothing about it. It was sound soldiering; the rear had to be
held by somebody, and the Cullers held things well. Later — all the rest
of her life — she would remember that she felt nothing, and count it the
most expensive nothing she ever felt.
