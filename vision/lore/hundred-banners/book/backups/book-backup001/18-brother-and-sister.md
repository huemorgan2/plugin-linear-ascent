# Chapter 20 — Brother and Sister

The survey came to the forge-barge in a strongbox with two locks, and
Ulfar wore both keys on a chain around his neck, under the shirt, the
way other men carry a saint.

He laid it out for her at night, in the barge's chart-room, on the
light-table the sealers had used for reading the pack — a slab of
cold-glass lit from below by a steady aether lamp. Vellum first: forty
sheets, joined at the margins, in the drafting-hand of the deep halls,
three generations of it, the early sheets in inks long out of use. Then
the overlays. Then the figures, in ledgers with sewn spines. The
Forge-Court had been surveying the Ascent since before either of them
was born, and had never once said so out loud.

"Begin where I began," Ulfar said, and set his finger on the first
sheet, and the tower rose under the lamp in section: all hundred floors,
root to throne, drawn true.

Vega had seen the Ascent every day of her life. She had never seen it
*whole*. That was the tower's oldest trick — from inside, it is only
ever the floor you are on; the design does not fit through a human eye,
nor a giant's. But it fit on forty sheets of vellum, and laid out flat
under lamplight it was not a horror. That was the first thing that
frightened her. It was a *work* — the greatest made thing in the world,
welded joint by joint with a rigor she knew in her own hands, and her
hands, unbidden, respected it.

"Here," said Ulfar. "And here, and here." His finger moved up the
column, touching the welds between floors, one by one. "At every joint,
a grav-engine. The engines that lifted the floors. The thief's own
tackle, still rigged, still sound. We have taken their measure through
the walls for sixty years — mass, seat, feed, and state. Not one has
been removed. He built them in, sister. He could not take them out
without unbuilding his own tower, so he left them, and stopped their
ears, and trusted nobody would ever hold the throne but him."

"And the throne is the winch-house," Vega said slowly.

"The throne is the winch-house. Every engine in the column answers to
the core and to nothing else. We have tried for three generations to
wake one locally — the Court's whole cunning, against one engine, on a
floor we could reach. It cannot be done from below. The feed comes down
the tower's own spine, and the spine is lit from the core." He spread
the first overlay across the section, and the spine came alive under
the lamp, a hair-fine tracery running root to crown through every weld.
"Hold the core, and every engine in the Ascent answers you like a
bellows-crew."

"To do what?"

He looked at her across the table. Their mother's brow, their father's
patience, and underneath both, banked and old, a hope so large she
could feel the heat of it off him.

"To lower them," he said. "All of them. Gently. Home."

He walked her through it the way their mother had walked them through
their first casting: nothing skipped, nothing hurried, every claim set
down where she could strike it.

The floors could not simply be cut loose; she knew that, everyone knew
that, and his figures said it with a terrible tidiness — a floor is a
country, and a country dropped from the sky arrives as a graveyard. But
the engines had lifted every one of those countries once, mid-harvest,
mid-flood, without spilling a hay-rick, because the thief had needed
his takings whole. What had gone up gently could come down gently. The
ledgers laid it out: mass by floor, seat-loads at every weld, the order
of release — lowest floors first, each one borne down and seated into
the earth before the next unweld began, the column shortening from the
root like a candle burning in reverse. A chain's length a day, no more,
each floor's descent read against the strain-figures of every joint
above it. He gave her the rate tables. He gave her the margins, and the
margins' margins.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "A generation, bearing the whole world
down to where it was stolen from. Every field seated back into its own
scar. The delvers' caverns rejoined to the Deep. The elves' wood laid
piece by piece back into the elves' country. Nobody falls, Vega. Not
one soul, not one fence-post. That is what the engines are *for*. That
is what they have been waiting rigged to do for eighty years."

She went at the mathematics for half the night, because she was a
Coalbrow too, and because somewhere in the second hour she had
understood that she desperately needed it to be wrong.

She tried the couplings first: differential strain across a part-lowered
column, the live floors' engines bearing against the dead weight of the
seated ones. His tables had it, with the staging worked out weld by
weld. She tried the seats: a floor's underside is not a foundation, and
ground settles. He had bore-samples. He had the *original survey* — the
thief's own, lifted from a records-floor by two Court agents who had
spent eleven years on floor thirty-seven as door-wards to get it — every
floor's footprint, mapped against the scars still waiting in the earth
below. She tried the feed: could the core light the engines at
half-fire, quarter-fire, anything short of full? The load-curves said
what they said. The descent drew the core's full breath for thirty-one
years, no less, or the margins went to nothing and countries began
arriving as graveyards.

Three ways she went at it, and three ways it held. The mathematics
held. She set down the last ledger some time in the black hours of the
morning and looked at her own hands on the cold-glass, and said the
thing plainly, because giants do, even when it costs.

"The figures are sound, brother. I cannot fault them. It could be
done."

Ulfar let out a breath he had been holding, by the sound of it, for
twenty years.

"But," she said.

"The core," she said. "Say what it burns for, besides your engines. Say
the whole of it."

"The Yoke rides the core's fire," Ulfar said, evenly, because he had
known this was coming and had chosen not to lead with it, and she made
a note of that, in the flat ledger she kept of everyone, even him.
"Keep the core lit, and the Yoke is lit. For thirty-one years."

"On every floor."

"On every floor. Damped — we would hold the throne; the broadcast can
be throttled from the crown, we believe —"

"Show me the figure for that."

Silence, the first of the night. Beneath the chart-room floor the
freed sea groaned at its thaw. Ulfar turned to the last ledger, the
thin one, and did not open it.

"There is no figure," he said at last. "The Yoke is his craft, not
ours. We know the engines because the engines are honest iron. The
broadcast we know only by its shadow. That the core carries it, we
have proven. That it can be throttled and the engines kept — that is
the Court's judgment, and I share it, and it is a judgment, not a
figure."

"A hope," said Vega. "One page of hope, in the middle of forty sheets
of proof, and the page is the one the whole work stands on. Brother.
You have built a bridge of numbers across the world, and where it
crosses the gorge, you have painted the span in."

"And what has your rising painted?" He said it without heat; that was
the worst of it, he said everything without heat, the way she did, the
way their mother had. "You mean to scram the core. Say it plainly, that
is the plan behind the plan — break every Warden, take the throne, and
put out the fire. And then? The tower stands. Dead iron, root to crown.
The engines' ears stopped forever, because the only hand that could
ever wake them again is the fire you killed. A hundred countries
hanging in the dark on welds that will hold for — what? A century? Four?
Forever is not a figure either, sister. Your plan ends with the world
in the sky *for good*. Every farmer you free lives out his days
whole skies above his own *jorth* —" he caught himself, the old tongue of the
deep halls rising in him as it only did at the anvil, and set the word
down gently, like a hammer: "— above his own ground. And his
grandchildren after him, leaning on a fence in the wind, a world
above the fields their fields were cut from. The climb
gives the floors their days back. Only the engines can give back the
earth. There is no third door. It is my hope, or your never."

And there it was. She could not refute the mathematics. She had tried;
it held. She could only name the price, so she named it.

"Thirty-one years of the fever, Ulfar. Damped, you say — *believed*
damped, on a judgment with no figure under it. Thirty-one years of that
hum under every cradle in the world. You have not lived under it. I
have, floor by floor, for two years. You feel it lift when a relay
falls — the whole floor breathes, people weep and don't know why. Ask
any shard what it hears at night, even here, even now, with every
Warden dead below us. There is a hum under the world's floorboards,
and your plan keeps it humming for a generation, and calls it mercy."

"Against eighty years in the sky, sister. Weigh it."

"I am weighing it. Now weigh mine." She flattened her palm on the
overlay, over the spine, over the small bright thread of the core. "The
tower's crime is not its height. The crime is that a whole world's
ground answers to one throne. Your plan does not end that crime. It
*staffs* it. For thirty-one years, someone sits the winch-house, and
every country in the sky hangs from his good intentions — whose floor
descends this year, whose waits a decade, whose village sits at the
bottom of the ledger. The mathematics chooses, you'll say. Brother, a
ledger that chooses over people is a king. We have one of those. I have
seen what the world looks like when it is something a hand can hold,
and I have seen it from the wrong end, and I will not spend thirty-one
years watching a Coalbrow learn to like the weight of it."

"Oath-bound," said Ulfar. "Stewards, not lords. The Court would swear
on the first anvil —"

"Skarn believed his collar was a rank," Vega said, "and died believing
it, in a cage-yard, wearing my mark."

That reached him. She watched it land, and hated herself for the aim,
and would not have called it back. He stood very still over his forty
years of vellum, this brother she had built dams with out of casting-
sand, who had wept at their mother's forge the day Vega left the Court,
and had not argued, and had said only, *someone must stay and think of
the other way.*

"You mend people," he said at last, quietly. "It is your whole heart,
little sister. You have spent your life on the belief that everything
broken can be mended, and I watched you learn it at the same anvil
where I learned mine. Well — this is my mending. The world is the
broken thing, and the engines are the seam, and I have spent twenty
years drawing the weld. Do not stand there in our mother's face and
tell me the flaw in my craft is that I believe things can be fixed. I
learned that from *you*."

The lamp under the cold-glass hummed. The sea groaned. Somewhere aft, a
watch changed, boots on the deck-iron, ordinary as bread.

"No," Vega said, slowly, because it had taken her the whole night to
find it, and it was not a figure either. "The flaw is this. To mend a
sword, you keep the forge lit. To mend a *world*, you must somewhere
find the nerve to let the fire go out — because the fire is what broke
it. Your weld is beautiful, brother. And it anneals the one crack that
must not be annealed. He built a tower so the world would answer a
throne. You would keep it answering — kindly, gently, and for the best
reasons — for exactly one generation. Name me one fire in the history
of our people that was kept lit for exactly one generation."

Ulfar looked at his sheets a long time. When he looked up, his eyes
were their mother's too, and wet, and entirely unmoved. Both of those
at once. She would know the look until she died.

"Then we will argue it before the banners," he said. "You and I. In
council, in the open, like Coalbrows. And the rising will choose with
its eyes open, which is more than any king ever offered it."

"Yes," she said. "We will. And you will lose."

"Perhaps. I am a patient man, sister. The engines have waited
eighty years. They can wait out a vote."

He gathered the vellum, sheet by sheet, squaring the edges the way he
had squared everything since he was seven. At the door — filling it,
the way their whole family filled doors — he stopped, and did not turn,
and finished his sentence, because he always finished his sentences.

"I would rather lose the vote than you," he said. "Let the record show
it, if you keep one. I know that you keep one."

Then he was gone, down the ringing corridor, and Vega Coalbrow stood
alone in the chart-room with the lamp and the after-image of the whole
tower in section — the world as a hand could hold it — and put her
head down on the cold-glass, and did not weep. That she did privately,
later, in the same hour she wrote to Ede that the survey was sound and
the schism was real and the rising's hardest argument had arrived under
her own banner and her own name, wearing her own face.

The host marched on the fiftieth floor at the week's end. Ash came
down the stair-drafts two floors below it and fell grey on white, and
the sledge-runners hissed through it, and the air by degrees forgot
the sea and remembered fire. Ahead, at the mouth of a living caldera, in a
ring of pyres that had not gone cold in a thousand years, something
older than the tower — older than the argument, old enough to pity
everyone in it — lay coiled around its hoard, waiting for the rising
the way mountains wait for weather.

Brother and sister rode toward it in the same column, under different
banners, and did not speak of the vellum again. The argument was not
over. It had merely banked itself, the way a Coalbrow fire does — coals
under ash, patient, holding its heat for a floor neither of them had
seen yet, high in the Made, where a lever waits by a light that has no
color, and every mathematics comes at last to its price.
