# Chapter 30 — Gathering the Blown

Rhovan had spent a life finding things that did not want to be found,
and he had never hated the work until the things were his own.

The queen's gales had sown the third part of the rising across seven
floors of sky, and the sky's floors were not kind ground. A company
of freed kobolds came down whole in the Char Forest and lit no fire
for four nights among the black glass trees, because Grint Stumpwell's
burners found them first and taught them which trunks still held a
live coal. Two hundred of the Frosthall levy landed on the ledges of
the Drake Aeries, among hoards, among drakes, and had the wit to touch
no coin; the drake-keepers of Scalewatch talked the aeries calm over
them, ledge by ledge, an old truce spoken in signals across the fever
of a thousand wings. A supply-train fetched up against the Wind Walls
and its drovers lived a week in a giant's casemate on Berrin's wax-
sealed bread — the quartermaster's crates had scattered as far as his
people, and, being his, were packed to survive it.

Others fared as the wind willed. The Jarl's Road took thirty-one
of the blown into its ghost-march before anyone understood the road
itself was hungry — swept them into a column that had been tramping
toward a fallen feast for a thousand years — and Hune Horncall walked
the verges for six days pulling the living out of the dead procession
one man at a time. In the Egg Galleries the watchers of Palewatch cut
nine climbers free of the racks; four of the cocoons they opened too
late. And on the seventy-fourth floor, in the wire-seeded quiet of an
elf-wood gone all to snares, a serjeant of Shieldwall gathered forty
survivors and, quite correctly, refused to let them take a single
step, and sat in the leaf-litter for nine days, waiting to be found
by someone who could read a cocked wood.

Rhovan was who found them. That was the shape of his war, that
season: down a chain, along a rampart, into the wire, whistling the
old Rides recall-notes into country that answered with crows. Above
him Vega's eleven skiffs quartered the sky like patient hawks,
lamps burning the fleet's muster-signal, lifting the stranded off
ledges no stair could reach; below him the keepers of seven gate-
towns walked their own deadly floors, gathering strangers as if the
strangers were their own lost isles. He kept the tallies in his head,
where he kept everything. Found, four hundred and six. Found, six
hundred and sixty. Found, nine hundred and one — and every evening
he brought the numbers to the general's lamp and watched Ede Stonhal
take them the way a woman takes lashes she has agreed to.

She had stopped sleeping; anyone could see it. She heard each day's
count standing, then walked the recall-lines half the night, learning
the name of every soldier the wind had given back and, worse, the
name of every one it had not. The muster-roll she had rebuilt from
memory after the feast rode in her breast like a second heart, and
she was crossing names onto it and off it in her own hand, hour on
hour, as if the ink itself were owed. Rhovan, who told the truth in
fractions even to himself, permitted himself a whole one, privately:
he had watched a queen offer this woman the sky for a crown she did
not want, and it had cost her nothing to refuse; and he was watching
the refusal's price come due one name at a time, and *that* was
costing her everything, and she paid it every evening at the lamp
without once letting the line move behind her.

An army does not watch its general count the lost, night after
night, and stay a stranger to her. The blown came back changed. They
came back *hers.*

It was, all reckoned, Ede's worst hour as a general, and the finest
he ever saw her.

⁂

The Wake chose that hour to present its bill.

The council met in the Sheltered Casemate of the Wind Walls, the one
wind-proof room on a floor of screaming ramparts — giants' work, deep
in the greatest wall, warm with lamps and crowded with the charter's
remnant: Ede grey with waking; Vega; Ulfar; Voss with his ledgers;
two keepers; Nix in the corner on a crate, holding the banner and
saying nothing, which in Nix was a mark of the times. And Calyne.

She stood when the tallies were done, grey as ever, precise as ever,
and laid a strip of birch-paper on the table as though it were a
blade.

— The Wake's council has met, — she said. — Below this tier lie the
lamp-woods of the Elves. The Kennel Courts you freed in passing. The
Snare Woods and the Quarry Runs you now propose to *cross* — to walk
through our stolen realm, wire and coursing-lanes and the hanging
dead of the Gibbet Glade, and leave it as you found it, because the
Made is calling and your charter is in a hurry. The Wake has climbed
beside you since Roothollow. Its bows are on your walls tonight. Its
couriers carry your muster-calls. Here is its price for remaining:
the elf-woods detour. Now. The host turns, gentles, and holds the
lamp-wood floors — all of them — before it climbs a step higher. The
woods are cut down from *our* country, general. We are done watching
every people's realm freed but ours because ours is merely on the
way.

Silence took the casemate. Rhovan, by the door, felt the old
handler's arithmetic assemble itself in him, smooth from long use:
what the Wake wanted, what it would settle for, which fraction of
the truth would move which vote. He had run that arithmetic for
Calyne for twenty years.

— How long? — Ede asked. Flat.

— Three weeks, — Calyne said. — Perhaps five. The woods are not
gentle work.

— I still have eleven hundred people on the wind floors, — Ede said.
— On ledges. In wire. In the racks of a hatchery. Every skiff, every
courier, every bow on this tier is a hand reaching for them, and you
are asking me to take a third of those hands and send them to hold
ground for five weeks.

— I am not asking, — said Calyne. — I am pricing. You have had a
season of the Wake's silver and the Wake's arrows. Everything is
bought, general; you learned that at the Warcamp. The only question
is who pays, and when, and the Wake answers: its debt is due now, or
its service ends tonight. — She did not raise her voice. She never
raised her voice. — Refuse, and the bows come off your walls by
morning.

And Rhovan of the Grey Rides heard himself say, from the door: — No.

⁂

He had not planned it. He wanted that understood, later, by the two
or three souls he ever told it whole: he, who had planned everything
since boyhood, who had walked into Roothollow with a sealed ledger-
page and a blackmailer's patience, did the one unplanned thing of his
life in a giant's casemate on the seventy-third floor, and it felt
like stepping off a rampart into the wind.

Calyne turned. Everyone turned.

— Agent, — she said, and the word had twenty years of leash in it.

— The woods will keep. — His own voice sounded strange to him: it was
not offering fractions. — I have served the Wake since before this
army had a name, and I tell this council what every elf at this table
knows and will not say: the lamp-woods have stood cut and stolen for
eighty years. They grieve, and they wait, and they will still be
grieving and waiting in the spring, because that is what woods do.
The blown will not. The wire is at their throats *tonight*. The racks
are quickening *tonight*. — He came forward into the lamplight, and
laid his bow on the table beside her strip of birch-paper, gently,
like a man laying down an argument. — You taught me the trade,
mistress. You taught me a debt called at the worst hour is not a
debt, it is a knife. This is a knife. The Wake is holding it to the
throat of the only rising that will ever free the lamp-woods at all,
and I will not carry the message, and I will not draw for it. Not
now. The answer is the gathering first. The woods after — and I will
walk into the wire at the head of that campaign myself, when the last
name on the general's roll is found. That is *my* price, and I have
never once named one before.

— Your price. — Calyne looked at him for a long moment, and something
moved behind her eyes that he could not read — he, who read everyone;
it looked, absurdly, like a woman re-checking a sum that had come out
wrong in a column she had trusted. Then it closed. — The Wake does
not receive prices from its instruments. It bought you a name when
you were nothing: Rhovan *of the Grey Rides* — the Rides are ours,
agent, the name is livery, and livery comes off. Speak one word more
and you speak it as no one.

— Then take it back, — said Rhovan, — and hear the answer from what
is left: no.

He took the token from against his skin, where it had lain since he
was seventeen — a thin disc of grey heart-elm, smooth as a knuckle,
carved with the closed eye of the Wake — and he broke it over his
knee, and the crack of it was very loud in the stone room, and he set
the two halves on the table in front of his handler with the grain
showing pale where it had never seen light.

— The bows will stay or go as the Wake pleases, — he said to the
council, nameless, feeling the name go, feeling — he would tell Ede
this much, one evening much later — feeling lighter, and colder, as
if a hand had been let go at a great height. — I stay.

Calyne gathered the halves of the token with the strip of birch-
paper, unhurried, exact, and left the casemate without a word — and
the Wake's bows did not come off the walls by morning. That was her
answer, whatever it meant; he never learned what she wrote to her
council, or what it cost her, or whether the sum she had re-checked
in that long look had come out wrong in his favor. She was one floor
ahead of everyone all her life. He mourned that too, later.

⁂

Ede found him at the rampart-stairs after, in the wind-shadow, and
stood beside him awhile without speaking, which was one of her ways
of speaking.

— You gave up your name, — she said at last.

— I gave up livery. — A fraction; he caught it leaving his mouth,
old habit, and pushed the rest out after it, whole. — No. I gave up
my name, and it hurts more than I expected, and I would do it again
before supper. Put it in the roll however the roll wants me.

— The roll, — said Ede, — has you as Rhovan of the rising. It has
had you that way, — she added, already turning back to the lamps and
the lists, — since the Broodthrone. You're the last to know.

The gathering took eleven more days. The last company came off the
Quarry Runs' border on a skiff flying Dun Harrow's old signal, and
Ede stood at the tally-lamp and crossed the final found name onto
the roll, and the count of the never-found was three hundred and
twelve, and she read every one of them aloud to the assembled
banners in the wind, which took from moonrise until the fourth
watch, and no one left.

Then the scouts came in from above with the thing Rhovan had already
smelled on the wind: the tier ahead narrowing, the tempest bending
strangely around its own heart, and past the roar — so said the freed
courtiers of Zephyra's court, who knew this sky — a stillness. The
Eye Wall. A calm at the storm's center, gold and windless, wide
enough to rest an army in.

The host needed rest the way the drowning need air. It would follow
by stages, regiments and skiffs, three days behind. The leadership
would go ahead — Ede, Vega, Nix and the banner, and a nameless elf
who was the best pathfinder alive on this tier — to read the calm
before trusting an army to it.

They went up toward the stillness together, the four of them, ahead
of everything they loved. It seemed, at the time, merely sensible.

The wood above was already watching the gap between the shepherds
and the flock.
