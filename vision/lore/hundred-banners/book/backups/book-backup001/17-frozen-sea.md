# Chapter 19 — The Frozen Sea

Vega Coalbrow learned to forge without fire on the forty-first floor,
and it humbled her the way only a new craft can humble an old master.

The Moraine Gates were a country of gravel — ridge upon ridge of it,
ground and dropped by an ice-river that had left long before the Theft.
Balanced boulders stood where the ice had set them down. Coldjaw had
denned before the stair-scarp in a shell of ice and erratic-stone, and
it had taken the host two days and eleven lives to break the welded
thing and still the moraine; and when it was done, Bruna Coldquay came
out of the Erratic Hollow with the warmth-charm in one fist and the
ice-road markers in the other, and looked at Vega's forge-wagons the
way a reeve looks at a cart with a cracked axle.

"Your fires are no good upstairs," she said. "Fire-forged iron goes
brittle in the deep cold and shatters on the first hard knock. You want
rime-iron. You work it cold."

So Vega worked it cold. Rime-iron came out of the melt-exposed seams
below the Terminal Ridge, and it would not be hurried: you hammered it
by the hour, a little at a time, letting the metal warm itself from the
work alone, never from a flame. A forge-matron of the Coalbrow line,
who had lit her first furnace at seven and could sing a weld shut in
her sleep, stood at an anvil in the wind and learned patience from a
gravel-reeve. There was a kind of honesty in it. Heat lies, sometimes.
Work does not.

They built the sledge-trains through the winter camp and finished them
on the moraine: runner after runner of cold-hammered rime-iron, beds
lashed with bought Broodthrone silk, nine sledges to a train and a
train to every company. Bruna's ice-folk taught the drivers Kindle, the
warmth that a body could wear like a coat, and taught the cooks the
coldsblood brew, and marked the safe lines across the tarn where the
frozen scouts stood upright in the shallows like a harvest nobody had
come back for. Vega broke four of those with her own maul —
rime-crusted figures that walked the gravel dragging the living toward
the melt-runnels — and watched each one thaw to slush and drain away
among the stones, evicted. She did not enjoy it. She recorded that it
was the mercy, and moved her crews on.

The floors went by like the verses of a winter psalm. The Signal Deeps,
where dead racks stood in aisles of cold light and the delvers walked
softly, out of respect for something that was not quite a grave. The
Bone Garths, walled and picked and rattling with crows. The Ogre Steps,
kiln-terraces climbing into frost-haze, where three collared terrace
ogres threw down their mattocks at the sight of Nix's banner and sat
down at his fire, enormous and shy. The Sunken Chantry, where the
faithful of Bellmarsh sang the host across the flooded fonts by
vigil-light, and even Voss took his hat off. Five floors, five problems
of cold and weight and carry; Vega solved them the way she solved
everything, with iron, arithmetic, and not enough sleep.

Then the stair opened on the forty-sixth floor, and there were no more
problems, only the one.

The Frozen Sea lay under a low iron sky, and it was exactly what its
name confessed: a sea, frozen — the coolant-water of her own people's
deep forges, a reservoir the size of a stolen ocean, kept just above
freezing for a thousand years by the heat it drank from the furnaces
below. Then the tower took the forges and the forges went cold, and one
night the whole sea locked at once, with the sealers out on it.

Pressure-ridges heaved up across the pack like city walls. Leads of
black water opened and shut without warning. Brine-gulls cried over
the floes, and the wind came off the ice with
nothing to slow it for a horizon. It was the most beautiful country
Vega had seen in forty floors, and it wanted them all dead, and it did
not even mean anything by it.

Orla Floeside met the vanguard at the sea's frozen shore with an
ice-staff taller than a man and no patience whatever for heroes. She
was a sealer-elder, giant-blooded, frost-white to the eyebrows, and she
sounded every floe she stood on out of habit older than thought.

"The pack lies," she told the assembled captains, "and under the Yoke
it lies on purpose. Frost-flowers bloom thickest on the thinnest ice.
The groan you hear is not the floe that breaks; the one that breaks is
silent. Nobody crosses my sea in front of me."

Nobody did. The trains went out onto the pack in her bootprints, a
nation strung out in lines across a white plain, and Vega walked the
length of her train with her maul on her shoulder and listened to the
sea talk under her feet. It groaned like a full hold. It ticked like a
cooling forge. Once, away north, it made a sound like a door closing,
and Orla stopped the whole column with one raised fist, stood listening
for the time a lamp takes to gutter, then moved them all four hundred
paces west without explaining, and nobody asked.

The floe-wolves came at dusk on the third day, white out of white. They
did not attack the sledges. That was the fever's cunning: they came in
wide crescents, silent, showing themselves only at the flanks, and they
*herded* — turning the rear teams gently, patiently, toward a lead that
lay skinned over with new ice and flowered with frost. It nearly
worked. A whole sledge was on the skin before the driver knew, the ice
sagging under the runners like wet vellum, dogs screaming, black water
breathing underneath.

Vega had learned Icebind from the sealers in a single evening, because
it was only quenching turned backward. She knelt at the lead's edge,
put both palms to the skin of it, and quenched. The water took the bind
the way steel takes a temper: a crack, a shudder, and the lead stood
solid, and the sledge slid across groaning onto sound pack. Then the
shield-line turned and did its work on the wolves, and that was crueler
and kinder at once, the way it always was with the fevered. Each one
that fell shed its wrongness like a coat of rime: the frost-armored
horror dropped away and left an ordinary wolf, lean and grey and
blinking, that gathered itself up off the ice and loped away across a
sound floe, cured. They were never evil, she reminded her crews, who
knew it. They were sick. Steel is the only exorcism on offer up here.

The coolant-wights were worse, because they had been sealers once.
Rime-shrouded figures that walked the pack on the old hunting-lines and
opened leads under the living — aether caught in the ice with the dead,
wearing the shapes of Orla's own drowned. The host broke nine of them
crossing the sea. Orla named each one before Vega's maul came down, and
afterward stood at each closing lead until the water stilled, and Vega
stood with her. Two giants at a graveside. Neither said anything. There
was nothing to say that the sea did not already know.

On the fifth day they came over the Great Ridge, and the Locked Fleet
lay below them in the pack like a drowned town.

Masts first — a forest of them, raked and rimed, standing out of the
ice at the angles the freeze had caught them. Then the hulls: eleven
floe-boats, broad-beamed sealers' craft, and in the middle of them,
vast as a guildhall, the forge-barge — a floating forge-hall of the
deep works, chimneys capped with ice, caught fast on the night the sea
locked, her fires still lit. Orla stood on the ridge a long time,
looking down.

"My grandmother's boat is the third from the left," she said. "We
walked out to them the first winter, to strip the holds. Couldn't do
it. It would have meant believing the sea was never coming back."

"The sea is coming back," Vega said. Slowly, the way her people said
things they intended to be true. "I am going to fetch it."

Fire was no use; she knew that before she started. Flame against an
iron hull in deep cold splits the plates and burns the rigging, and the
ice, insulted, only grips harder. What she did instead took three days
and three nights, and it was the finest work of her life to that hour.
She sank warm-brine channels around each hull, sounding as she went;
she set the Kindle-callers of four companies working in shifts to her
measure, gentling heat into the ice — not melting it, *persuading* it,
a breath of warmth at a time, the way a fist forgets an anger. She sang
to the iron the long slow forge-songs that tell a metal what shape it
is, so the hulls would remember themselves as boats and not as bones.
And finger by finger, grudging every knuckle, the sea let go.

The forge-barge came free on the third night with a groan they heard in
the camps two floes away, and settled into the black water of her own
thaw-pool, floating, upright, whole. Orla's sealers wept openly on the
ice. Vega climbed aboard among them and walked the cold forge-hall with
a lamp, and lit the barge's great hearth with her own hands — the first
fire the sea had allowed in a generation — and stood in its light while
the sealers cheered, feeling, for once, entirely clean.

Then she found the manifest. It hung on the rail in a tin case, and it
listed the cargo the barge had carried when the freeze took her, and
halfway down the list, in a clerk's tired hand: collar-blanks, four
hundredweight, Coalbrow mark.

They were in the third hold, stacked like ingots, stamped with the
hammer-and-ring she had worn on her own seal since girlhood. Four
hundredweight of collars that had never reached their necks only
because a sea froze. Vega stood over them for a long while. Then she
carried them up to the new-lit hearth one crate at a time, letting no
one help her, and struck every blank into boat-nails and sledge-shoes,
and said nothing about it to anyone. Mending is not absolution. But it
is the shape absolution would have, if it ever came.

Floeback met them at the sea's far edge, where the stair-scarp stood
sheathed in blue rime — a swell troll grown vast and welded under a
shell of pressure-ice that regrew as fast as it broke, heaving whole
floes on its shoulders to spill the host into the leads. Its one tactic
was a good one: make the ground itself a trap-door, drag the battle
into the black water. It had worked for eighty years on everything
the sea had sent against it.

It had never fought a fleet. The freed floe-boats came on under sail
and oar with shield-lines at their gunwales, and the ice the Warden
heaved was only weather to a hull. Vega stood in the forge-barge's bow
with her palms out and thawed the welded shell as fast as it regrew —
her new craft and her old one in the same two hands, heat and cold in
harness — and the boats closed in from three sides, and it ended the
way such things end. When the shell finally cracked, what drained out
of the welded core went down through the ice like smoke through a
grating, evicted, and the swell troll underneath — huge and slow and
blinking, cured, never an ice-thing at all, only a Deep-dweller the
forge-shafts had brought up long ago — put down the floe it was
holding and lumbered off across the pack to find a way home and down.

The pack settled. The leads closed. In the Melt-Grotto the sealers
opened the coolant-sluice, and somewhere far below, warmth their world
owed them began, at very long last, to be repaid. By morning the whole
sea was groaning — not the war-groan of the pack, but something longer
and deeper and glad, the sound of an ocean remembering water. The old
floe-roads would carry the host to the stair before they softened. It
was, Orla said, the exact size of miracle a sealer could trust.

The Forge-Court came up the freed lifts and out onto the ice on the
last morning, and Vega heard them before she saw them: horns of the
deep halls, slow and enormous, the marching-note her childhood had gone
to sleep to.

A legion. A thousand giants in slab-mail, in column, with engines on
grav-sleds and coal-wagons and a rolling smithy, crossing the pack in
perfect order past the standing masts of the Locked Fleet. At their
head walked a thane she would have known at any distance, because he
had their mother's brow and their father's way of walking as if the
ground should be grateful — Ulfar Coalbrow, her brother, whom she had
not seen since the day she left the Court with her forge-mark burning
in her mind like a brand.

He picked her up. In front of both armies, in front of her own crews, a
matron of the Coalbrow line, and swung her like a door — and she was
laughing before she could stop herself, because he was her brother and
he was *here*, and the whole white world smelled suddenly of home: coal
and iron and the cold-tallow soap of the deep halls.

"Little sister," he said. "You look thin. Your host looks thinner. We
have brought food, coal, forges, bridging-engines, and a thousand of
the Court's best, and I have brought you an apology twenty years old. I
mean to make it at supper, at length, sitting down."

Welcome muscle. The camps cheered the column in, and there was reason:
the rising had been outrunning its supply for ten floors, and here came
the deep halls with the weight of a whole people behind them. That
night the forge-barge's hall held the first high table of the whole
climb — sealers and thanes, keepers and captains, Berrin beaming over
his manifests like a man whose prayers came itemized.

But Vega was a smith, and a smith reads a work by where the maker's
eyes go. Through the whole of that bright loud supper, her brother's
surveyors never once looked at the freed floors, the freed fleet, the
freed people. They looked at the welds. They stood at the hall's ports
with plumb-lines and cold-glass, taking the measure of the great
anchor-chains where they ran up into the dark, and writing.

And the banner. The Court's banner had been the hammer and the hearth
since before the Theft. What flew over the legion's rolling smithy was
new work: the Ascent itself, the whole tower, stitched in black — not
breaking, not burning. Standing. And beneath it, cradling it, a giant's
open hand.

She asked him plainly, at the end of the night, when the hall had
thinned and the two of them stood alone at the ports with the sea
groaning its thaw below. Giants do not ambush one another; it is not in
the grain. She asked what the Forge-Court had come for.

And because he was a giant too, and her brother, and had never in his
life left a sentence unfinished, he told her.

"We have come to keep this host alive across the ice, and to put our
shoulders under your war, and to see the Demon King cast down," he
said. "And when he is down, sister — we have not come to break the
tower. We have come to inherit it."

The sea groaned under them, long and deep, the sound of something
enormous letting go. Vega stood beside her brother in the lamplight and
understood that the rising had just been joined by its largest ally and
its next war, and that they had arrived in the same column, under the
same banner, wearing her mother's brow.

"Bring me the survey," she said at last. "All of it. Every figure. If
you are going to be wrong, brother, be wrong in numbers, where I can
show you."

"And if I am right in numbers?" said Ulfar.

She did not answer. Below them the Frozen Sea worked at its own
unlocking, patient, enormous, indifferent — and ahead, past floors of
moor and meadow and frozen forest, something older than the tower was
waiting in a ring of fire for whichever of them the mathematics spared.
