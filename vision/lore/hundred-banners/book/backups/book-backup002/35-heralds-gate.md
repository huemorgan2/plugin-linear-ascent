# Chapter 41 — The Battle of the Herald's Gate

Vega Coalbrow had built many things in a long life, and broken more, and
she stood in the dark before the battle with her hand flat on the flank
of the last thing she would ever need to build, and finished her
sentence, because her people finished their sentences even when the
sentence was a prayer.

"I have put into you the brass of the chapels that taught the bound to
worship their chains, and the brass of the horns that shouted the thief's
glory, and the steel of the yards that quenched the shackles, and my own
mark, which I once put into collar-steel and have spent this whole climb
buying back. You are the apology of four floors and one smith. Ring
true."

The bell said nothing. Bells don't, until asked properly. It hung from a
gantry of grown engine-iron on a carriage the freed parade hulks had
hauled up the Herald's Road in the night, and it was the size of a
cottage, and it had no name, because every name the council proposed had
been too small. The freed just called it the bell.

Around it, in the last dark, the rising formed up for the greatest
battle of the age, and Vega walked the line the way a smith walks a
finished weld, looking for flaws and finding, floor by floor, the whole
climb standing in ranks. Rivet-steel shields out of the First Outwork.
Chain-flails and grapples of quench-steel from the yards. Grate-iron
griddle-shields; seam-steel plate off the racks, reforged for the freed,
not the bound; the drilled regulars of the Bastion Line standing at
Bastionheart's own parade-rest; Fluegrim's warren-soldiers in the
tower's own livery; the garden-thralls' grown rams and towers creaking
at the rear on part-ripe limbs. Two years ago this army had been one
disgraced captain, one lying archer, one guilty smith, and a goblin
hiding behind a dropped standard. The tower had spent eighty-nine floors
arming the thing that had come to unmake it.

In the night the line had given the ground its own names, as lines do,
because a soldier will not die at a map-mark. The stretch of yard-iron
where the shields would plant was the Anvil. The lane kept clear behind
the center for the bell-carriage was the Bell-Road. The flat
killing-ground under the gatehouse's fixed lines the Bastion men called
the Alley, with a drilled man's flat humor, and nobody crossed it
standing tall. And the yards themselves ran beyond any seeing — a
fortress-square the size of a lowland shire, wall to far wall a hard
day's march, the King's banners on the distant ramparts reading as
weather, not cloth. Vega walked the front of all that and looked down
the ranks, floor stacked upon freed floor in one iron morning, and
thought the thought the whole muster was thinking, the one the
Frosthall had signed and the climb had spent two years proving: not a
tower. A stair.

And past the last shield stood the muster-yards of the Herald's Gate,
and Malgrim's answer.

The King's legions had been forming up since the gate swallowed Berrin
Or — rank on iron rank filling the fortress-square from wall to wall,
imps and hellknights and gate-tender hulks, ballista-lines on the
gatehouse, banners of the King hanging like judgments, and over it all,
on the high drum-dais they called the drum-throne, the Herald himself,
beating the muster. The drums never stopped. They had beaten all night,
and the beat did a thing to the blood that the chantry-priests said was
kin to the liturgy: it told the bones *you are a soldier, you have
always been a soldier, there is nothing under the collar but the drum.*

Ede came down the line in the iron morning, and stopped at the bell, and
looked up at it a while.

"The whole book of the war is in that brass," she said. "Every floor
gave it a page. You're certain of the ringing?"

"I am certain of the ringing," Vega said slowly. "I am certain of the
priests, and of the word, and of my seams. What I cannot promise is what
no smith has ever promised: what the metal remembers. If it remembers
the chapels, it will only frighten them. If it remembers what I asked of
it —" she laid her hand on the cold flank once more — "then today the
rising fights a battle mostly by declining to fight it, and I will have
mended, before I die, one thing of the size of the thing I broke."

Ede nodded, and did not offer comfort, which from her was a kindness,
and went to her place under the hundred banners. Away down the ranks
Vega could see Nix at the head of his collar-breakers — four hundred of
them now, in threes and fives, the Kettle at his shoulder, the freed
imps of six floors wearing Passfor like a borrowed coat, ready to walk
into the enemy as the enemy — and she could see the chantry-priests,
two thousand strong, forming their choir behind the bell-carriage in
ranks deeper than any shield-wall, and she thought: no army
in any history was ever drawn up like this one, with its heaviest engine
a mercy, and its elite trained above all else in the touching of an
enemy's throat *gently.*

The sky, for what it was worth, was empty. The Storm Queen's gales,
promised by embassy and sworn by treaty-debt, were reported by the
sky-skiffs to be two floors below and beating upward against their own
contrary winds. Rhovan, hearing it, had said only: "Of course they are,"
and gone back to fletching. No promised relief in any tale worth telling
has ever arrived on time.

The drums quickened. The gates of the forming-yards swung. Vega Coalbrow
spat on her hands, took up the maul she had forged for her
great-grandmother's forge and carried up a hundred floors, and the
Battle of the Herald's Gate began.

It began with steel, because it had to.

That was the bitter arithmetic the council had swallowed whole nights
choking on: the bell could not ring at once. Nix's threes and fives
needed time to walk deep into the mustered ranks under Passfor's coat;
the priests could not carry the word past the drums' own thunder unless
the choir stood close, and the choir could not stand close until the
enemy's charge was spent against the shields; the whole mercy of the day
hung on the rising's line holding, unbroken and unhelped, through the
worst hour the Made could hammer out of its yards. So the battle the
histories would remember for its gentleness opened as ugly as anything
since the Red Warcamp, and Vega fought that hour in the crush of the
center hinge, where the gate-tender hulks came on like siege-towers
walking.

The first charge was drummer-driven and total. Ballista-bolts came down
the fixed lines off the gatehouse in flat black sleet; the shield-line
staggered, planted, held; the drilled regulars of the Bastion Line took
the shock at the hinge with a discipline the tower itself had spent an
age beating into them, and gave it back with interest. A hulk broke the
third rank's corner and Vega met it maul to gate-arm, her feet furrowing
the yard-iron, and it was strong the way engines are strong, and she was
strong the way mountains' daughters are strong, and she broke its knee
and wept while she did it, because under the plate was some great
captured soul that had hauled gates for a thief for fourscore years, and
her maul could not tell it so.

That was the hour's true wound, worse than the bolts. Every blow the
rising struck before the ringing was a blow against the bound. The line
chanted Break-collar where it could — a shield-man's free hand on a
fallen imp's throat between one press and the next, the word said
through gritted teeth — and collar by collar, even in the crush, the
count that Nix had started at the First Outwork went on climbing. But
the drums beat doubt back into whatever the word loosened; the freed
staggered up only to be ridden under by the next driven rank; and the
dead of the enemy, heaped where the shield-line met the charge, were
climbing too, a heap of deaths that should not have been needed, each
one of them somebody's, each one of them the tower's oldest crime
committed one more time with the rising's own hands.

Vega fought, and counted seams, and behind her, rank by deliberate rank,
the choir came up the killing-ground with the bell.

She heard the fighting change before she saw why. Deep in the enemy's
mass — far beyond the line, out where no soldier of the rising could
possibly be — collars were breaking. Faint, then not faint: that
ice-off-a-rope crack, in fifties, in the enemy's own rear ranks, and
around each crack a knot of stillness spreading in the charge like calm
spreading in a pot taken off the fire. The threes and fives had gone in
under the borrowed coat, and the collar-breakers were doing their work
in the heart of the King's army, in the crush, in the dark of it, one
gentle hand on one throat at a time. She could not see Nix. Nobody could
see Nix; that had always been the point of Nix. But she could read his
path across the enemy's formation the way you read a mole's run across
a lawn — a raised seam of confusion, of ranks turning inward, of drums
suddenly drumming at their own soldiers — and she found she was grinning
in the middle of a battle-line with her maul running red, because the
smallest soldier of the rising was carving the war's whole meaning into
its greatest battle, and the tower had no craft to answer him. How do
you drum a soldier back into the collar you are standing on the wrong
side of? How do you press a man who has, this very hour, been *unpressed*?

By mid-morning the field had settled into a shape, and the shape held
long enough to be learned: the shield-line bowed across the yards like
a drawn bow, anchored at the Anvil where its hinge took the weight;
the choir and the bell coming up the Bell-Road behind the bow's grip,
slow as a tide-mark; the enemy's mass pressed on the line's whole
outer face, deepest at the hinge, fed by the gatehouse down the Alley;
and out in that mass, past any help, the threes and fives — a scatter
of freed hands nobody could see and anybody could read, stillness
spreading from them in rings, like rain beginning on a dusty road.

The Marshal answered. Malgrim's second — a captured officer collared so
long and so hard that his men said the collar had grown a man around it,
rather than the reverse — wheeled the whole second echelon off the
center and drove it not at the shield-line but at the bell.

He had seen what the day was, and he spent his legions the way you
spend coin you despise: at the choir, at the carriage, at the gantry of
grown iron, at the two thousand unarmored singers walking a bell into a
battlefield at a hymn's pace. Ballistae found the range. The carriage
lost a wheel and the hulks bore the axle on their shoulders. Vega left
the hinge — Ulfar's Forge-Court legion closed the gap behind her, her
brother's voice roaring slow giantish oaths she had known since the
cradle — and she got her shoulder under the low side of the axle, into
the place a wheel had been, and walked.

She would remember very little of that walk. A bolt took the priest
walking at her elbow and the priest behind him stepped over the body
and kept the line of the hymn unbroken. The wave of the Marshal's
echelon broke against the drilled square that Bastionheart had thrown
around the carriage, drill against drill, the tower's discipline
fighting the tower's discipline, and the square held, and the carriage
rolled, and the yards of the Herald's Gate came on and on beneath her
boots as slowly as anything in her slow long life. Later she learned
the walk had lasted from mid-morning to noon straight up. It had
felt, she said once, and never said
again, like carrying her own forge-mark the last mile back to the
forge.

At noon the carriage braked. The square opened its north face. And in
the sight of both armies, under the drum-throne of the Herald of the
King, the choir of the freed chantries drew one breath and began the
Recant, and Nix's voice — Nix's, of course, whose whole body was
lighter than the striker's counterweight, riding the gantry with a
hammer-cord in both fists, an imp of the rising above the ranks of the
imps of the King — cried the word that eighty-nine floors had waited
for, and swung.

*Break-collar.*

The bell rang.

⁂

Vega Coalbrow stood under her own bell when it spoke for the first
time, and what she felt was not sound. Sound was the least of it, the
carrier, the cart. What rolled out of the brass and across the
fortress-square at the pace of a slow tide was the *word* — the word as
the priests had sung it into the casting for nine nights, the word as
four hundred collar-breakers had said it one throat at a time up the
whole height of the world, Break-collar and Break-chain and Unseam and
Recant and Countermand gathered into a single spoken mercy the size of
a battlefield — and it went through the King's mustered legions the way
thaw goes through an ice-field.

The Pressed ranks halted mid-charge.

Not as men balk. As frost breaks. All across the yards, in a wave
front running outward from the bell faster than any charge, the
collars snapped — thousandfold, that same small cracking she had first
heard in a mine-dark cage on floor twenty, multiplied past counting
into a sound like a frozen sea letting go of a fleet all at once — and
the army of the Herald's Gate sat down.

That was what the histories would never get right, Vega thought,
standing in the ringing after. They would paint a rout, or a
surrender, banners falling, weapons flung. What happened was smaller
and enormous: freedom took the legs. Rank on iron rank, the imps and
the knights and the hulks of the King's last great muster sat down in
the yards where they stood, weapons loose in their hands, and stared
at their own fists as though the fists had just been handed back, and
began — helmed heads bowing, iron mouths working — to say names.
Their own. Their lost. A hundred thousand soldiers of the tower,
remembering at one stroke that they were somebody, inside a battle
that had been arranged specifically so that there would be someone
standing by, in threes and fives, to tell each one of them: nobody
drives you. Ever again. The enemy's line of battle did not break. It
*woke.* An army was freed inside an army; the rising's ranks grew in
the middle of its greatest battle by more than every muster it had
ever counted, and the whole vast engine of the day simply stopped,
because a battle requires two armies, and now there was only one, and
it was everyone's.

From the gatehouse wall, where the freed clerks of Lastmuster stood
watching their whole world change owners, the wave itself could be
seen, and their telling of it became the age's: a ring of stillness
widening out from the bell through a hundred thousand standing
soldiers, the way a struck cymbal stills under a laid finger — and
that ring, held one breath at its widest, every rank inside it seated
and every rank beyond it still charging, was the picture the histories
kept: the drawn line, moving outward, between the tower and whatever
came after it.

Not all. Nothing in the tower was ever all. The Wrongmade fought on,
having nothing in them to free — the living chains, the wall-flesh
things off the gatehouse — and were broken and evicted by crews who
worked now almost tenderly, the day's rage gone out of everyone. A
core of the deep-collared, too — souls like the Marshal's, worn so
long that the collar had rooted past any single word's reach — reeled
back toward the gatehouse in fighting order around the drum-throne,
perhaps one man in twenty, the bell's wave washing off them like water
off old grease. The Recant reached some of those, choir-sung, one at a
time, through the afternoon. The Marshal it reached last of all,
kneeling, his blade planted, his eyes shut against it like a man
refusing a sunrise; and when his collar went at last it went quietly,
and he carried his sword to Ede across the yards laid flat on his two
palms, and could not speak at all.

And through it all, over it all, the drums.

Malgrim beat the muster from the drum-throne through the whole of the
breaking, and Vega, watching that far figure drum for ranks that were
no longer his, understood down in her smith's bones what the morrow's
parley would be, and grieved it in advance. The drum said *you are a
soldier, there is nothing under the collar but the drum,* and ten
thousand of the freed sat in the yards weeping with their helms off,
proof it had always been a lie — and still he beat it, kind,
reasonable, wrong, the one soul in the fortress-square the bell could
not touch, because his was the one collar in the tower that had been
put on from the inside. No one stormed the drum-throne. It would not have been
needed. His army had been not defeated but *dismissed*, man by man, by
a better word than his; the gate below him was already thick with the
freed unbarring it from within. The Herald of the King beat the muster
alone over an empty yard, to no one, for no one, and the sound of it —
Vega would say this much, once, to her brother — was the loneliest
made thing she ever heard, and she had heard the Yoke itself.

The drums were still beating when the sky arrived.

It came over the south wall at mid-afternoon with a sound like the
world clearing its throat: the gales of the Tempest Court, treaty-sworn
and two floors tardy, Zephyra's whole unbottled cavalry riding down
onto a battle that had been over since noon. The banners of the rising
stood straight out from their poles. The wind swept the fortress-
square from wall to wall exactly once, majestically, gathering up the
last knots of the deep-collared rearguard still skirmishing at the
gatehouse stair and depositing them, disarmed and upside down, in a
quench-trough; it snuffed what fires the ballistae had started; and
then it wheeled above the yards as if awaiting applause.

Rhovan put it best, as he put everything best, watching the gale-
riders dress their glittering ranks over a field of the sitting freed.
"A battle late," he said, to nobody, an elf's reckoning of a debt,
"and they'll want a verse in the song regardless. Give them the verse.
The wind can have the tidying. The noon belonged to the bell."

They rang it once more at dusk — not for battle, for the dead.

The rising buried its own below the south wall, and buried the
Pressed who had died before noon with the same rites in the same
earth, because the difference between those two burying-grounds had
been the entire meaning of the day, and there was to be no difference.
Vega worked the graves with her crews, as smiths did, setting iron
markers, and the count was cruel enough — the morning's steel had
cost the rising more than any day since the Frosthall, and cost the
bound worse — and the count was also this, cried down the lines at
the lamp-lighting by a goblin voice hoarse as a hinge: the rising had
marched to the Herald's Gate that dawn some sixty thousand strong,
and camped that night, inside the taken yards, at more than twice it.
The greatest battle of the age, and the rising had won it by refusing
to fight nine parts in ten — had won an army, not off a field, but
out of one. Every history of every war ran the other way. That was
the whole crime of the tower, Vega thought, and the whole answer to
it: it had believed, as every conqueror believed, that the sum of a
battle must be subtraction.

She was banking the forge-wagon's fire when Nix found her, the Kettle
looming behind him with two bowls. The goblin looked entirely spent,
and entirely at peace, and about nine years old.

"I counted," he said, "you'll want to know, I counted the whole way
down the yards, I've been counting for two years and I want it said
out loud to a person who builds things. Since the outworks. Since the
first trench on eighty-one." He held up his slate, and on it was a
single number, and the number was larger than any muster-roll of the
whole climb, and under it he had written, in his careful,
taught-late, fair-day letters: *joined, not lost.*

"Say it to me tomorrow also," Vega said slowly, "when we go up to the
drum."

The drums had stopped at moonrise. That was somehow worse; the whole
camp heard the silence. Up on the gatehouse the freed had hung a
hundred banners over the King's iron, and behind the opened muster-
gate the stair-lift to the throne-tiers stood in its arch, lit and
waiting, one Warden from open — and on the drum-throne over the empty
yard sat the Herald of the King, his sticks laid across his knees,
watching the rising's cook-fires the way a man watches weather coming,
waiting, everyone understood, to talk. He had sent down, in a clerk's
flawless ledger-hand, a note of three lines.
Ede had read it and passed it round the fire without a word.

*Item: your bell. I heard it. Come up in the morning and tell me what
you think it proves, and I will tell you what I know it costs. Bring
the thirty. I have something of theirs to return — a ledger entry does
not retire.*

Somewhere behind the gate, in the iron dark, in the last unfreed rooms
of a nearly freed world, a man who smelled of bread was learning what
the tower paid its willing. In the morning the rising would climb to
the drum-throne and parley with the best talker in the Made, and Ede
Stonhal would look her dark mirror in the eye, and after that there
would be a judgment to give that no bell could give for her.

The banners moved all night above the taken gate, a hundred of them,
restless in the last of Zephyra's punctual-at-last wind. Vega slept
under the bell, as she would every night now until the top, and the
cooling brass ticked over her like a great slow heart, remembering,
seam by seam, everything it had been before it was melted, which was
the tower's story too, and the world's, and would be the morning's
work, and the next floor's, until the whole stolen country of the sky
was rung back down to earth.
