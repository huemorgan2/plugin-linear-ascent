# Chapter 37 — The Wake's Price

The grain stopped moving on the second day of the muster, and it took
another day for the reason to climb the gate roads, and when it came
it came addressed, in part, to a man with no name.

Rhovan was at the arrow-counts when Voss found him — the
quartermaster-general moving at the pace he reserved for money in
danger, two clerks trotting. The rising was three days from
touching the first rampart, and an army three days from a siege
eats like a siege already.

"Bayhollow," Voss said, without greeting, which was his greeting.
"The gate below the Egg Galleries. Shut since yesterday's dawn —
a chain across the lift-arch, hired bows on it, and seventy banners'
worth of bread on the wrong side. I have eleven days of full
rations at this camp, elf. Eleven." He held out the letter. "It
came up under a grey seal. It names you."

The letter was a strip of birch-paper, and Rhovan knew the hand
before he knew the words: not Calyne's — the council's own
scriveners, the hand that had paid him for twenty years.

*To the general of the rising, at her muster. Item: below your
camps lie the lamp-woods of the Elves, freed in the passing of your
army as a road is freed of weather. Item: freed is not restored.
The wire stands slack in ten thousand snares, and slack is not
gone; the lamps of nineteen fragments of the old wood stand dark,
and dark is dark. Item: a price was named in the Sheltered Casemate
of the Wind Walls by the instrument formerly styled Rhovan of the
Grey Rides — the woods after the gathering, himself at the head of
the work, when the last name on the general's roll was found.
Item: the last name is found. The roll is closed. The debt stands
due. The gate at Bayhollow opens when the woods are made whole, and
not a wagon sooner. The Wake's bows are recalled from your walls;
its seat at your charter stands vacated. The Wake wishes the rising
victory entire, and will not feed it one more day on credit.*

Rhovan read it twice and heard, under the scrivener's hand, what
the letter was actually saying: *you left. See what your word is
worth where it was given.* Every item true, every truth aimed. That
was the poison of it: the Wake would strangle the one army in the
age of the tower that could deliver its own purpose, over the order
of entries in a book.

Ede read the letter standing, grey with the muster and with the
something else he had chosen not to read.

"No," she said. "We don't clear freed ground with soldiers. That's
the tower's answer." She folded the letter and held it out to
Rhovan. "It's your bill. Go down and pay what you can, short of the
army. The muster can't spare me and the iron won't wait. Take Voss.
Nothing frightens a blockade like an honest man who wants it
priced."

Berrin walked them to the skiff-stage in the morning dark, an old
man doing up other people's buckles, and loaded Rhovan down with
two sacks of loaves — "for the queue, mind, not the parley; feed
the ones stuck standing first. Nobody argues honestly on an empty
stomach, and that goes double for elves, no offense to present
company." Rhovan watched him wave from the stage and thought that
the rising ran on two arts — Ede's, of making people brave, and
Berrin's, of making them fed — and that history would only ever
write down the first.

Two days down the gate roads taught Rhovan what a chain across one
arch does to a country of countries.

The blockade itself was nothing: a length of tower-iron slung
across the lift-arch at the coursing-lane's end, and on the stands
above it two hundred hired bows of Men, toll-road companies with
their pay in Wake silver and their faces arranged in the studied
blankness of men holding a bridge nobody had attacked. But below
the arch the queue began, and the queue did not end. Grain-wagons
stood axle to axle down the whole vale of the runs, out of sight
into the evening, five floors of harvest walking uphill into a
wall: ox-teams grazing the verges, drovers' fires in the wheel-
shadows, freed folk of nineteen peoples sitting on their own sacks
in a wood that had hunted them a season ago and fed them now. The
smell of it reached a mile up the lane — bread wanting to be bread,
going over sweet and sour in ten thousand sacks while the arch
stayed dark. Voss took one look from the last rise and went down
into the queue with his clerks, pricing spoilage, buying verge-
feed, writing the rising's paper against grain that could still be
ground.

Nael Bayhollow met Rhovan at the gate-town with his tally in his
fist like a hammer. The quarry-warden had kept run-lore alive
since the Theft, had lived to see the runs crossed at last, and was
now watching his floor's first free harvest rot before his own
arch.

"I want it said," he told Rhovan, in the keeper's voice that is
never quite shouting, "that this wood was freed by a goblin's soup,
a giantess's hands, a dead hound's collar, and an army of every
people but mostly not mine. And the first thing my own people build
on it is a *toll*. You go up to that table and say Nael Bayhollow
is ashamed in front of his floor."

The Wake had come into the open. Grey canvas among the run-oaks, a
camp without one banner, elves at their evening work who did not
lower their hoods — faces he had never seen and had corresponded
with for decades, signatures walking around on legs. For fourscore
years the Wake had been a rumor with a purse. Now, with its purpose
lying freed all around it and nothing left to hide from, it had
surfaced into the daylight of the world at last — and its first
public act in the age of the tower was a chain. The grief of that
was so exact that Rhovan stood a moment in the lane and let it have
him.

They received him at a trestle table under the hunting-arch: three
elders of the three lamplighter houses, and between them, under
oiled cloth, a book he had never seen and had served his whole
life — the Leaf-Ledger, current for the age of the tower, out under
open sky for the first time since the grove it was founded in.

The eldest did not open with his name, there being none to open
with.

"You were our best," she said. "Enter that first. The Wake does not
regret the spending — regret is a gap in a ledger. State the
rising's answer."

"The rising's answer is me. The price stands as named in the
casemate: I walk into the wire at the head of the clearing work,
and I stay at it until the lamps burn, once the tower falls. Open
the gate and I will sign it in whatever ink the houses keep. But
the army climbs *now*. Every day this chain stands, the freed
floors learn that the rising's word is worth what a faction's
patience allows. You know what that lesson costs. You taught it to
me."

"The instrument's price is accepted as to the instrument," the
eldest said, and one of the others entered it, pen moving, and
Rhovan watched twenty years of his life become a line in a book
with no name in it. "It is insufficient as to the host. The woods
waited eighty years while every people's realm was freed around
them, because ours was merely on the way. The host gentles the
woods before it climbs, or it climbs hungry. The gate stays shut."

"You'd starve the rising you want to win."

"We would collect from it," said the eldest of the Wake, without
heat, without hurry, fourscore years of patience folding its hands
on a table. "Everything is bought. You sat at the feet of the one
who taught us all to say so."

And a voice Rhovan knew better than his own name — the old one or
the new — said, from down the lane:

"She also taught you to keep books. Stand aside and I will show you
how it is done."

Calyne came up the queue on foot, dust to the knee, two days down
from the muster and unannounced, as she arrived everywhere. She did
not look at Rhovan. She walked to the trestle table as she had once
walked to a council fire — as if she had cut its timber herself and
found the workmanship wanting — and laid three papers on it, beside
the Leaf-Ledger, squaring their edges with two fingers until they
lay exact.

"Mistress," the eldest said, and there was something in the word
Rhovan had never heard an elder give her: the wariness of a
creditor greeting a better creditor. "The council did not summon
its eyes."

"It never does, when it is doing something it would rather not
see. Attend. Paper the first." She turned it to face them. "The
contract of hire for the bows on this arch, drawn on the founding
purse. The founding purse spends for two objects only — the keeping
of the lamps and the keeping of the watch — by a clause in the
founders' own hand, which I have copied out fair, the council's
memory wanting refreshing. A chain across the bread of nineteen
freed peoples keeps no lamp and wakes no watch. The hire is void
from its date; the silver is recalled by sundown; I have already
served the captain his notice, and he is aware that bows follow
pay. By dark you hold this arch with three elders and a book.

"Paper the second. The council has spent this season entering
grievance — the woods crossed and not gentled, the wire slack and
not drawn, the name of the Leaf-Ledger spent at a spider's court by
an instrument the Wake no longer owns. All true. All entered. And
in the same season it has left the founding ledger in arrears, so I
have brought the accounts current myself. Enter: the Grey Rides,
freed. The kennel courts, freed. The Snare Woods, the Quarry Runs,
the pale court and every green fragment of the sixty-sixth floor,
freed. Enter: ten thousand snares slacked in one breath by a
calloff no elf alive could sound. Enter: the Gibbet Glade's dead,
cut down and laid in green ground. Enter last, and rule it twice:
the only army in the age of the tower that has ever moved the
wood's freedom from the credit column to the till — fed, this
season, by the floors it freed, and starved, this week, by us. Read
your own book, elders. The Wake is not the rising's creditor. It
has been living off the rising's payments for two years and calling
the income grievance."

Silence went down the table. Below the arch a mile of queue made
the sound a country makes waiting.

"The debt of the woods is real," the eldest said at last, evenly, a
creditor conceding nothing yet. "Slack is not gone. Dark is dark.
Who clears the wire, mistress? Who relights the lamps?"

"They are owed this," Calyne said, and laid down the third paper,
and Rhovan, reading it upside down in the old reflex, felt the
parley's ground move. It was a grant in the charter's form, sealed
at the muster: wardship of the lamp-wood floors — named one by one,
the Grey Rides to the pale court — vested in the Wake under the
charter of the Frosthall, with the labor of the freed floors
pledged to the clearing, and the timber-right and the lamp-right
and the whole restoration standing in the Wake's care until the
wood was whole.

"Signed before I came down. I asked her for it the night before I
followed your letter's answer here. It took the general of the
rising, at her own muster, on the eve of her own siege, as long to
grant the Wake's whole purpose as it takes to read it. You have
spent the founding purse, the Wake's name before nineteen peoples,
and four days of harvest, to buy what was already yours for the
sound of your own voices in the open — and the entry cannot be
unmade, because the whole queue below this arch is the receipt."

The eldest of the Wake sat still a long time. Then she rose and
unhooked the chain's near shackle with her own hands — it was done
that fast, in the end, after eighty years; iron is always the
smallest part of a blockade — and said, to the lane, in the formal
voice, the one for entries: "The gate is open. The Wake takes up
the wardship of the woods." And to Calyne, quietly, in the other
voice, the one for prices: "The council will require your seat,
mistress. You have audited it in public. There is no reading of our
law under which you keep it."

"There is no reading under which I want it," Calyne said. "My
resignation has stood entered in the council's own minute-book
since before I came down — third gather, left margin, in my hand;
you will find I dated it the day your letter went up." She gathered
her papers and was done with the table. "I was the Wake's eyes for
the whole of my life. The eyes are the one part that cannot help
seeing which way the ledger actually runs. Keep the woods well,
elders. It was always the whole of what any of us wanted."

The queue moved at dusk.

It moved the way a river moves when the ice goes — a shudder, a
groan of axles taking weight, and then the whole vale of the runs
turning slowly from a warehouse back into a road, wagon by wagon up
through the hunting-arch into the lift-light, drovers' horns
answering down the lanes for miles, Berrin's loaves going hand to
hand to the ones stuck standing farthest. It would take four days
to clear. It would clear. By the arch the Wake was folding its grey canvas,
hoods down at last in front of strangers, its elders already deep
in run-lore with the quarry-warden who had been ashamed of them —
the best-kept ledger in the world bent, at last, over the only work
it had ever wanted, and not one of them climbing another step. The
rising's rear was now held by a faction that had blockaded it a
week gone and would die for its supply lines by spring, provided
the entries were fair.

He found Calyne at the foot of the lift-queue, going up with the
grain like any wagoner, and stood beside her awhile without
speaking, which he had learned from a better general than either of
them.

"You spent your seat," he said at last. "A lifetime's seat, for a
chain that would have come down in a month regardless, once the
purse ran dry."

"A month," Calyne agreed. "On the twelfth day of which your army
stands at half-bread under the first rampart, and the Made prices
the difference. It was arithmetic, Rhovan, not sentiment. Do not
make me regret telling you the sum came out."

"And what do I call you now, with the seat gone? Mistress of
nothing. Eyes of no council."

"Calyne," she said. "It was always the only part of it that was
mine." Then, as the queue took her forward, she put out one hand,
palm down, the old signal — *receive* — and into his was pressed a
small weight wrapped in grey paper: two halves of a broken disc of
heart-elm, grain showing pale where it had never seen light,
gathered off a casemate table on the Wind Walls by a handler who
had not explained herself then either. "The Wake does not keep what
it cannot pour," she said, not turning around. "Carry your own
livery, or bury it. It has been paid for twice now — once by you.
The second entry is closed."

He carried it. Up the lift-queue in the dark, up the gate roads
under the wagon-lamps strung like a lit wick through five floors of
freed country, the two pale halves of a name riding in his breast
where the whole one used to, lighter than the whole had ever been.
Behind him the artery ran full again, a country's blood moving; and
Rhovan of the rising climbed home toward the siege counting, floor
by floor, the things his people had fought each other over in two
years of climbing — gates, tolls, ledgers, woods, seats, names —
and the one thing they had never fought over once.

The direction. Nobody had ever fought about the direction.

He was at the muster by middle-night and gave Ede the ledger of it
standing, all of it, no fractions; and the last wagons of the
relieved queue were still coming up through the gates behind the
army three mornings later, when the rising touched the first
rampart of the Made, and the war for the tower's own country began.
