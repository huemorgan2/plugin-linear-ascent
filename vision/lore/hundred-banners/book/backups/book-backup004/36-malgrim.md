# Chapter 42 — Malgrim

The muster-drums of the ninetieth floor had beaten since the floor was
poured, the captives said, and no one living had ever heard them stop.
They stopped
an hour before dawn, when the last drummer imp was freed of its collar and
set down its sticks and sat looking at its own hands, and the silence that
followed was so large that half the host woke where it slept among the
wreckage, certain some new weapon had been loosed against them.

Ede walked the forming-yards in that silence. The iron squares were sown
with broken collars, thousands of them, burst in the wave of the great
Break-collar and lying open in the muster-moss like frost-litter after a
thaw. Here and there a freed man sat among them, turning one over and
over, not able yet to put it down. The banners of the King had been pulled
off the gatehouse in the night and lay heaped for burning. High on the
wrecked dais the drum-throne stood with its skins split. Zephyra's gales,
come too late to matter, had tidied the rout and gone, unthanked and
knowing it.

*Forty-one climbers,* Sliver said against her palm, dry as a shelf of old
paper. *I have stood in a good many mornings-after. This is the first one
that is quiet.*

"It isn't over," Ede said.

*No. He is waiting for you under the throne. He has been waiting since
the drums stopped. He sent a clerk with his compliments.*

She went up through the yards past the rows of the hurt, past the vault
of the conscript-rolls where Dara Lastmuster stood at a lectern striking
names — one stroke each, thousands of strokes, a work the muster-captain
would give to no one else. Nix stopped at the dais stair without being
told, and Rhovan took the other side of it, an arrow loose on the string.

Malgrim sat in the ruin of his throne like a man in his own parlor.

⁂

He was built like a man who had carried sacks and then carried orders.
The weld-iron took him at the collarbones and ran up the neck in old
silver seams, and above it his face was ordinary — lined, kind about the
eyes, a provisioner's face. One leg was crushed under a fallen drum and he
had not troubled anyone to move it. Through his gauntleted fingers, link
by link, ran his chain.

"Ede Stonhal," he said. "Sit, if you like. The step is clean. I had a
clerk sweep it."

She stayed standing. "Malgrim."

"Just so. Not *Herald* — you've broken my gate, the title's in the wreck
somewhere." He said it without bitterness. "I watched you come up the
yards. You walk
like a woman going to a burying. That's a compliment, general. Most
conquerors walk like they're going to a wedding, and those are the ones
who burn the larders."

"I've come to offer you what we offer everyone."

"I know what you've come to offer." He lifted the chain a little, let it
pour from one hand into the other. "The word that breaks steel and the
word that unsays oaths. A name on the rolls of the freed. Work, which is
the part your people underprice when they make the speech — the work is
the whole of it, I could have told them that. A place at a fire." He
smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the terrible thing. "It is the
best offer made in this tower in an age. I've read the reports of it
going up floor by floor, and I never once found a lie in it. You mean
every word."

"Then take it."

"No," said Malgrim, gently, as if declining a second helping.

Ede sat down on the swept step after all.

"You were one of us," she said. "You were taken in the Theft or born to
the taken. Somebody freed you, once, on some floor far down."

"The forty-third," he said. "A climber with a grey beard and a splinter
of crystal in his ear, and I never learned his name. He broke my collar
in the morning and climbed on before noon, because the climb was always
the thing, and I stood in the gate-town of a freed floor with my whole
life in my two hands and nowhere to set it down." The chain moved through
his fingers. Link, link, link. "You want to know what freedom was,
general, on the forty-third floor, thirty years gone? It was a door
standing open onto a cold room. The keeper fed us a week. The strong went
down. The clever went up. I was neither, and I had been a tallyman under
the collar, and the freed did not want a tallyman — they wanted very much
to burn the tallies, and I understood them, and I starved understanding
them." He looked up at her without any anger at all. "The tower took me
back in the spring. And here is what I have told no soul before you: when
the collar shut the second time, I wept with relief."

"That's not a choosing," Ede said. "That's a trap closing on a leg."

"Is it. Who chose you?" He asked it kindly. "I know your story — I
bought most of it, page by page, that's my trade. Thirty names on a
stone, two years dry, and then a shardmind chose you *again*, in front
of the whole gate-queue, and the shards don't re-choose." He leaned
forward as far as the fallen drum allowed, his voice never rising, never
hurrying. "Something reached out of all the world and picked you, Ede
Stonhal, you and no other, and everything you have done since — every
floor, every banner, this whole gathered morning — grew out of being
chosen. Well. The tower reached out of all the world and picked *me*.
Out of every starved tallyman in the Made it put its hand on my shoulder
and said: you, I will keep. Every other hand that ever held mine let
go." He sat back. The chain lay still. "Yours freed you. Mine fed me.
You will forgive a poor man for not seeing the difference from inside."

"The difference," Ede said, "is standing in your yards learning to walk
out of step. Sliver chose me and I am free to shame it, fail it, set it
down. Set the chain down, Malgrim. That's the whole test. What loves you
lets you set it down."

For three breaths she thought she had him. Something crossed the kind
ordinary face like wind over a pond, some tallyman thirty years starved
leaning toward a fire. Rhovan said afterward that his arrow-hand had
eased. Then Malgrim shook his head slowly, the way a man refuses a
kindness too large to owe.

"There's my girl the general," he said. "No — hear my offer instead,
for you haven't heard it and you should. I am the best herald in this
tower. I know every roll, every road, every provisioner and every price
from here to the throne, and the throne is going to fall — I have kept
the ledgers, madam, you are going to win, and a rising needs a herald
more than it knows. Take me up with you. I will serve you better than
any free man on your rolls, for the reason you'll refuse me: a free man
wants, and wanting wavers. I have not wanted anything in thirty years.
I am the most reliable instrument you will ever be offered." He opened
his hands, chain across both palms, an honest tradesman showing honest
goods. "All I ask is that you leave me the collar. Not the King — the
King is spent, I've kept his books, I know it to the copper. Just the
collar. Just the being-kept. You may hold the other end yourself. You'd
hold it lightly. I know you now; you would hold it so lightly."

And it was reasonable. That was the horror of him — it was kind, and it
was reasonable, and every word was wrong the way a well-kept ledger of
stolen grain is wrong, wrong in the sum and true in every line. Ede
looked at his open hands and saw the whole tower in little: the thing
that makes the cage so warm and the door so cold that a man will hand
you the key and beg you to lose it.

"No," she said, and her voice broke on it, and she let it break. "We
don't keep anyone. Not even you. Not even asked."

"Then we are at the bottom of the page," Malgrim said, without rancor —
with pride, even, as at an examination fairly passed. "Do the last
mercy, general. Say your word over the steel. I should like to hear how
it sounds."

She said the Break-word. The collar at his throat parted with the sound
of ice leaving an eave, and fell into two clean halves in his lap, and
Malgrim, Herald of the King, picked the halves up in his gauntlets and
held them the way another man holds a dead bird — and nothing in his
eyes changed at all.

"There," he said softly. "Now you have it whole, what I am. You cannot
strike a roll a man keeps in his own hand." He set the broken chain
aside with great care and took up his mace from beneath the fallen
drum, and got up onto his one good leg, tall against the morning. "I'd
not have you carry a murder, girl. You've enough on the stone already.
Let it be a battle. One more muster."

"Malgrim —"

"Drums up," he said, kindly, and swung.

It was short. He was crippled and spent and thirty years out of
practice at wanting to win. She took the mace on her shield and it
drove her to one knee, and Rhovan's arrow stood in his shoulder-seam a
heartbeat later, and Ede came up inside the second swing the way Hale
had taught her when she was young and hit him once, true, where the
weld met the man.

She caught him going down. She was not strong enough and it did not
matter; she went down with him, and knelt in the muster-moss with his
head against her arm while the kind ordinary face did the last ordinary
thing a face does.

"Item," Malgrim said. His voice was a ledger closing. "One general.
Honest. Note to the King —" and whatever he had noted, he carried it up
himself.

She freed the broken chain from where it had tangled and would have
cast it away, and then did not, and folded his gauntlet around it
instead, because it was his, because it was all that had ever been his.
Then she sat with him while the yards went quiet around her, and
grieved for the man from the forty-third floor as if he had died thirty
years ago, which he had.

To Dara Lastmuster, afterward, she said: "Enter him. Not as Herald. A
man of the forty-third, a tallyman, taken twice. Write that the second
taking was the crime, and write it plain, so that whoever keeps rolls
after us knows what the tower could do."

By the time the entry was written, the light over the yards had begun
to change: the ninetieth's veil coming off the books with its keeper,
the grey lid tearing far out where the walls stood a day's march off,
and a long seam of honest brightness walking in over the iron toward
the host. The whole
muster paused to watch it come — no cheer, no order, thousands of
faces turned one way. Ninety floors had taught the freed what a
changing sky meant.

They brought Berrin to her at noon.

Two of Malgrim's clerks brought him — freed clerks now, quill-fisted
and blinking in the light, but they had been given the errand before
the battle, and a clerk finishes an errand as a river finds the sea.
Berrin Or came bound at the wrists with muster-cord, walking small
between them, and the year had eaten him: her oldest friend, the man
whose bread had kept her alive through the drunk years, gray as ash and
looking at nobody. The willing collar was gone from under his shirt —
not to the great Break, which had rolled through at noon and left that
one iron unmoved; the freed clerks had struck it off by hand before
they brought him. He had not fought them, the clerks said. He had not
anything.

The elder clerk held out a ledger-page, folded once. The last entry, in
Malgrim's fine hand:

*Item: delivered to the woman who climbs, one quartermaster, used. He
asked me a second time for extraction. I keep my answers: a ledger
entry does not retire. Item, final: the account passes to you, general.
See that it balances better in your keeping than it did in mine.*

The yards went still, the freed and the host together, for everyone
knew the story by now — Calyne dead proving it, the courier who smelled
of Roothollow bread — and the whole climb watched Ede Stonhal stand
before the friend who had sold her thirty and fed her after, and waited
to see what the rising was.

Berrin looked up at her once. "You owe me rope," he said. His voice was
a ruin. "For the bread, if for nothing else. It's cheap, Ede. I'd buy
it myself but they've bound my hands."

She had turned this over every night since the trophy wall of the Pale
Court, and the thinking had taught her one thing: the judgment was not
hers. She had known, and climbed beside him pretending; she had
loved him; she had eaten his bread. A judge needs cleaner hands or a
colder heart, and there was one company in the world with the right.
She was certain of the rope, certain as a drawn sword; and she had
learned — it had taken her a tower — to spend her certainty slowly.

"No," she said. "Not mine to give or refuse. Shieldwall holds your
trial."

⁂

They held it at dusk in the lee of the broken gate, by firelight, and
the freed muster-captives of Lastmuster stood back in a great quiet
ring and let Shieldwall have the ground.

Twenty-two of the thirty were living. Dun Harrow was not, nor seven
others, and the company called the full roll anyway, and for each dead
name the line answered *held* — "Dun Harrow." *Held the north
door.* — until the thirty were all present, the quick and the dead
together, and Marta Reed, who had been senior after Dun, turned to the
prisoner.

There is no need to write out the whole of it. He confessed to all of
it, in the flat voice of a man reading a delivery-bill: the route sold
to Skarn's buyers, the toll-payments, the seating-list of the
Frosthall, the year and the price of each. Asked why, he answered
with the clerk's own creed — word for word as it had been said to him
over a heel of bread when he was seven, at the bottom of the world,
and he told them that too, since they were counting: "The tower feeds
what it counts." He had gone up a grown man and knelt for the collar
with his eyes open; not an excuse, he said, an inventory. Asked what
he sought, he said rope, and said it hoping.

Marta Reed said rope too, when the company spoke. So did five others.
Then it came around the fire to Wat Kello, who had been welded, whose
face was iron from the jaw down, whose first word in five years — spoken
in the Welding Halls with his general's name in his eyes — had been
*Bread.* The speech-slit they had cut him at floor 84 made every word
cost.

"Rope's a wage," Wat said. "Paid once. Done." He worked the iron
jaw. "He was a provisioner. Twenty years he fed floors to the tower."
A long breath, the fire ticking. "Let him feed them back."

The word went around the fire once more, and around it turned. Not
mercy — no one there would have called it mercy, least of all Berrin,
who began, somewhere in the middle of it, silently to weep, in the
manner of a man whose one remaining door is being bricked. What the
thirty built between them, sentence by plain sentence, was this:

*Berrin Or shall live. He shall walk down the freed tower, floor by
floor, to every floor he sold, beginning at the twentieth, and he shall
provision it — granary, seed-corn, salt, and roads — one year's keeping
for every soul his ledger cost it, at the reckoning of that floor's
keeper. When the list is done he shall begin it again, for the list of
what he owes is longer than his life. He shall carry no blade, hold no
office, and keep no books but the open kind. And he shall walk it under
parole.*

"Whose parole?" Marta Reed asked the fire.

Nix stood up. He was the smallest thing in the ring by three heads, and
he had the Shieldwall standard on his shoulder that he had picked up at
floor ten to hide behind, and he did not hide behind it now.

"Mine," Nix said. "I held a cup for a king who was a joke, I can hold a
leash for a baker who was a lie. I know floors. I count things, it's
what I'm for — twenty, twenty-one, the fairground that begged us to
pass by, sixty. Sixty was the feast. I was in the kitchens; I counted
the cooks that got out the south door and the ones that didn't. Eleven
and four, if you want the numbers. I always want the numbers." He
looked at Berrin, and all the fast talk went out of him, and what was
left was the book's oldest small voice. "You'll walk in front. I'll
have the list. Every sack gets counted twice, once for the floor, once
for the dead. You don't get to die until it balances, and I count
slow."

Berrin Or bowed his head to a goblin, and the sentence stood.

⁂

It was Ede's to speak at the end — the thirty had built it, but the
rising's seal was hers. She stood at the fire and understood, in the
saying, that the price of what her company had done was to be paid by
her.

Because she would have hanged him. She knew it as she spoke; she had
known it since the Pale Court. Rope was clean. Rope ended the knowing.
And more than that — worse than that — the thirty had taken the one
thing Ede Stonhal had wanted for herself since the Red Warcamp, and
they had given it to *him:* a life spent to the last hour making
amends, every day a payment, every road a penance, no possibility of
the ledger ever closing and no obligation to be anything but sorry.
She had wanted to die making amends since the day she came down alone,
and instead she would live and be followed, and Berrin — Berrin got
the amends.

The rising's justice was labor, not blood. She sealed it with a steady
voice. It cost her more than Malgrim had.

"It's done," she told Sliver, in the dark after, on the gatehouse
stair. "Ninety floors done. Tell me the rest is simple. Lie if you have
to."

*I am a poor liar,* Sliver said. *It is a fault of crystal.* Its light
moved on her palm. *Above this gate the throne-tiers begin. The first
of them is a stair of black glass, polished to a mirror an age deep,
and the glass is not idle. They say every climber goes up it beside
themselves.*

Ede looked at the small door in the great broken gate, and the dark
going up.

"Then it can meet me," she said. "I've been wanting a word."
