# Chapter 40 — The Vizier's Floor

Five floors lay between the dead welding-rigs and the Herald's Gate, and
the rising took them in nineteen days, and Rhovan of the Grey Rides —
lately of nowhere, his Wake-token half a year broken over his own knee —
spent those nineteen days doing what he had always done best, which was
watching everyone, and being wrong about the one that mattered.

He would think of it afterward in exactly those terms. An archer's
reckoning. Nineteen days of perfect watchfulness, every shaft in the
quiver counted, and the whole time the target stood at his elbow handing
him bread.

The Imp Warrens fell first, and fell strangely: not stormed but *woken.*
Floor eighty-five was the barracks-slum of the Made — iron cell-blocks
stacked in reeking, smoke-hung tiers where the tower's soldiery denned
between shifts — and Wend Sootside's whisper-network had been working it
from within for years before the rising ever reached the stair. The
warrens ran out of sight in every direction, tier over tier under a
soot sky of their own making, flue-fires guttering like low red stars,
and the din of a hundred thousand soldiers at rest never once went
quiet in the two days the army spent inside it. The Warden
himself was the proof of what the smoke-net claimed: Fluegrim, the
warren's enforcer, a hellknight welded huge, whose collar was old and
crazed with a hundred half-doubts. Nix went to him under a parley-rag,
alone but for the Kettle, and talked for half a day — nobody ever learned
what about, and Nix, asked, said "shift-rations, mostly, and being small
in a big war" — and at the end of it Fluegrim reached up and broke his
own collar with his own hands, which no one had ever seen, and his whole
warren woke behind him. A barracks of the King's army came over in a
single evening, tier by tier, like a hillside letting go. The rising
gained a floor, a spell for walking unchallenged in the tower's own
livery, and four thousand soldiers who knew every corridor of the Made
from the inside.

Berrin Or fed them all by midnight. Rhovan stood in the ration-line dark
and watched the old provisioner move down the cell-tiers with his
tally-slate and his lamplit smile, learning the freed imps' names as fast
as they recovered them, doubling the portion of any soldier who looked at
the food as if it might be taken back. Twenty years past, a man of the
Grey Rides had taught Rhovan that you know a faction by what it does when
nobody bids on the outcome. He remembered thinking, in the warrens: there
stands the only one of us with nothing underneath. What Berrin did came
from Berrin. It made a man restful just to watch.

He filed the observation where he filed everything, and the army climbed.

The Bastion Line tried to drill them to death. Floor eighty-six was
discipline made a landscape, ramparts and parade-grounds running out of
sight, ranked ballistae on the firing-steps and the tramp of the endless
drill going through the ground like a pulse; it was the first floor to
kill more of the rising than it lost, and for two days it was the war at
its oldest and worst. The parade-ground had no far side that any scout
found; the drill came out of one horizon and wheeled away into the
other, and the slave-crews of Wallrest were still building the
ramparts longer at both ends, as if the tower meant its army to drill
to the rim of the world. Then Dara Wallrest's stolen counter-signs reached
the Command Bastion in a freed clerk's hand, and the word *Countermand*
went down the drill-lines like frost down a window, and whole columns
wheeled on their own officers' orders into confusion. Bastionheart's
collar broke at the drill's dead center, and the drill stopped — the
first silence on that floor in an age — and an army that had never once
been permitted to halt stood at rest, and then, rank by waking rank,
formed up again facing the other way.

The Siege Gardens they blighted in an afternoon and marched for three
days after, Vega's crews and the garden-thralls souring the iron-seed
in its beds so that a season's crop of rams and towers rotted unripe in
the rows; the Warden flailed and fell apart into dead scrap, and Nael
Ramside wept at the seed-vault door, and the rising marched out with
engine-iron and iron-seed enough to grow siege-works of its own. Three
days, because the gardens were a country: terraced beds stepping away
to the horizon under trellised girder-vines, oozing black oil for sap,
half-grown rams creaking in their rows like an orchard in wind — the
Made's mockery of every green floor it had been built by unmaking. The
Iron Chantries were harder, because the
enemy there was belief — the King's black war-chapels, censer-smoke and
iron liturgy working to make the bound *mean* the words — and Rhovan, who
had spent a life being loyal in fractions, hated the floor with a
completeness that surprised him. There was no light on all its miles
but censer-glow, and no weather but the incense — a smoke that hung to
the horizon and worked at the mind the way damp works at iron. They
rang the Iron Belfry against the liturgy and the whole compelled
congregation cracked awake; the counter-
creed came out of the Reliquary and became a word, *Recant,* the art of
breaking a faith that had been welded on; the freed furnace-priests
stripped their censers and asked, as one body, to march with the van.
Vega took the chapels' brass — censer-brass, and later the great
sounding-brass of floor eighty-nine's silenced proclamation-horns — and
began, in the night-camps, to found and hammer at something the size of
a cottage that she would not yet name.

And on the Herald's Road, under the last colossal horns, with the King's
titles choked to silence and Trumpetsteel's freed voice blaring the
King's own decrees backward into the muster ahead, Rhovan stood at the
road's head and got his first sight of the Herald's Gate: a fortress-
square of mustering-yards under a gatehouse like a cliff, the drums
beyond it beating men into soldiers, beating the tempo of the whole Made,
never stopping. Five floors in nineteen days. Behind the rising, the
warrens fed it, the bastion drilled it, the gardens grew its engines, the
chantries sang for it — and behind it, tier under tier, five grey lids
had torn and come down in the wake of their broken wardens, so that the
Made, which had never once owned weather, was getting its first lesson
in a changing sky. The tower's last approach had been made the
rising's own arsenal.

That night Mistress Calyne found him at the picket-line and asked him,
in the flat grey voice that twenty years of hating her had taught him to
obey anyway, to call the full council for the morning. Every banner,
every keeper, every captain of the freed. Staged on the road's head, she
said, in open sight of the gate.

"Whatever it is," Rhovan said, "you might tell me first. Once. For the
novelty."

Calyne looked at him a long moment, the crows of the Grey Rides in her
grey look, his handler once, the coldest constant in his sky. "I have
been wrong in my method my whole life," she said. "I worked hidden, and
so the truth I found could always be hidden from. This one gets no
shadow to die in. It gets noon, and witnesses, and a wall at its back.
Call the council, Rhovan."

Which was, he would understand within a day, the most she ever told
anyone, and more than she had ever told him.

They staged the council on the Herald's Road's last rise, beneath the
silenced Proclamation Arch, with the muster-gate filling the whole north
sky. A hundred banners on the poles the King's standards had hung
from — the last thirty sewn since the outworks by the Made's own freed.
The charter-table down the middle, the decade of it scarred and ringed;
Ede at its head; the keepers and the captains and the freed Wardens in a
horseshoe five ranks deep. Drums beyond the gate, always, like weather.

Rhovan stood at Ede's shoulder with his bow unstrung, as protocol asked,
and watched Calyne rise with a folio under her arm, and watched — because
watching was his craft — the faces. So he alone, perhaps, saw the exact
instant at which Berrin Or, laughing at something a Frosthall thane had
said, counted the council's exits.

It meant nothing to him. He filed it. The target handed him bread that
very morning.

"A year ago at the Frosthall," Calyne began, without preamble,
without warmth, without anything a listener could love, as ever, "the
feast was struck by Heralds seeded through the serving-lines. I said that
morning there was a collar inside the charter's circle. I was not
believed, being myself suspected. Here is the serving-list Nix took from
a dead Herald's boot." She laid it on the charter-table. "The seating it
follows was settled in council, five days before the feast, by six
people. I am one; the rest you trust with your lives, and may keep
trusting four of them. Attend, please, to the sixth."

The council had gone very quiet. Rhovan had not moved, and could not
afterward say when his heart had started to slam.

"Item," Calyne said, in a voice from which she had removed everything,
and Rhovan, who knew her ciphers better than any soul alive, understood
that she was quoting the enemy's own ledger-form back at him deliberately,
a huntsman's courtesy, a spymaster's contempt. "The toll-ledger Rhovan
took at floor eleven shows a payment moved *up* the tower the same week
Shieldwall's route was sold, drawn against Roothollow accounts. Item: the
granary of Roothollow, burned that spring, was rebuilt that same summer,
by unrecorded coin. I have the mason's dates. Item: at the Pale Court
hangs a Shieldwall shield sold upward through the toll-roads out of
Roothollow that identical week. Item —" and here she paused, the only
pause she permitted herself, and into it the drums beat twice — "three
days ago, in the Welding Halls, a soldier of Shieldwall's thirty, sold
five years since, was unsealed. His witness is one word wide. He has
carried it since the cages. He smelled the courier who came to count the
merchandise."

Ede said, quietly, "Say your word, Wat."

He came forward through the horseshoe on iron legs, the banner-fold still
in his fist, and the iron mouth worked, and the word fell into the
council like a stone down a well.

"Bread."

Rhovan did not understand for one whole breath. He was watching Berrin
with everyone else and his mind was still running the numbers of every
suspect he had ever kept books on — Calyne foremost, always Calyne — and
the column would not sum, and then it summed.

The bread at the ration-lines. The bread at the cell-tiers, at midnight,
learning the freed imps' names. *Roothollow bread,* the freed prisoner
had said at floor twenty, a hooded man who smelled of it. The granary.
The tolls. The seating. The supply-trains that arrived, every single
time, one march behind the enemy's best-laid ambush — never before. The
arrow Berrin took at the feast, in the shoulder, in the fleshy part,
saving Ede in front of two hundred witnesses. Talking Ede out of hiding
at the war's very first hour, the oldest friend, launching the hunt —
launching the hunt *for himself,* steering it, feeding it, twenty years
of warm scenes and Rhovan had eaten from his hand that morning, and
every one of them turned over at once like soil under a plow, and what
was underneath had been underneath the whole time.

Berrin Or stood up slowly at the charter-table, flour on his cuff, the
face the entire rising trusted above any face alive, and said, mildly,
"Wat. Lad. You've been iron five years. Memory's a cruel smith, and
this cruel woman —"

"Open your shirt," said Calyne.

The council did not breathe. Berrin looked around the horseshoe once,
at all of them, keepers and captains and thanes, and Rhovan watched him
find no exit in any face, and watched the warmth leave his eyes the way
heat leaves a forge — not blown out; *banked,* hidden down under ash,
where it had lived all along. That was the worst of it, Rhovan thought
ever after. He had expected a stranger to surface. What surfaced was
Berrin exactly, the same kind eyes, sums running behind them, and the
sums had simply always been these sums.

"You were the best of us, Cal," Berrin said, and his voice was the
gentle, floury, evening voice that had fed a hundred floors, wholly
unchanged, and that gentleness in that moment was the most obscene thing
Rhovan had heard in a long life. "You always knew. Nobody would ever
believe you. That's why I never had to have you killed."

He was old, and a provisioner, and cornered, and he was faster than all
of it — faster than his years, faster than the two Frosthall thanes at
his shoulders, faster by far than any reader of any warm scene would
ever have dreamt. The knife came out of his sleeve where a tally-stylus
should have been — a thin boning-knife, a kitchen thing, honed away to a
whisper — and it went between Calyne's ribs on the upstroke before her
folio hit the table.

Even then, dying, she was working. Rhovan saw it and would see it the
rest of his days: she did not clutch the wound, she clutched the *man* —
both hands on his collar-band, hauling his shirt open to the whole
council as her knees went — so that every banner of the rising saw with
its own eyes, in noon light, the dull iron ring seated against the old
man's breastbone. Worn smooth. Grown into the flesh at its rim. A
Herald's collar, willing, chosen, twenty years polished by the skin of
the rising's beloved.

Proof laid, she let go, and fell against the charter-table, and was
dead before Ede caught her.

Berrin ran.

He ran north, toward the gate, into the open killing-ground before it,
and the council was a heartbeat behind him in shock and would be a
hundred years behind him in understanding, and only two things in all
that noon moved fast enough to matter. One was Rhovan's hands, which had
his bow strung and drawn while his mind still knelt by Calyne — a Grey
Rides shot at a fleeing back at eighty paces, loosed clean, and it flew
true and did not arrive: a gate-tender hulk, iron doors of shoulders,
stepped out of the wall's shadow and took the shaft in its plate the way
a roof takes hail. The gate had been watching its investment. The gate
had *always* been watching.

The other was the muster-gate itself, which opened.

Not wide. A hand's width, a door held by a doorman for a man expected to
dinner, and Berrin Or went through it without breaking stride, out of
the rising, up into the Made, home to the owner of his collar. The last
Rhovan ever saw of him on the free side of the wall was flour on a cuff
and the glint of the iron at his throat, and then the gate swallowed
him, and the drums beyond it did not so much as change their beat, and
that was the most eloquent thing the tower had ever said. It had priced
this day in long ago. A ledger entry does not retire.

They buried her at the road's head that evening, under the arch her trap
had needed and gotten, and the whole rising passed the grave in file, and
the freed of eight floors who had cursed her name in cipher for two years
laid iron and brass on the mound of the woman who had been hunting their
enemy since before most of them were taken. The hateful ally, loyal all
along. Rhovan spoke the Wake's parting over her because there was no one
left of her faction to do it and because he had been wrong about her the
longest, which he said aloud, at the grave, in front of the army, in
whole sentences, the first unfractioned truth he had told in twenty
years.

Ede stood after the file had passed, alone with him, Calyne's cipher-book
in her hands. There was blood dried on its margin. She did not wipe it
off, then or ever.

"You knew," Rhovan said. It was not an accusation. He had spent the day
recasting every warm scene of the age of the world; he had one recasting
left in him. "Since the Pale Court. You knew and you climbed beside him."

"I knew and I climbed beside him," Ede said, "because knowing isn't
proving, and proving him quietly would have broken the rising in half —
his half. She taught me that." The drums beat on beyond the wall. She
looked up at the gate, one hand flat on the dead woman's book, and
Rhovan watched the grief bank itself down under ash to where her sums
were kept, and understood at last, coldly and completely, how alike the
general and the traitor had always been, and why he had loved them both.

"He's behind that gate," she said. "With his owner, and his owner's
army, and every drum in the Made. And in the morning, so are we."

The hundred banners snapped above the grave in a wind come down from
nowhere, the first breath of a distant unseasonable storm, and beyond
the wall the muster-drums beat men into soldiers, on and on, as they had
beaten for an age, and had, though no drummer knew it, one morning left
to beat.
