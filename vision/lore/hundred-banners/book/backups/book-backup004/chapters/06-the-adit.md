# Chapter 6 — The Adit

While she waited for the captain, Vega Coalbrow mended the bearing.

It was the great flywheel's outboard bearing, and it had been running
dry and half a hair out of true for longer than the machinists of
Sprocket Row wanted to say in front of a stranger, because Grib
Sprocket had been keeping the wheel balanced by ear for thirty years
and his ear had grown old along with the wheel. Vega did not say any of
that either. She asked for the loan of a jack and a ladle of grease,
and she set her shoulder under a housing that three men could not have
shifted, and she took up the slack the way you take up a grief — slowly,
evenly, all the way to the end of the thread, so that nothing is left
loose to work itself worse. It cost her the evening. She had the
evening. The wheel came true, and the hall's note came true with it,
and old Grib stood listening to it with his eyes shut and did not
trust himself to speak, and that was all the payment there was, and it
was plenty.

She was oiling the ladle when the captain came — a woman of Men, worn
sharp, with an elf at her shoulder and a goblin at her heel carrying a
grey banner bigger than himself. Vega stood. She was used to what
happened when she stood, and waited through it, patient as a doorway.

"You've been asking for me by name," said Ede Stonhal.

"I have," said Vega. "My name is Vega Coalbrow. I am a forge-matron of
the mountain folk, out of Roothollow these twenty years, and before I
say anything else to you I am going to show you papers that I have
carried up sixteen floors, because if I talk first I will talk myself
kind, and the papers do not have that failing."

She unwrapped the oil-cloth. She unlocked the iron bands. She had made
the bands herself, and the lock; she had wanted the carrying of it to
be hard.

The manifests were the tower's own paper, salvaged out of the Counting
Halls' unpicking and sent down the column to Roothollow with a hundred
other recovered accounts, for the freed to search for their dead in.
Cage-work, the sheets said. Collar-stock, the sheets said. Lock-plates,
hasps, chain — so many hundredweight, received in good order at the
twentieth landing, for the use of the garrison there. And at the foot
of every sheet, stamped into the tally-wax the way a smith stamps her
warrant into steel that she is proud of, a maker's mark: a brow, and
under it three sparks.

"That is my mark," Vega said. "I will say it plainly, and once, and
you may do as you like with me after. The steel of the cages on the
floor above us came off my anvils. I forged it for a factor who paid
in good coin and changed his name every season, and he told me it was
drove-chain and beast-collars and gate-locks for the low farms, and I
had lean years and two apprentices to feed, and so I chose — you must
hear the word, captain, I *chose* — not to wonder why a drover would
want a lock I could not pick myself." She laid her hand flat over the
mark, and it covered it entire, and that did not help. "Light no fire I
cannot quench; keep no fire past its work; and answer for — " she said,
the oath her mother took of prentices at the anvil, and for once in her
life did not finish it. "I make things that hold. It is the one gift I
have. Somebody borrowed it, and I let them, and your people are behind
it."

The camp had gone quiet around them, hall-quiet, so the flywheel's
true note ran under everything like water under ice.

"Why bring it to me?" said Ede. "Roothollow has magistrates."

"Because a magistrate would fine me," said Vega, "and I can pay a
fine, and that is why it would be worthless." She knelt, which brought
her eyes only a little above the captain's own, and she said the thing
she had climbed sixteen floors to say, in order, to the end. "Every
lock has two doors in it. The one it was forged for, and the one the
smith leaves in it, the flaw only she knows, because no honest smith
in the mountains will make a thing she cannot unmake. I know my
locks, captain. I know their tempers, and their prides, and where each
one keeps its fear. Take me up to the warcamp, and put me at the
cages, and I will open every door I ever shut on your people. And
after that, if there is an after, I will spend what's left of me
arming the freed, and mending what this tower has broken, until the
account is paid or I am."

The elf said nothing, in the way of a man filing everything. The
goblin counted the manifests aloud, softly — nine sheets, nine — as if
putting a number on a thing could tame it. And the captain looked at
the giant kneeling in the grease-light with her mark under her hand,
and Vega watched her decide, and saw — she told no one this, for
years — that it was not mercy and was not arithmetic. It was
recognition. One woman who had led her people into cages, looking at
one woman who had forged them.

"Get up," said Ede. "We march on the vaults at lamp-trim. You'll want
a hammer."

"I brought four," said Vega.

The approach country took them nine days, and Vega mended her way up
it, because her hands shook if she left them empty.

On the Glass Flats the mirages walked, and the heat lied in sheets,
and the gleaners of Shardside taught the column the blindfold trick —
trust the feet, the glass rings true or it rings false — which suited
Vega, who had trusted her feet over her eyes since girlhood, the
mountain way. The flats tried her once anyway: showed her, off on the
shimmer, a forge with its fire lit and two apprentices at the bellows,
waving her over, no older than the day she'd sold her first lock. She
stood a long breath looking at it. Then she said, aloud, "You have
built it wrong. My forge has the flue on the north wall," and walked
on, and the heat-ghost gave her up for a bad bargain.

At the Millrace she re-hung a wheel that had run crooked since the
Theft, and the millers ground true flour under it the same evening,
and she watched Nix eat griddle-bread so hot it burned him, counting
his bites, and found she was smiling, which she had not expected to do
again on this climb. It was there the sappers took on the millers'
blasting-powder — weir-clearing charge, kept bone-dry in the Drowned
Mill's loft through everything — and Vega packed the casks herself,
each one wedged and wrapped and cradled the way she'd carry eggs,
because powder is only a servant while somebody respects it.

Then the vaults.

The Fusion Vaults were her people's work and her people's shame now: a
country of racked aether-rods glowing their sick perpetual dusk, where
shadows hummed and had edges, and the vault-crews of Half-Light came
out lead-aproned and grey to meet the host, counting their exposures
even as they cheered. The Warden they called Coreburn held the lead
blast-door at the floor's head — a forge-remnant grown vast, half-
melted iron walking, burning from the inside around a cracked rod. The
van broke it the hard way, in relays, no shame in any of it; Vega's
part was after, and it was the part she was made for: drawing the
cracked rod from the cooling wreck with tongs she re-jawed on the
spot, sleeving it in lead, making the terrible thing merely dangerous.
A cure is quick. Mending takes longer, and holds.

The blast-door swung on the last floor before the warcamp, and the
adit lay beyond — the old approach-gallery, giants' work, arrow-
straight into the dark with the drums of the twentieth floor coming
down it, faint and endless, like a heart heard through a wall.

And at the mouth of the adit, the dig-crew put down its tools.

They were kobolds, twelve of them, a rod-handling crew spent down to
the wick — glow-sick, every one, luminous at the knuckles and the
throat where the sickness pools, set by the garrison above to cut a
counter-gallery so the warcamp might drop the stair behind them if the
climb came. The Yoke had gone quiet on the floor when Coreburn fell,
and they had felt their orders go quiet with it, and they had talked
among themselves in the dark, and then they had done the bravest thing
Vega ever saw done on the Ascent. They came out to the lamp-line with
their picks laid down and their hands open and their collars shown,
kneeling, all twelve in a row on the stone — trusting, on no evidence
the tower had ever given them, that the people coming up the gallery
were better than the people who had sent them down it.

Ede was forward at the shaft-head with the sappers. What came up the
adit first was the rear-sweep, and the rear-sweep was Cullers.

Vega was thirty paces back, helping a vault-crew rack the last loose
rods, when the sound began, and her hands knew it before she did,
because it was a sound out of her own trade: steel going into
something soft, in rhythm, workmanlike. No battle-noise. No cries but
short ones. By the time she had turned and covered the ground —
giants cover ground, when they choose — it was nearly finished.
Eleven kobolds lay where they had knelt. They had not run. Kneeling
was the one card they had thought to play, and they had gone on
playing it while it was trumped, one, and the next, and the next, in
good order, down the row.

Serjeant Hale stood over the twelfth with his blade drawn back, and
between his blade and the kobold stood the goblin.

Nix had no weapon. He had the banner, the grey one, dry-stone wall
and crooked star, and he had planted it in front of a glow-sick
kobold child of a
conscript race that goblins had no love for, and he was holding the
pole with both hands, and he was shaking so hard the cloth above him
shivered as if in wind. His voice, when it came, came fast and
counting, because that was the only way it knew.

"Eleven," he said. "You've got eleven. Eleven of twelve, that's your
count, that's plenty, you don't need the twelfth, you never needed
the *first* — " and then, done counting, with everything in him:
"They put their picks *down*."

"A collar is a uniform," said Hale. He was not angry. Vega understood,
later, that this was the whole horror of the man: he was never angry.
"Doctrine's plain, goblin. What's collared serves the tower, and what
serves the tower dies, and whether it kneels first is between it and
whatever gods conscripts keep. Stand off my line."

"Then the doctrine's counted me," said Nix, not moving. "Say it's
counted me. I wore one nine years, it's on the floor of the
Kingsfield if you want to go and cull it — "

Hale's blade did not waver, and the ash-grey line behind him did not
waver, and Vega — arriving, enormous, too late for eleven and perhaps
in time for one — saw the next heartbeat the way a smith sees a crack
running in cooling steel: certain, and only not yet.

Ede came through the Cullers' line from behind, from the shaft-head,
at a dead run that ended in a stillness, and put herself between the
blade and the banner without a word. Then she drew. She did it slowly,
the way you do a thing you can never take back, and she laid her point
low, at guard, at her own teacher.

"Serjeant Hale," she said. "Put it up."

"You're interposing for the enemy, captain."

"I'm interposing for my standard-bearer," said Ede, "who is stood in
front of a surrendered prisoner, on a freed floor, over the bodies of
eleven more. Look down, Hale. Look at what your doctrine holds when
it's held all the way to the floor."

And he looked. Vega gave him that much, always, in every telling: he
looked. The lamp-light stood in the blood between the cobbles, and
the twelfth kobold breathed in whistles behind the banner-cloth, and
something crossed the old serjeant's face that had no doctrine in it —
crossed it, and found nothing to hold onto, and went out.

"I taught you what a line is for," he said.

"You taught me to hold one," said Ede. "Hold this: you and yours are
out of my column. Sweep behind us no more. Guard the gates you've
taken, kill what comes at you armed, and when this war has a law, it
will come looking for the ledger of today. Until then keep west of my
fires, serjeant, because I am the only thing on this floor arguing
against the goblin's arithmetic, and I am losing."

For three long breaths the two lines stood, ash-grey and mudded
motley, and the oldest quarrel in the world stood with them: whether a
thing that surrenders is a person or a problem. Then Hale put up his
blade, and turned his company with a hand-sign, and the Cullers went
back down the gallery in perfect order, taking their dead-count of
nobody and leaving their work on the ground. He did not look back.
Vega had mended enough broken things to know one walking away from
her when she saw it.

They buried the eleven at the adit's mouth, in the giants' fashion,
under raised stone, because Vega asked for it and dug first, and no
one in that column was going to tell a grieving giant with four
hammers that conscripts go in a pit unmarked. Pressed dead. Deaths
that should not have been needed — the shardminds' own word for it,
and the only epitaph the twelve had, eleven under stone and the
twelfth wrapped in a militia cloak with fever-light coming through
at the knuckles, asleep against the banner-pole with a fistful of
Nix's sleeve in one hand.

Vega sat by the cairn through the camp's first watch and mended
nothing. Some things are not for mending. Some things you are only
supposed to carry — the mountain folk teach that early — and she was
still learning the difference in her fortieth year at the anvil, and
suspected the learning was the climb, the whole of it, for her.

Up the adit's dark, close now, always, the drums beat the march that
never departs. Somewhere above the stone the Red Warcamp burned its
watch-fires over a caged people. Somewhere in it stood her steel.

Ede came out of the lamp-line near watch-end and stood by the cairn,
and for a while said nothing, in the manner of a woman out of practice
at asking.

"The cages," she said at last. "You're sure of your two doors."

"I am sure of my steel," said Vega. "I made it well. That has become
the best news on this floor, captain, because it means I know it to
the grain — and tomorrow I am going to stand at those locks and be
the flaw the smith left in them."

Ede nodded, slowly, and looked up the adit toward the drums, and Vega
watched the muster begin behind her eyes — three companies, and the
mine-dark, and twenty years of names — before a word of it was spoken
aloud.

"Then sleep," the captain said. "We open the mountain tomorrow."
