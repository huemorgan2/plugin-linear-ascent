# Chapter 22 — What Roothollow Sends

The rising ate eleven thousand loaves a day. Nix knew the number because
he had asked, and he had asked because the number was getting harder to
find in the wagons: nine thousand mouths, giants counted double, the new
ogres counted honestly, which was worse. An army does not starve all at
once. It starves the way a lamp goes out — a little less every night,
until somebody finally says it out loud.

The quartermasters said it out loud two days past the caldera, at the
morning muster, in the apologetic voice of men reporting weather. Three
grain-trains overdue below the Barrows. A tangle at the thirtieth gate,
lift-rights, nobody dead yet, the *yet* doing a soldier's work in the
sentence. And ahead lay the Deep — a city hung on silk over a hole in
the world — and no general takes nine thousand people across a chasm
on a half-empty sack.

Ede found him at his fire that evening. She had a paper in her hand and
the look she wore when she was about to spend somebody she liked.

"The floors below trust the rising," she said. "The rising is a rumor to
them, Nix. A wind that went up. What they trust is a goblin who took a
surrender at the Broodwarrens without one arrow loosed, and taught their
cousins their own names back. I need the trains moving and I need it
done without a sword anywhere in the doing. And —" the paper, folded
once — "the season's dead, for the masons. Somebody carries the list
down every season. This season I'd have it be somebody who knew them."

"Down," Nix said. "Down is the rare direction. I had that from a wyrm,
so it's likely true." He took the list, his hands doing the agreeing
before the rest of him was consulted. "If I miss the Deep crossing,
general, I'll want it known I objected."

"You'll be back before the silk. Ride the post."

He left at first light with his standard on his shoulder and the list
against his chest, on a wagon of empty sacks going home to be filled —
the most honest company, he decided by the second lift, that he had
kept in years.

The post went down the world the way water goes down a stair, and Nix
went with it, wagon to wagon, gate to gate, and made his counts.

The winter floors first, in reverse: the Doorstone Moor with the ash
already washed off it, the Rime Forest, the ice-road across the Frozen
Sea — staked now, inned now, Coldquay's markers copied onto boards at
every mile, a road where a battle had been. At the Moraine Gates he
stood in his first true lift-queue and could not stop looking at it. It
ran back from the gate-arch a quarter of a mile: grain-sledges, seed-
casks, a rope-walk's whole winter of cable coiled on drays, forty young
folk of some low floor in new boots, spears tied in bundles like
firewood, a bride going up to a wedding on the forty-
fourth, two coffins coming down. Nobody had ordered any of it. Below
the rising's boots, without asking anybody, the world had grown itself
an artery.

He rode it for six weeks, and the artery taught him its arithmetic.

A sack of Roothollow barley spent a season getting to the war: lift by
lift, floor by floor, each floor a country wide, each gate-road a week
of real weather — rain on the low pastures, frost above the fortieth,
one stretch of the Grey Rides where the escort changed twice a day out
of respect for old habits. Way-lofts stood at every gate-town now, and
every way-loft kept the same fair-copied rolls: what came through, what
was owed, which company wanted its loaves baked black, which floor's
oxen would not pass a windmill. Somebody in the provisioners' trade
had a hand like a hedge in winter, bare and exact, and that hand had
copied itself down fifty floors until the whole road wrote alike. He
asked a loft-keeper on the thirty-eighth who kept the master rolls, and
the man looked at him the way you look at somebody who asks who keeps
the sky up. "The office," he said, and thumped the ledger, and fed him,
and that was the whole theology of it.

At the Broodthrone the down-post crossed under the silk in file, and
the wagon-masters banked their fires high at the crossing-camps, paying
the warmth-clause the way you pay any toll you'd rather not discuss.
The court hung folded and formal in its high galleries, took its
tribute of heat, and let the world's grain go by. Nix rode that whole
floor with his head back. A contract kept by a spider, he thought, is
worth ten kept by men, on account of the extra legs.

The quarrel at the thirtieth gate took him four days, and no swords.

It was harvest below, that was the root of it. Hedgeharbor and the
drover floors and the warren country had all sent at once, and the
Barrowgate lift could take sixty loads a day, and there were six
hundred waiting, and the gate-clerks were working precedence from a
Tollman ledger older than the rising, which ranked floors by what
they had paid in the bad years — so the floors that had been robbed
longest went up last, and knew it, and had begun quietly unhitching
one another's oxen at night. Nobody dead yet. Yet.

Nix stood on a toll-post where everyone could see the standard, and
counted. It was all he did the first day: counted loads, out loud,
floor by floor, until the counting shamed the ledger — because a count
is honest the way a rank is not, and that gate knew the difference by
smell. Then he made
them a rota with no floors in it at all: grain by the ton, sons by the
company, seed before beer, and every tenth lift held empty — down-space
for the hurt and the letters and the coffins, because the road ran both
ways or it was not a road. Last, because he had learned it from a
spider, he made the two loudest floors swap stewards: your clerk rides
their train, theirs rides yours, and each of you feeds the other's man
at your own fire. Hostages, said an old drover, approving. Guests, said
Nix, and everybody was kind enough to let both words stand.

The trains moved on the fifth morning. Watching the first sixty go up,
he heard a wagon-master say the thing he heard everywhere afterward,
always with the same knock on wood: two years this road had
run, through a war, past floors still fevered, and never once — not
once — had a train been taken. Ambushes there had been, and burnt
toll-houses, and one bad spring at the Fair — but always a march behind
the grain, somehow, always a day late and a road short. The luck of the
rolls, the road called it. Nix, who counted luck the way he counted
everything, gave it a knot of respect and no further thought. Good
paper, he decided. Good paper is a kind of armor.

Two floors below the Barrows the road ran out along the rim, and out
in the slot, clamped round one of the great stays in collars of
timber and rope, a town hung — houses one below another like beads on
a wire, ladders between them, cisterns rigged to catch the falling
rain, lamps burning openly down the whole strand of it over four
miles of air. Its people came out on their platforms to watch the
wagons pass, the way rim-folk watch weather, and did not wave, and
Nix did not wave either. He counted the lamps — twenty-two — and gave
the town a notch of its own: small folk living where the tower had
left no room, owning nothing under and nothing over. The train did
not stop; he judged nothing ever did, and that this was likely why
the town was still there.

At the Broodwarrens the gate was kept by kobolds, and the rota-board
was chalked in the hooked running-hand of the warrens, and Nix sat at
the gate-matron's fire and was fed twice and argued with once, which is
kobold for beloved. Her name was Sesk; she had chosen it herself two
winters gone; she asked after the ninety-one by name until he ran out
of news, and then sent her grandson to the wagon with a sack of
seed-cakes, for the road, for the fire, for the ones learning letters.
The grandson said his own name at the tail of the errand, unasked, the
way you show a traveler your best knife. Nix rode away from that gate
with his eyes forward and did not count anything for a while.

The twentieth floor he had meant to pass quickly, and did not. The Red
Warcamp was a granary now. The mine that had eaten Shieldwall stood
open and lamplit and smelled of dust and barley, and in the deep
gallery the cages still stood in their rows — too much iron to haul
out — so the cages held grain, rank on rank of them, filled and
tallied and clean. The
drums of the Warcamp had been cut down long since; somebody had made
the drum-shells into measures, and the store-boys scooped barley with
them, and did not know. Nix stood a long time in the lamplight, hearing
what the shells did not say. Then he chalked his mark on the rota-board
like any other courier, and went down.

He came out of the Root Gate queue on the fortieth morning, into
Roothollow, and did what everyone from the up-floors did in the first
hour: he looked for the tower, and could not find it.

There was no tower. From the yards of Roothollow there was a ladder,
and the ladder was the sky. It went up past the roofs, past the kites,
past the weather — floor over floor over floor, each one a country
seen edge-on, a hairline of coast and cloud riding a shimmer of
rim-pillars too thin for what they carried, one bright thread of a
spine slanting between them — and it did not narrow and it did not
lean and it did not end: you could walk a day out into the scrub and
turn around and it had not become a shape; it had only become more of
the world, thin lands fading up into the haze until the air gave out
before the counting did. Clouds slid out of one land's rim, dragged
their shadows across the land below, and came apart. The war stood in
it too, the one way Roothollow ever saw the war: as color. The low
lands read gold-edged now, freed floor above freed floor with honest
weather riding their rims; one grey rung held among them, where a
queen kept her own dark by contract; and above the gold the grey went
on up into the haze, which was the size of the work remaining. On
clear nights,
they told him, you could see the undersides of the low floors
glimmering far up the stack, hanging lamps in the black, a country's
worth of them, like stars that paid rent. Nine years of his life hung
somewhere up that ladder, too high to point at; he stood with his head
back like any other pilgrim and did not try.

He delivered the list first, because the dead go first, that was the
whole rule of him. The masons had the Stone of the Climb and a sister-
slab beside it now, new-quarried, because the war wrote faster than one
stone could hold. The gate face glowed with fresh cuts — Wardens broken,
lifts opened, letters lit from within like coals — and the north face
took the season's dead in plain dead letters, floor by floor, while a
mason's boy read Nix's list aloud slowly and got the warren names right,
every one, because Nix stood at his elbow until he did.

Then the mason showed him the thirty.

They stood where Ede had cut them, the winter she was sober, and beside
most of the thirty names — not all; a few stood bare still, and the
mason's face closed over the bare ones like a lid — there was new work:
a small open gate, cut deep, the height of a thumb. "When the word came
down from the twentieth," the mason said, "half the town wanted them
ground off the north face, seeing as they'd been found alive. Stood
here shouting about it, whole families. I wouldn't. You don't unsay
stone; stone's not for unsaying. So: a gate. Cut it myself, one a
night, same as she cut them." He put his thumb in the gate beside DUN
HARROW, STANDARD-BEARER, the way you test a mortise. "Came back
through," he said. "That's all it means. It's enough."

The moot of Roothollow met in the long cellar under the gate-hall, and
it was running a war the way a blind man runs a farm he was born on —
by feel, by count, and by refusing to stop. On the cellar's end wall
someone had chalked the whole climb as a ladder of strokes, one to
sixty, no map, no country, no names: just strokes, and a rag hanging by
it that had rubbed out nothing in two years. They gave Nix the end of a
bench and asked him everything — the silk, the sea, the wyrm.
He did his best, and watched the words get to the door of the cellar
and refuse to fit through it, and understood at last what the low sky
did to a town: Roothollow was provisioning a story. It sent its barley
up into a wall and the wall sent back lists — casualty lists, requisition
lists, letters, once a season a wagon of broken collars for the forges
— and it believed, and argued, and levied, and never once got to *see*.
The gate-reeve put the year's true question the way a man puts his last
coin down: the seed-corn. Hold this year's back, against the rising
failing and the world coming down hungry? Or send it up whole, and bet
the town's next bread that the story was true?

They voted to send it. It took them until the lamps guttered, and the
granary-mistress wept angrily through her own aye, and they voted to
send it, all of it, the seed included, and Nix sat very still on his
bench-end and made the list he had come down, without knowing it, to
make. What Roothollow sends. Barley and seed-corn. Rope, arrowheads,
lamp-oil, cable. Its sons and daughters, forty a season through the
Root Gate in new boots. Letters by the hundredweight. The names of its
dead, cut by lamplight, and gates beside the found. And all of it
blind, all of it uphill, all of it on the word of people it could not
see, to a war it would never watch, under a sky that was a wall.

In the morning he walked past the drill-yard, where a broad-backed
woman he did not know was teaching eleven recruits how a hedge-lane
fights, and the horse trough wore its skim of dust, and the counts came
back at her in one voice, a breath early, the way counts always come
back the first year. He stopped at the granary row, where the ovens had
worked the night shift so the dawn queue could carry bread up warm, the
smell lying down the whole street like a hand on your shoulder. Then he
took his place in the up-queue with the levies and the seed-wagons, a
goblin with a standard in a line of everything a town could give, and
the Root Gate opened, and Roothollow sent.

The going up took longer, because the going up always does; he was
eight weeks with the great spring train, and he spent them being useful
in the manner of goblins — riding forward to chalk the rota-boards,
teaching the new levies their counts at night until they could say them
asleep, eating at fifty fires and hearing at every one some floor's own
war: Hedgeharbor's flood-quarrel, the drover floors' great question
of the bulls, two warrens' worth of kobold couriers who made him say
the census-line out loud — *goblins: one* — and then argued about it,
fiercely, as if the counting had wronged him. Once, from a lift-cage
at first light, a garden went by outside the bars: soil packed into
the saddle of a great cable-knot, three crooked apple trees growing
sideways out of it, miles above anybody's country. Nobody had planted
it, and he let it go by uncounted. The train climbed through
harvest and frost and the Broodthrone's silk, paying warmth, losing not
one wagon, and the higher it went the more it looked like what it was,
which Nix put into words for nobody on the last night below the Deep:

Not a supply-train. A country, walking. The rising was nine thousand
people up a stair — but the *rising*, the real one, was this: sixty
floors breathing in and out along one road, trading and quarreling and
marrying at the gate-towns, sending sons and grain and letters up into
weather they had to take on faith. Kill the army, and the low country
would grieve, and gather, and send another. The tower had spent eighty
years making sure no
two floors could want one thing together. Well. Here was one thing,
in six hundred wagons, wanting.

The host lay camped at the chasm-mouth when the train came up the last
gate-road, and it came out to meet its bread the way a town meets a
bride. Nix rode the lead wagon in with the seed-cakes for the ninety-
one in his lap and the moot's letter for Ede buttoned inside his shirt,
and gave the general her mail, and the freed kobolds their cakes, and
the quartermasters their tally — down and back, every load accounted,
nothing lost, nobody dead, *yet* retired from the sentence entirely.

"Well?" said Ede, when the letter was read.

"They sent the seed-corn," Nix said. He had meant to say it lightly and
it did not come out that way, so he let it stand. "All of it. Now feed
me, general. There's a city of bridges up ahead wanting walking, and I
mean to count every one of them twice."
