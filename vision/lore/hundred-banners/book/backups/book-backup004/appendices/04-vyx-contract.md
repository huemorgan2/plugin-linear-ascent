# The Contract of Vyx, in Full

*Taken down at the signing by the Matriarch's scrivener in the silk-
white book, under the hanging lanterns of the Broodthrone. This copy
was furnished to the compilers by the Matriarch herself, at posted
rates; her marginal annotations were charged for separately, and are
worth it.*

## Memorandum of the parties

The party of the first part: Ede Stonhal, called the banner-general,
for the host of the rising, its captains, its witnesses, its wagons and
stragglers inclusive.

The party of the second part: the Matriarch Vyx, freeholder of the
fortieth floor by right of survival.

## Recital

The party of the second part declines to fight. The parties are to
understand that this is not mercy but arithmetic: an army that mourns
its dead by name would win, at the price of a tenth of its banners and
the whole of a house nine hundred years in the furnishing, and ruin
profits no signatory. Therefore: terms.

## Clause the first — passage

The host of the rising crosses the floor of the second part unhindered,
by the gallery road, van to rear-guard, wagons and stragglers
inclusive; and the stair-lift behind the throne, kept wrapped these
many years against exactly this negotiation, is unwrapped for its use.
In perpetuity. Reinforcements, being wagons or stragglers within the
meaning of this clause, pass also, for years, until there are no more.
What is sold is the fortieth floor, open, without a single funeral.

## Clause the second — warmth

Tribute, annual, part the first: once in each year, for one night, the
cook-fires of the host are lit in the hall of the second part, and the
brood of the second part sleeps in the warmth of a nation's suppers.
The first part provides the suppers. The first part provides the
nation.

## Clause the third — the story

Tribute, annual, part the second: one true story, never before told to
any ear, delivered to the second part, at this court, in person, in
each year, for so long as this contract runs, which is forever. Truth,
novelty, and delivery are the coins of this clause and no other coin is
accepted. The second part will know a false one. The first part is
advised, in the instrument itself, not to try.

*Annotation of the second part: they ripen. Everything I keep does.*

## Clause the fourth — the hostage

Tribute, part the third: one hostage of rank, to dwell in the court of
the second part as surety while the host and its trains are upon her
floor, and to be surrendered whole, unwrapped, and unpuppeted — the
condition of the goods warranted by the second part — upon the rear-
guard's clearance of the tier-gate. Rank is of the essence. The second
part does not warehouse spear-carriers; the pledge is only as dear as
the head.

## Clause the fifth — board

The hostage eats what the court eats. *Annotation of the second part:
the hostage's correspondence, admitted under clause the ninth, rates
the fish above the army's. The court does not disagree.*

## Clause the sixth — liberty

The hostage is permitted any gallery whose silk bears his weight, and
forbidden the Larder. The first is a courtesy and the second is a
mercy, and the hostage is warranted intelligent enough to know the
difference.

## Clause the seventh — idleness

Boredom voids the warranty. The second part will accordingly supply
instruction in cradles, the stakes being one true answer per crashed
cradle, owed by the crasher. The parties acknowledge that the game
cannot be won, only lost more slowly, and that this too is
instruction.

## Clause the eighth — the furniture

The court of the second part, wrapped and puppeted, is her property
and her furniture and is not on the table. A party may dislike a
clause and sign. It is done every day.

## Clause the ninth — letters

The hostage may write. The court may read.

## Clause the tenth — the instruments

The clauses of the second part hold whether a party is present or
absent, paid or owing, living or otherwise; or her word is worth
nothing, and the first part has wintered with a beast. The second
part's instruments have held nine hundred years. The first part's
attention is drawn to the distinction between the second part and the
tower: there are always clauses.

## Memorandum, entered at the first part's insistence

This contract buys passage. It does not buy forgetting. The first part
will come back for every hanging light.

*Annotation of the second part: forgetting was not offered. The
annotation is supplied at no charge, being her own.*

## Discharges, in the scrivener's later hand

The first year's story: paid at signature, and handsomely; the Wake's
declined correspondence of three centuries is regarded as answered.

The hostage-clause: discharged with the thaw; warranty honored; goods
returned whole, and, the court notes, reluctant at the gallery's edge,
which the warranty does not cover and the second part did not cause,
much.

The story-clause, in the year of the fall: paid in the whole of the
war, from the first page to the light going home, told over some
nights; ruled by the second part an overpayment by some years. The
payer's answer — that he knows his clauses, and calls it interest — is
entered, and the account, uniquely in the books of the second part,
runs on by the free choice of both signatories.

The wrapped court, not being on the table, was not bought, and was not
forgotten. The cocoons stand empty; the rates for websteel are posted,
and countersigned, and the Matriarch charges a copper to see them.
