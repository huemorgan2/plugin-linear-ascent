# Chapter 11 — Hale's Lesson

Nix kept his counts on a tally-stick of bog-oak, because paper burned and
goblins forgot nothing but were believed about everything less than
sticks.

By the Smelterworks the stick read like this: nine floors since the
Kingsfield, notch. One collar thrown down at a banner-meadow, his own,
deepest notch on the stick, still tender to the thumb. One warren
yielded whole to one goblin's word, notch with a ring around it, which
was Nix's notation for *don't you dare ever stop being able to do that.*
Two ridge ogres of the Slag Barrens deserted alive, who had rolled a
whole slag-tip flat instead of rolling it onto the column, and now
walked with the baggage-train eating for six and pointing out good metal
in the heaps like enormous shy uncles. And eleven of the Pressed dead
across floors twenty-six and twenty-seven, eleven flat ugly notches
Nix cut slowly, because a dead conscript freed exactly nothing, and
somebody in this host had to keep the column with no heading, and Ede
kept it in ink and Nix kept it in oak and neither of them had ever
discussed it.

The Mirefields they had crossed dry-shod on a drowned road an elder
remembered — an elder the peat had kept whole a hundred years, which Nix
had opinions about and kept to himself — and the cairn-lights that tried
to lead the column into the pools had been broken one by one, guttering
out and draining down into the peat, evicted, ta-ta, no tears; things
made of drowned men's aether did not get notches. And then the
Smelterworks, floor twenty-eight, which was the loudest hottest floor in
the world and the first one Nix had walked onto and smelled his own
childhood: molten metal, wet stone, and small folk being spent.

The ladle kobolds worked the tap-bridges over the metal rivers, collared,
burn-scarred, flinging molten shot at the vanguard because a smelter-boss
with a lash stood behind them and a drop into the runnels stood behind
him. The host's shields could have pushed the bridges in an hour and the
cost would have been every kobold on them.

Nix went out onto the middle span instead, alone, hands empty, and stood
in ladle-shot range being visibly, unmistakably, a goblin.

"Evening," he called. "Look at me. No, properly look. You know what I
am? I'm the after. There's an after, that's the whole message, I'm the
proof and the messenger both — collar came off me nine floors down and
nobody's put one back on, and I eat twice a day, bread in the morning,
soup at night, sometimes a sausage when morale wants building — you're
throwing that at me, by the way, you're throwing sausages away every
time you miss —"

A ladle wavered.

"— and I know, I know, the boss behind you has a lash, but count him.
Go on. One. One boss. Now count us." He swept his arm back at the
gallery-mouth, where twelve hundred stood. "I ran out of numbers, and I
count *everything*."

It took until the middle of the night watch, and it was not clean —
nothing on a Pressed floor was ever clean. Four kobolds died before the
end: two to a smelter-boss who saw the yielding start, two on the shields
of men too tired to check their push, and Nix cut four flat notches by
firelight with his jaw aching, because a kill of the Pressed was not a
cure and not an eviction, just a bill the tower ran up in other people's
blood. But nineteen collars came off on the tap-bridges before dawn.
Vega snapped them cold in her fists, one by one, like a woman shelling
nuts, and the freed kobolds stood in a wobbly row not knowing where to
put their eyes.

The smallest one answered every question with her whole title: Third
Ladle, Second Bridge. Nix hunkered down in front of her.

"That's a job," he said. "Worse — it's a *shift*. You don't wear a shift
home. What's your name?"

She looked at him for a long time. Kobolds and goblins were cousins the
way a millstone and a hearthstone are cousins — same quarry, different
lives — and Nix waited, and the runnels glowed, and finally she said a
word in the old warren-tongue that meant the click a lock makes when it
opens.

"Pib," Nix said, delighted. "Tremendous. Best name on the floor. Pib,
first of her line." Notch — a new kind, a little door cut clean through
the edge of the stick. He was still admiring it when the runner came.

She came up the freed lifts from below, which nobody did, which was the
first wrongness — a Silkstead vault-girl, fifteen maybe, silk-cut across
both palms and burned along one arm, and she had run three floors' worth
of gate roads alone, day and night, through half-cured country, which
told you everything about the message before she said it.

She said it anyway, to Ede, in front of everyone, in the flat voice of
someone who has decided to be a bell and ring once and fall over:

"They're burning the Nursery. The grey soldiers. The ones your banner
left to guard our gates. Wend of Silkstead says — she says come and
see what your word is worth."

Nix did not remember the next hundred heartbeats honestly, and knew it.
He remembered noise, and Vega's hand closing his shoulder like a gate,
and Ede already giving orders — the van to hold the Smelterworks under
Bruna Stave and the banner, the party down the lifts, now, light, no
baggage.

Four lifts down. A fortnight of hard march, through the fair where the
great wheel still burned its funeral-light, through the Anvil Commons,
past sentries of their own rear-guard who would not meet anyone's eye,
grey pennons over every gate and every gate opening slower than it
should. Nix stood at the front of every platform with the tally-stick in
both hands, doing the worst arithmetic of his life. A warren yields to a
goblin's word. A goblin's word is guaranteed by the host. The host's
rear is guarded by men whose whole doctrine is that nothing with more
than four legs, or fewer than two, can yield at all.

He had left the ring-notch floor in the keeping of the one company that
did not believe in it.

"Too late" turned out to be a place. You could climb down into it. It
had a smell: silk doesn't burn clean, it melts and drips and stinks like
hair and sugar, and the smell met them a whole gallery above the warren
gate.

⁂

The Nursery was char. Where the egg-sacs had hung like grey fruit there
were black strings, still swinging, and the great patient midwives who
had listened to Nix in the dark — who had come down the web-lanes with
ten thousand children behind them and chosen an old truce over a new
fever — lay burst and curled at the chamber doors where they had died
holding them. Nix counted. That was his work; someone had to. Fourteen
midwives. The Nursery entire. And the first one, the very first, the one
who had touched his knocking hand with one forefoot like a bishop
conferring something — her he knew by the chip in her leg-armor, and he
stood in front of her a long time, and the stick stayed in his belt,
because there was no notch for this.

The brood had answered the way anything answers when its children burn.
Not the fever — the fever was five floors dead; this was only nature,
old honest nature, the kind that had kept truce for a thousand years and
knew exactly what to do when truce died. The warren had taken the
Cullers in the web-lanes where the silk still hung thickest.

Twenty of them, cocooned in grey, hanging in rows down the dark like the
tower's own larder — men who had marched in to burn a surrendered
nursery *before it hatched*, that was the phrase, Nix heard it from
three different grey mouths that evening, said each time with less
conviction, *before it hatched* — and the silk had come down on them
faster than any blade can cut, and what was in the cocoons was not
anything Wend's people would let the young girls see.

And Serjeant Hale was carrying them out.

He had led it. Nobody said so and everybody knew; you could read it in
the way the Culler survivors stood, in a horseshoe, facing away from
him, the way men face away from a fire too big to put out. He had led in
forty to *clear the rear*, kill-all, standing doctrine, the doctrine he
had taught to half the low tower and to Ede Stonhal herself once, in
another life, in a training-yard in Roothollow — and the doctrine had
walked into a yielded warren and met something that no longer had any
reason to hold back, and now he came up out of the web-lanes with a
dead man across his shoulders, and laid him in the row, and straightened
his arms, and went back down. Alone. Every time.

Vega offered. He looked through her. Wend Silkstead stood at the vault
door her people had opened on a goblin's promise and had now barred
again — barred against *the rising*, and that was a thing Nix was going
to carry the rest of his life, that sound, the Sealed Vault sealing —
and she said to Hale's back, in a town-warden's voice that carried the
length of the gallery:

"Twice, now. The first time it was the tower's fever broke the truce.
Say your reason. I want to hear what the free one is."

Hale did not answer. He went down, and came up with the eleventh body,
and laid it straight.

Nix watched all twenty. He owed that, he had decided; his word had been
the coin this was bought with, so he would stand at the ledger and watch
it spent to the last. Eleven. Fourteen. Seventeen. The silk-shadow lay
over the gallery in long grey bars, and in it Hale knelt each time
beside each cocooned man and cut him down himself, one hand bracing, one
hand working, armor of his own dead company hanging around him in the
dark like moths, and by the twentieth Nix understood what he was looking
at. This was not penance and not pride. The man was keeping a muster. It
was the only liturgy he had.

When the last body lay in the row, Hale stood over it awhile. Then he
walked to where Ede waited by the gate — she had not said one word to
him in five hours, had only worked, hauling silk and water like a
private soldier — and Nix, holding the lamp between them because
somebody had to hold it, saw the oldest soldier in the tower come to the
end of everything he knew.

"They yielded to a goblin." Hale's voice was ash and wire. "Ten thousand
of them. To that." A jerk of the head at Nix, without contempt — that
was the terrible thing, there was no contempt left in it, only a man
pointing at an answer he could not read. "Forty years I've held the low
stairs. Everything I ever kept alive, I kept alive by clearing what
could turn on it. It's arithmetic. It has *always* been arithmetic." He
looked at the row of his dead, twenty long. "Then arithmetic walked in
here and the silk took it. So you'll tell me, Stonhal, because you were
my recruit and you owe me one true answer and I'm calling it in." A
breath. "How does the goblin do it?"

Once. He asked it once, the way a man jumps off something.

Ede looked at Nix — asking leave, Nix realized, actually asking a
goblin's leave with her eyes, in front of Hale, which was its own kind
of answer — and then she said, quietly:

"He means it. That's the entire trick, and it can't be counterfeited,
and that's why you can't learn it the way you learned the sword. Every
yielded thing in this tower has spent its whole life reading the blade
behind the offer. Nix walks in and there's no blade. Not hidden. Not
anywhere. They look at him and see the after."

Hale stood in the silk-shadow among the cocooned armor of his own, and
Nix watched forty years of doctrine settle onto the man's shoulders with
its full weight, no longer load-bearing, just heavy — and because Nix
was Nix, and because silence was a country he had never once been able
to hold, he heard himself say:

"Twenty of yours. Fourteen of theirs. That's the score doctrine kept
today, serjeant. Next floor, try for nothing-nothing. It's a very
clean number. I can teach you the knocking in an afternoon — the signs
are easy." His voice cracked on the joke and he let it. "Meaning it
takes longer."

Hale looked at him — really looked, first time in ten floors — and said
nothing, and picked up the first body of the twenty, and began carrying
his dead up the long stair to the light, one at a time, refusing help to
the last man.

⁂

The brood had gone deep, past all lamplight, and would not answer the
knocking — not Nix's, not the vault-folk's, maybe not for a generation,
said Wend Silkstead, maybe not ever. The truce was dead. The floor
stayed freed;
freedom, it turned out, was the kind of thing you could not give back
even when you wanted to. Silkstead would live. Everything else was
notches.

They buried none of the Cullers on twenty-four — Wend's one condition,
delivered flat, and no one argued. The grey company wrapped its dead for
carrying and turned up the stair, bound for the only ground on the climb
that was made for it: the grave-country three floors above, the Tithe
Barrows, where Men had buried Men since before the Theft, and where — the
runners said — Voss's Tollmen had already chained the lych-gate and were
charging silver at the stair, and the Wake's grey bows had taken the
Tollmaster's fee to stand guard on the chains, and the whole host was
funneling up toward all of it with its blood still hot from the silk.

Nix climbed at the rear of the burial-column, keeping his counts. Twenty
dead men ahead of him going up to be buried in a floor that charged
admission, carried by a company that had stopped believing in its own
arithmetic, into three armies' worth of quarrel over one gate.

He got out the tally-stick, thumbed the ring-notch once, for courage,
and cut a fresh mark of his own invention: a warning-notch, first of its
kind, for a debt still running.

Above them, the crows were already gathering over the barrows.
