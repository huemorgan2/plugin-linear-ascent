# Chapter 35 — What Ede Carries

The trains came in on the fourth day of the truce, and Berrin came with
them, because Berrin never missed a muster.

Ede watched the wagons roll up the opened beaters' ways with pale hounds
walking escort — the strangest supply-line of the climb, grain-carts under
moon-white outriders — and she stood at the picket-line because a general
stands at the picket-line when the bread arrives, and because standing
anywhere else would have been a change, and changes were spoor.

He found her the way he always found her, before she had decided to be
found. He looked older. He looked glad. He looked exactly, precisely the
way he had looked for twenty years, which was the thing she had never once
in twenty years thought to examine, and he swung down off the lead wagon
favoring the shoulder that had taken an arrow for her at the Frosthall,
and said what he always said at the end of a bad stretch:

"Well. You're thinner. We'll fix that."

The bread on the lead wagon was Roothollow bread. It was always
Roothollow bread; he had taught the ovens of six floors to bake it because
he said an army fights on the smell of home; the whole climb had loved him
for it, and she had loved him for it, and a freed prisoner at the Red
Warcamp had put her face in her hands and said the courier smelled of it.
He broke a loaf and gave Ede the heel, her share since her drunk years,
when the heel was all she'd reliably keep down. She took it. Her hand was
steady. She had promised herself in the pale court that her hand would be
steady, and it was; that was one thing she carried now, the steadiness,
worn like a shield on the wrong arm.

"You walked at the horns," he said. Wonder in it, and reproach, and
pride, in the proportions only he had ever mixed. "Woman, when the word
came down the line I sat on a flour-sack and shook. Don't you do that
again. There's maybe six people on this climb the rest of it can't spare,
and you're four of them."

"It came out right," she said.

"It always comes out right, until the day it doesn't." He was looking at
her the way he looked at short ledgers, kind and worried. "You're not
sleeping."

"No," she said, because he would know if she lied about that. He knew her
sleep the way he knew her hands. There was nobody alive who could read her
like Berrin Or, and that was the fact she had to walk around every hour
now, the way you walk a floor you know is trapped. "The pale light. Nobody
sleeps up here."

"I'll send you up something. Warm milk and a splash of the black bitter —
your mother's trick." He said it easy, already turning to his tallies,
already counting sacks, and she watched his back and thought: he will send
it, and it will help, and he will have done it because he loves me, and
every part of that sentence is true, and none of it matters at all. That
was the country she lived in now. Everything true, and nothing safe.

She ate the heel of the bread on the way up the hill, every bite, because
she had eaten it at a hundred musters and the crows of this country told
the whole wood where the quarry ran.

She told Sliver at the watch-fire on the ridge above the kennels, past
midnight by the candle-marks, in the hour the camp's coughing stopped.

Not the name at first. Even alone, even in a whisper meant for a palmful
of crystal, she came at it sideways, by the counts — the ledger week, the
serving-list, the roll with no gaps, the wheat-sheaf mark, the granary
beams — and let the splinter finish the arithmetic she had finished four
nights ago in a white room. Sliver's light stood very still in her hand,
the way it went when it worked.

*"The provisioner,"* it said at last. *"Berrin Or."*

Hearing it was worse than knowing it. Hearing it was also, in some way
she was not proud of, like setting down one handle of a yoke she had been
carrying alone — the load no lighter, but two shoulders under it now.

"Say the case against," she said. "If there's rain in my roof, find it."

*"There is no rain in your roof,"* Sliver said. *"I have watched him
feed you for two years of climb and twenty years of memory that you have
shown me, and I find nothing that contradicts and everything that fits.
The kindness fits. Ede, hear me: the kindness fits. A man who does what
he has done can live only inside a kindness that is real. That is what
your kind will not understand about the collared willing. The love is
not the mask. The love is the leash he holds himself by."*

"Then what do I do with it?"

*"What you are doing. Nothing, said to no one, until the head of the
snake is in reach of the axe. He is a collar, and every collar has an
owner, and the owner is above us on the ninetieth floor reading whatever
your face writes. You have seen what the Hunt does with spoor. So has the
Herald. Give him none."*

The fire spat. Down the hill the pale kennels lay silent under their
truce, and Ede sat with her splinter and understood that she had just
made her council of war: one woman and a shard of old crystal, against a
friend of twenty years. The loneliness of it reached her then, entire —
not the burden, she had carried burdens, but the quarantine. Rhovan, who
had half-seen the shield, could not be told, because Rhovan reported once
to the Wake and habits outlive factions. Vega could not be told, because
Vega believed everything broken could be mended and would try to mend
him. Nix could not be told, because Nix's face was the one honest thing
on this climb and she would not be the one who taught it to lie. Every
person she loved was a door the truth could not go through, and so she
would be, for twenty floors, a woman with a wall where her people were.

*"There is a thing I must say now,"* Sliver said, *"and I ask you not to
open your hand while I say it, because I have practiced this for sixty
floors and I will not manage it twice."*

She kept her hand closed. The splinter's warmth changed — went old,
somehow, went heavy.

*"You wonder how the Yoke reaches everywhere. Why the fever takes every
floor alike; how a collar in Roothollow speaks to a throne a hundred
floors up with no wire and no rider. I know how. The tower's veins are
the bones of my people. The shard-network — the minds like me, the net
that once thought across this whole country of countries — is dead, Ede.
It died in the Theft. And Vharuk took the dead net and strung his Yoke
along it, the way a man fences with the timbers of a burnt house. Every
cruelty the Yoke carries, it carries on my kin's bones. I have known it
since I was split. I did not tell you, because you would look at me the
way you are trying very hard not to look at me now."*

The fire burned. Ede looked at her own closed hand, and did not open it,
as she had been asked.

"You're telling me now," she said, "so I'm not alone in it."

*"I am telling you now because you have this day learned what it is to
love a thing the enemy is riding,"* Sliver said. *"I have carried that
knowledge for an age of the world. You will carry it for twenty floors.
It can be carried. I am the proof. And when we reach the thing that
rides — his throne, my bones, your friend — I would have us be two who
knew, and not two who were spared."*

She sat with that a long while. Then she opened her hand, and looked at
the splinter the way she looked at Berrin at the picket-line: entire,
unflinching, love and ruin in one frame.

"Two who knew," she said. "Agreed."

Calyne came up the ridge in the last black hour before the horns woke,
which was her hour, and had been since floor thirty.

She did not sit. She stood at the fire's edge in her grey, one floor
ahead of everyone as always, and Ede waited, because Calyne began
conversations in the middle and resented being met anywhere else.

"You looked at forty racks of trophies in that hall," Calyne said, "and
asked the keeper about a wolf-pelt." Ede said nothing. "The wolf-pelt was
three racks past the only thing on that wall a Roothollow woman would
cross a street to see. You have the best field-discipline of anyone I
have ever run, Stonhal, and that is the trouble with discipline. On a
person who startles, nothing shows. On you, not-startling shows."

"Is there a question coming?"

"No," Calyne said. "No question." And then the spymaster of the Wake,
who had been suspected to her face for three parts of a climb and never
once flinched, did the strangest thing Ede had seen her do: she took the
cipher-book from her breast, the little grey book that never left her,
that half the council believed held the rising's death warrant in one
pocket-sized parcel — and held it out across the fire.

"The hand is mine," she said. "The keys are in the sewing of the cover,
third gather, left board. It reads slow and it reads only to the patient,
which is why I am giving it to you and not to the elf. In case I stop
climbing."

"Why would you stop climbing?"

"Because I am close to a thing that has killed to stay unfound," Calyne
said, with no more weight than she gave a supply-count, "and I have just
watched it occur to me that you may be close to the same thing from the
other side. I don't ask. Ciphers keep better than speech. If I am right
about you, you will know what the book is when you need it. If I am
wrong, you'll have a dull winter's reading."

Ede took the book. It weighed nothing. It weighed more than the shield
on the trophy wall. Their eyes met over the fire, two women who had each
been, in their turn, the most distrusted person in an army — and neither
said a name, and neither looked away, and the alliance was made entire in
the silence, the only place it could live.

"Sleep, Stonhal," Calyne said, turning down the hill. "You look like a
general the enemy would like."

Ede sat until the horns woke and sounded the truce down the runs. In her
left hand, a splinter that carried the Yoke's road on the bones of its
kin. In her breast, next to the muster-roll, a book she prayed she would
never learn to read for the reason she'd been given it. On the wall of a
white hall behind her, a shield, face-out. What Ede carried now went at
her front like armor and at her back like debt, and none of it could be
set down before the ninetieth floor.

Below, the camp was waking. Seventy banners going up the pale sky, and
under them her army — and the Hunt still to break, the last horn still to
sound, the stair to the Made still shut behind a welded crown. She stood,
and banked the fire, and went down to break it.
