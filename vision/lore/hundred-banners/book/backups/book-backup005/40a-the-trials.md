# Chapter 48 — The Trials

There was no morning on the summit. There would never be one; the black
sky over the highest hall in creation owed nobody a dawn, and the freed
world camped beneath it woke instead to a bell.

Vega's bell — rung once for a battle and once for the dead — spoke the
third ringing of its life across the iron acres, and it was not the
Break-word this time and not a mourning. It was a summons. The tower
had rung bells at people for an age; here, Ede thought, under the
gantry while the brass settled, was a bell that asked.

What it asked them to was the thing nobody had wanted to name the night
before, while the light went home and the world argued about supper.
The dead King had left the living behind. The courtiers of the gilded
tiers taken alive when their floor fell. The under-heralds of Malgrim's
order, willing clerks of a willing trade, swept up alive at the Gate
and after. The factors and lift-lords and levy-brokers of the freed
floors, who had never worn a collar and had grown fat selling the
collared their neighbors. And the collared-by-choice, the quiet willing
of a hundred floors, whose iron had fallen dead off them in the same
breath as everyone else's, and who stood now in the crowds of the freed
wearing the same bare necks and not the same hearts.

"Battle is a bad court, but it sits quick," Ede had said at the council
fire, the night the throne came down. "This is different. This is us,
in the morning, with time to think. Whatever we do now is the peace,
doing its first day's work."

She would not take the judgment. She had refused it at the ninetieth
floor with her oldest friend bound in front of her, and she refused it
now, for the same reason: she had climbed two years to keep the rising
from becoming a thing with one neck and one hand. What she did instead,
with the council, in the night, was build a court.

They built it out of what the climb already owned. The charter-table
came up from the baggage — the long plank of the Frosthall, scarred and
ringed by the whole war's councils — and was set where the throne had
stood until yesterday, on the bare patch Vega's crews had left when
they carried the King's chair away as scrap. Dara Lastmuster brought
the conscript-rolls and her striking-pen. Bly's servants brought the
long ledger of the gilded court's crimes, read once over a ruin and
carried up so the King would hear it; the King was past hearing, and
the book remained, which was the way of books. Nib's waiting-folk
brought the petitions — eighty years of the taken's unread grievances out
of the Filing Crypt — and stacked them at the table's end in bundles
tied with grey cord: the case against the tower, written by its victims
and filed by its own clerks. And Voss came with his scales and his
day-book, because sentence in this court would be counted in grain and
roads and years of keeping, and somebody had to price a year of keeping
who could not be accused of mercy.

The jury was the freed. That was the whole invention, and it was not
even new; it was the sentence of Berrin Or, grown up. For each of the
willing, the wronged floor's own people to hear and answer — not the
general, not a bench of the grand. The ones who had paid were the ones
with the right to set the price.

*Evil kept good books,* Sliver said from her breastbone, as the first
bundle was untied. *I told you at the Warcamp to learn the habit. I did
not think you would improve on it.*

The court's first entry was the rising's own.

Ede had settled that with herself in the night, and with nobody else.
When the table was seated and the hall's vast hush had gathered in, she
went to Dara Lastmuster's open book before any accused was called, and
gave her name to the clerk as plainly as a recruit at a gate.

"Enter this. On the ninety-eighth floor, the court of the gilded tiers
being taken and its lords refusing all terms, I ordered killing past
the needs of the fight. The men and women who did it did it on my word,
and their names do not belong in this line. Mine does." The pen
scratched. The hall did not breathe. "Write it plain, so that whoever
keeps rolls after us knows the rising did it, and knows the rising
wrote it down."

Rhovan stood at her shoulder while it was written. On the third floor
of the world, a lifetime ago, he had logged a kill of hers in silence,
a surrendered kobold, one line in a book; neither of them had ever
spoken of it, and he did not speak of it now. He only took the pen
after her, as witness, and signed, and that was the whole conversation.

It was not absolution and was not meant to be. It was the ledger's
oldest law, the one the tower had taught them by obeying it: what is
written down can be answered for. What is not written down is loose in
the world, doing its work in the dark.

Then the willing were called.

⁂

The first called was a woman of Lampfall, Annis Cray, and her case
taught the court its measure.

She was old, a miner's widow, the collar-scar at her throat rubbed
smooth with years. Nobody had informed on her; she could have stood
among the freed forever. She came to the table herself, first of all
the willing in the world, and said the iron had been her own asking —
taken in the famine winter the tolls trebled, for a granary post and
a double ration that fed three grandchildren whose parents the tower
had already taken. She had weighed it then, her neck against their
winter, and would weigh it the same again; and they should do as they
liked, since everyone she had made the bargain for was grown now, and
safe, and here — and she nodded, once, at two young miners in the
crowd with their caps in their hands.

The jury of Lampfall was out no time at all: the tower had built a
world where a grandmother's neck was the price of bread, and then had
the gall to keep her receipt; the crime was the famine, and the
famine had an owner, and the owner was dead. Annis Cray's name was
struck from the registers of the willing and entered in the rolls of
the wronged, where it had belonged for twenty years.

Ede sealed it and found her voice needed a moment. Somewhere behind her
eyes a man on a broken drum-throne poured a chain from hand to hand and
asked her who chose you? The line between the willing and the starved
ran through every soul the tower had ever touched, and the court would
misplace it a hundred times in the years ahead. But it had begun by
knowing the line was there.

The second was Corven Dale, and he was another matter.

Levy-master of the Anvil Commons, a free man all his life: no collar,
no scar, no famine at his back. For eleven years he had filled the
tower's labor-drafts under license, and his manifests — kept in his own
neat hand, seized in his own strong-room — showed four hundred and six
souls of his floor sold up the lifts, debtors and strangers and the
awkward poor, priced by age and trade. He had grown mills on it. He
stood before the jury of his own floor in a good coat and made the
defense Ede had known was coming, because she had heard its shape
before, from a kinder mouth.

"The drafts were the tower's law," Corven Dale said. "Refuse, and the
press-gangs took at random — the old, the mothers, anyone. I chose. I
chose carefully. Fewer went up under my hand than under any factor
before me, and the Commons stood while harder floors burned. Read the
numbers, if you can read anything. I was the gentlest thing the law
allowed to exist."

True in every line, Ede thought, and wrong in the sum. The tower's
arithmetic, still walking around in a good coat.

A woman of the Commons stood up in the jury with her hands shaking and
asked for rope. Her name was Edda Trench; her two sons were in the
manifests, one priced at fourteen, one at sixteen; neither had come
back down with the freed, and Dara Lastmuster's rolls had one of them
struck at the Welding Halls, and of the other no line at all. She said
rope, and said it again, and the hall's great crowd made a sound under
its breath like weather changing, because four hundred and six is a
number, and a mother is not.

It was Marta Reed who answered her — senior of the thirty, who had
stood in the ring at the ninetieth floor and voted rope for Berrin Or
and been outvoted by her own company.

"I asked for rope once," Marta said. "Ten floors down, for a worse man
than this one. My company voted me down, and I have had the tiers to
think about it, so I'll say what the thinking came to." She did not
orate; Shieldwall had never had an orator; she said it the way you read
a delivery-bill. "A man of ours said it at that fire: rope's a wage.
Paid once, and done, and light. That man —" she looked at Corven Dale
the way you look at weather, "— owes four hundred and six years of
other people's lives, and I'm not minded to let him settle for one of
his own. The sentence of Berrin Or stands at the ninetieth floor,
written by the thirty and sealed by the general. I ask this court to
cite it, and better it."

So it was cited — read whole into the record by Dara Lastmuster, the
first precedent of the freed world's law: *he shall live; he shall
provision what he sold, one year's keeping for every soul, at the
reckoning of that floor's keeper; no blade, no office, no books but the
open kind; and he shall walk it under parole.* And the jury of the
Anvil Commons went out and came back and bettered it.

Corven Dale would live. His strong-rooms would open to Voss's
assessors, and his mills would grind under the floor's own wardship —
and he would do the accounting: name by name, he would trace his
manifests through the tower's registers, the welding-lists, the
conscript-rolls, the grave-wards' books, until every soul he had priced
was found living and provisioned, or found dead and brought home, or
written into the rolls of the lost with his name beside the entry as
the man who owed the finding. One year's keeping to every household, at
the keeper's reckoning. No blade, no office, no ledger but the open
kind. Under parole.

"Whose parole?" Ede asked, as the form required, and Edda Trench stood
up before Nix could, and said, in a voice with nothing left in it but
the iron:

"Mine. He'll start with my sons."

Nix, who held one parole already and had come ready to hold more, took
a fresh slate from his bag, wrote her name at the top, and handed it
over, and showed her, leaning close, the way he ruled his columns: once
for the floor, once for the dead.

The third was a man of Fairstead, Garet Hode, and his was the verdict
Ede carried away from the whole day's sitting.

No collar, no license, no coin taken. For six years he had whispered
names at the Fairstead toll-gate — who grumbled at the levy, who
hoarded seed, who taught their children the old letters — and one in
three of the named had gone up the lifts. The gate-clerks had held
his daughter's name over him all six years; men of his own lane stood
and said so; and the daughter herself stood in the crowd, grown and
living, and did not look at him. The jury of Fairstead had buried the
named. It was out a long time, and it came back, and it entered Garet
Hode in the registers of the willing, at the willing's whole
reckoning: a year's keeping to every household emptied on his word,
under the parole of a woman whose brothers he had named.

Ede had weighed him while they were out, the way she had weighed
Annis Cray, and the beam had come down otherwise. Fear is a collar
too, she thought; it only leaves no scar for a court to find. Had the
judgment been hers, his name went to the rolls of the wronged, beside
the widow's.

It was not hers. That was the whole of what they had built: a court
she could overrule would only have been her own arm in a longer
sleeve. She pressed the seal down, slow, and her hand did not shake.
That night she could not call up the man's face at all — only the
daughter's, watching the wax.

They sat until the lamps were twice trimmed, and the shape of the peace
came out of the cases the way a road comes out of walkers.

The under-heralds of Malgrim's order, seven taken alive: the floors
they had marked for the Herald-fire sentenced them to rebuild what the
fire took, gate by gate, at the keepers' reckoning — and then to a
longer work. The petitions. Eighty years of the taken's grievances stood
bundled on that table, unread by the power they were addressed to, and
the court sentenced the Herald's clerks to read them — aloud, floor by
floor, on the floors where they were written, to whoever came; every
petition, to its end, however many years of evenings it took; because
witness was half of every sentence this court gave, and because men
who had spent their lives writing the tower's answers could spend the
rest of them delivering the world's mail.

The courtiers of the gilded tiers, the willing who had yielded when
their lords did not: sentenced each to the floors their fortunes had
been farmed from, to work the reckonings their own stewards' books laid
out, under the parole of those floors' keepers. Two refused — a silk-
voiced pair spared the Vizier's end, who declined, with perfect
courtesy, to work at all. The court did not keep them. It kept nothing
and no one; that law was older in the rising than the court was. Their
refusals were written into the book, witnessed, and sealed, and the two
were let walk out through the crowd of the freed with their names gone
ahead of them down a hundred floors of a world that kept books now, and
knew what they were owed, and owed them exactly that.

"They'll starve on courtesy inside a year," Rhovan said, watching them
go.

"Then they'll come to a reckoning hungry," Ede said. "The table sits on
every floor. That's the sentence. They're only choosing the door they
come in by."

Late in the day, Aldis Bright came to stand at Ede's shoulder and
watch the court work, the oldest soldier in the world reading the
freed world's first law the way quartermasters read, fast and twice.

"We never got this far," she said at last. "My climb. We had the word
for the collars and the steel for the Wardens and nothing at all for
the day after. I used to think that was why we lost." She watched Edda
Trench bend over a slate with a goblin, ruling columns. "Write this
part down twice, general. Any age can break a thing. This is the craft
that doesn't come back on its own."

⁂

The bell rang the court down at dusk — the fourth ringing, and the
first that meant only: done for the day, and more tomorrow, and
tomorrow's tomorrow, on the floors, going down. The court would travel
with the wagons. It would sit at the Herald's Gate and the Frosthall
and the Fair and the Warcamp, wherever the willing had debts and the
wronged had voices, for years, in gate-towns Ede would never see. The
best of what they had built was that it did not need her.

The keepers' reports had come up with the last supply-trains: true
light standing inland where the gloom had been, pale trees gone over
to honest green, the first heavy harvests in eighty years, the dens of
the deep middles beginning to empty. Ede had them read before the
sitting; a court should know what world its sentences are for.

She stayed at the table after, in the lamp-hush, with the seal cooling
under her hand. It had cost her all day, the old cost: rope was clean,
rope was quick, and every hour of that long sitting some tired soldier
at the back of her skull had asked for the gate at dawn and the short
answer — and she had paid the day out in the old slow coin instead,
and the spending held, and holding it was work, and would be work all
her life. Mercy was not the wage of the strong. It was the discipline of
the tired, kept up daily, like drill.

Across the hall, in a ring of lamplight, an under-herald of the King
sat on a wagon-board with a grey-corded bundle open on his knees,
reading aloud to the waiting-folk the first petition anyone had ever
answered: a request, eighty years old, in a hand gone brown, that the
lamplighter of a drowned village be told his daughter was alive. The
clerk read it badly, his voice going; the waiting-folk listened the way
the freed listen, which is completely; and when he finished, an old
woman at the ring's edge stood and said the daughter's name, and her
own, and where she had died, and that she had died free — and the clerk
wrote the answer on the petition's back, the first line of a work of
years.

Tomorrow the wagons. Tomorrow the long stair home.

Tonight the reading went on into the dark, petition by petition, name
by name, and the freed world sat up listening under its black sky, and
nobody anywhere was waiting for a number to be called.
