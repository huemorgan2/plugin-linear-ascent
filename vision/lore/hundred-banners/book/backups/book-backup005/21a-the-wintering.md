# Chapter 25 — The Wintering

The Frosthall's own smithy had stood cold for eleven years, because a
feast that never ends has no use for a forge, and Vega Coalbrow spent
her first free morning on the sixtieth floor bringing it back.

She did it the way her mother had taught her to wake any fire that had
been wronged: slowly, and with an apology. She raked the old dead
charcoal out by hand and found under it the last smith's work where he
had dropped it the day the song took the hall — a sledge-shoe, half
drawn, one end still in its curl. She set it on the bench where it
could watch. Then small wood, then coal, then wind from the great
bellows an arm-stroke at a time, until by noon the chimney remembered
its trade and the ice let go of the roof-slates over the smithy in one
long slide, like a season's coal let down the chute at once, and half
the yard came to look at the smoke as if smoke were a banner.

It was, she thought. That one was.

The jarldom was a winter country the way the sea is a wet one: whitely,
hugely, and without apology — steadings strung down the firth-vales a
day's sledge apart and then a week's, herd-halls and byre-towns out to
a horizon that smoked with snow, the last herds still walking in off
the summer grass a full week's droving out, and over it all a hard
white sky of its own, going up further than kites went. Sixty floors
below, in another life, there was a town at the tower's root built out
of everyone the theft had spilled, her own folk among them. She had
not expected to meet its like again halfway up the sky. But the word
had gone out from Hrimgar's
hall — *the host winters here; open the doors* — and the jarldom had
opened them, all of them, a country of them, and the army poured out
into it and thinned and settled, company by company, like meltwater
finding old channels. Shieldwall quartered in the great hall and its
crofts. The keepers went to the near steadings. The Red Orcs took the
high shieling that nobody offered them and sent down smoked fish as
rent, which after some study the thanes decided to be honored by.

And twice a week, out and back along the whole strung length of the
jarldom, went the sledge-post — six teams, bells on the hames, carrying
letters and salt and parliament-summonses and the hall's slow argument
out to the far steadings and the steadings' answers in. The war had a
front somewhere, floors above, past a lift none of them had opened yet.
The winter had a route. Vega shod its runners, every team, twice over,
and kept a tally of the shoeings on the smithy doorpost, and found she
liked the tally better than most of what she had written that year.

In the hall the parliament argued from the morning horn to the dousing
of the lamps; that has its own chapter in the annals, and her own worst
days are in it, and this is not that chapter. What the annals kept less
carefully was the yard. The hall made clauses that winter. The yard
made a town.

The weddings began at midwinter, the way they always do in a camp that
has stopped being sure it will die soon.

Eleven of them, before the thaw. Vega counted, because each one came
through her smithy: the jarldom's custom asked iron of a marriage, and
the host's dozen customs asked a dozen things, and somewhere in the
first snows the two had struck a bargain at her anvil without asking
her. The couple brought two pieces of their own iron — a buckle, a
spearhead, a hinge-pin, once a whole ploughshare hauled up sixty floors
in a dower-chest — and Vega forge-welded the two into one bar, and
drew the bar, and cut it, and rung it into two rings, so that each
wore the whole of both. It is beginner's work, a weld like that, and
it is also the whole craft: heat honest, strike true, and no seam to
find afterward, though both grains go on all the way through.

The one she kept was Edda's. A thane's daughter of Hearthold, freed
eight weeks, the collar-mark at her neck still pale, and her man a
Shieldwall pikeman who had come out of the cages of the twentieth
floor — the two of them standing in the smithy heat with
their iron in their four hands like people holding a bird. Neither one
had slept a whole night that autumn; the whole yard knew it; the yard
knows everything. When the weld took — clean, first fire, the two
grains biting — Edda laughed at nothing, the way the newly freed
laugh, upward, as if the sound had been let out of a cellar. Vega made
their rings a half-measure heavy, for the wear.

Bruna sang at that one. The feast after was barley-cakes and one cask,
in a byre, among the wintering oxen, and the fiddler was a Culler and
the cook was a kobold and the bride's father had been a shield on a
frost-birch tree eight weeks before, and nobody remarked on any of it.
That was the thing about that winter Vega set down in her flat ledger
of the world, the entry she went back to afterward more than any
other: nobody remarked. The gathered world had stopped being a wonder
to itself. It had begun keeping house.

Old Marn died in the second month, in her sleep, in the hay of the
long byre with her boots set together by the wall, and the army
learned that it had elders now, and that it could lose one to nothing
at all — to seventy years and a life of lading — and the loss would
still need carrying.

Marn Deepfurrow had come to the rising at the Gear Galleries the same
week as Vega, a wagon-matron of the deep halls with a back like a
keel, and had walked every floor since with the trains: hauled the
pontoons at the Frozen Sea, corduroyed the ash-roads at the
Cindermouth, pulled two oxen and a boy out of a crevasse on the Troll
Bridges with her hands. She had no family in the host. She had the
host.

The giants' rite is short, because it is mostly digging. They took her
out past the in-fields to a rise the jarl gave freely, and dug through
five feet of snow and three of frost to the true ground, the *jorth*,
turned dark and honest under all that white, and they laid her in it
with her lading-hooks and her hame-bells and the little whetstone she
had carried since her own mother's forge. Her sledge-team stood
unharnessed at the graveside the whole while, blowing steam, because
the drovers judged the beasts had the right and no one argued. Then
every soul who had ever ridden her wagons — and it took the muster-
clerks no time at all to discover this was most of the army — filed
past and put one thing each into the grave-fill: a nail, a button, a
knot of rope, tokens of carried things, so that she went into the
ground of the sixtieth floor under a yard of the whole rising's
freight. Vega spoke the forge-words at the head of it, the old ones,
about iron coming out of the ground and work being the rent we pay for
it. Then, because the rite asks it, the Frosthall smithy stood cold
that night, one night, the fire let go out on purpose and lit new at
dawn — a thing Vega had done for others perhaps thirty times in her
life, and that cost her more that winter than it ever had, though she
could not have said toward what the cost was paid.

Hrimgar came to the grave last, alone, bare-headed in the falling
snow. He stood a long time. "Eleven years I fed on my own folk," he
said at last, to the mound, in the deep tongue, slowly, the way his
kind finish what they start. "You died having fed mine. I will hold
the difference where I hold my crown, which is at arm's length, where
I can see it." Then he bowed, a jarl to a wagon-matron, and the snow
went on filling the tokens' little hollows, and that was the funeral.

The next sledge-post out, every far steading sent its children in to
be counted on the rising's rolls, which the clerks recorded as a
courtesy of the jarldom, and Vega recorded otherwise: a country does
not lend its children's names to a passing army. It lends them to a
neighbor.

Nix's fire had become a school by then, and the school had walls,
because Bruna had thrown the goblin out of the wind and into the
Undercroft's smallest vault, which had held the feast's endless casks
for eleven years and now held a hearth, nine benches, ninety-one
kobolds in shifts, and one standard leaned in the corner like a broom.

He was teaching letters now. The names had come back first — that was
old work, done in the winter camps below — and a name, Nix said, wants
somewhere to stand. So: letters. Vega came down in the third month
with what the school had commissioned of her, the only commission of
her life she had filled for a fee of burnt crusts: letter-punches, a
full set, sized for kobold hands, so a learner could strike a word
into a strip of lead and hold the word after, heft it, carry it home
in a pocket. Learning to write, the goblin explained, was mostly
learning that a word will hold still. "Paper doesn't convince them.
Paper's weather. Lead they believe."

She stayed the evening and watched the one they called Chalk — an old
digger with a made name and hands like tree-roots — strike his own
five letters into lead, spell them back, and then, unasked, strike a
second strip and carry it across the vault to her, formal as a herald,
the metal still warm from the work. It said VEGA. It weighed what a
key weighs. She has it still.

They were not her people, the Pressed. Her people had forged the
collars; that mark was on her; she had stopped asking the mark to go
away and started asking it to be useful. But she sat in the smallest
vault of the Undercroft with lead-strips cooling on nine benches and
thought: sixty floors down, the tower believes it is fighting an army.
It is fighting *this*. It never once planned for this, because this
cannot be planned for, only fed.

Upstairs, by the great hearth, the other school kept its own hours.

Dun Harrow had claimed the settle nearest the fire-dogs the first week
of the wintering, and every evening after supper he mended the banner.
It needed a winter of it. Sixty floors were written on that cloth in
the only script cloth keeps: hedge-thorn rips from the Fencerows,
drum-fire scorch from the Warcamp, the fine eight-legged snags of the
Broodthrone silk, and all through the weave the grey of the
Cindermouth's ash, which would not full out, and which he had stopped
trying to full out. "Cloth's honest," he said, when Bruna's women
offered him new bunting. "It shows what it's been through. That's most
of what it's for." He sewed one-handed, the pole steadied under his
boot, the needle slow and exact, and it was Vega's place, fallen to
her by the same wordless bargain as the wedding-iron, to sit by him
with the thread and keep his needles fed. A giantess threading needles
draws comment the first evening and none after. Her hands had set
rivets finer than any needle's eye. They talked, or they did not. He
was the restfullest man she had ever kept a fire with.

And each week, a bench nearer, came Hale.

He had wintered at the hall's far cold end at first, out past the
dogs, grey cloak and grey face, mending the yard's practice-blades
that no one had asked him to mend, running a whetstone down nicked
edges by ear in the half-dark. Then one week he was mending them six
benches in. Then four. He never joined the settle's talk; he corrected
a young thane's shield-grip once with two fingers, the way you'd
straighten a picture in passing, and was gone before the boy turned.
Vega watched the whole slow transit across a winter's worth of
evenings and entered it in the flat ledger under the only figure it
fit: cold iron coming back to temper. You cannot hurry it. You can
only keep the fire lit and the door unbarred, and let the metal make
up its own mind about the distance. By the last month he sat at the
bench nearest the kitchens, in the traffic of the cooks' boys, who
stepped around him the way you step around a post you trust, and if
anyone had asked her — no one asked her — she would have said the
serjeant was becoming, at his own pace, by his own stair, a man who
would be all right.

Ulfar came to the smithy in the last month, too, on an evening she had
not sent for him, with his sleeves rolled, and did not mention
engines, or votes, or the hall. "Cast with me," he said. "It has been
twenty years, and you still shortcut your sand." They built the molds
together past midnight, brother and sister, four hands in one box the
way it had been when the dams they built were knee-high and the river
was their mother's slack-tub, and they poured hinges for the
Undercroft doors, and argued about the gating, and were happy, and did
not say so, being Coalbrows. The hinges hang there yet. They carry
well.

On the last day of the deep winter the parliament signed its charter —
the hall's bell went at noon, and the yard heard the roar through
three walls and grinned at itself and went back to work, which is a
town's way of celebrating a treaty. The feast was set for three nights
on. Bruna's kitchens went to war. The goblin cooks swore an oath about
a boar. Berrin came down the smithy row that evening with his lists,
walking backward against the wind, and begged of her one great
fire-crane for the main hearth by feast-night, and she forged it in a
day and hung it herself, and he stood under it beaming like a man
handed the keys of a larger kitchen, and the cooks cheered her out the
door.

She banked her forge the night before the feast the way she banked it
every night that winter: coals raked to the heart, ash drawn over,
everything kept that could be kept. In the yard the snow came down
soft through the light from the hall — every door of the Frosthall
stood open, gold on the blue drifts, eleven years of darkness being
paid back at the jarl's own order — and from the doors came the sound
of trestles going up, and Bruna's singers at their rehearsal, and
somewhere under it the small ring of a whetstone, evening-steady, from
the bench nearest the kitchens.

Vega stood in the snow a while, warming her hands at the whole of it.

An army, her mother used to say, is a fire in a field: it eats and
moves on, and the field remembers it a year, and then the field
forgets. But a town — a town is a hearth. A hearth is a fire that has
been *kept*: banked at night, apologized to, fed slow, built where
walls could learn to hold its heat. The rising had come up sixty
floors as a fire in a field. Somewhere in the wedding-iron and the
grave-tokens and the lead-strips and the sledge-post's bells, on one
white country halfway up the sky, it had become the other thing.

She went in through the smithy-yard door, out of the cold. Behind her
the hall stood open on the winter, all its lamps lit, all its doors
wide, warm gold clean out to the frost-birches — a hearth the size of
a country, banked and breathing, keeping its heat for everyone the
world could send.
