# Chapter 39 — The Welded

The seam split at dawn, if the Made had dawns, and Ede Stonhal led the
rising into the Welding Halls, and after that nobody spoke above a
murmur for a day and a night, and it was not an order. No order was
needed. Some floors give their own orders.

The halls were lit blue-white, arc-light stuttering off black iron so
that everything moved even when nothing did. The air tasted of flux. Under
the flux was the other smell, the one that had come down through the
welded seam and silenced the muster on the cooled roads below, and Ede
made herself stop naming it, because naming it did not help and she would
need her wits.

Rows of rigs ran away into the dark on either hand, arc-arms hanging
slack. Between the rigs stood the racks.

She had been told what the racks held. Sef Seamside had come down through
the sap-ways two nights before — a weld-captive, thin as a file, with the
un-welding lore scratched on slate and lead in a bundle under one arm —
and had told the war-council in a voice worn perfectly flat what the floor
above them was for. This is where they take a captured soul and weld it
into a soldier, seam by seam, iron over flesh, until what is left stands
up and takes orders. This is where imps are made. This is where
hellknights are made. Every welded thing you have fought since the First
Outwork was made in these halls, and every one of them was somebody,
dragged back up from a floor you freed.

The council had heard it in silence. There was nothing to ask. There was
only the one thing to do, and the plan for doing it: take the Overseer's
Gantry, and run the rigs backward.

Now Ede walked the aisle between the racks with her people fanning quiet
behind her, and the telling, which had been terrible, turned out to have
been kind. The fresh-welded hung cooling in their rows, rank on rank of
them, iron half-closed over faces, and some were still, and some were
setting, and some — she did not let herself look away; a general who
orders a thing looked at must look at it — some were awake inside the
seams, and screaming, and no sound came out, because the mouths had been
finished first.

Beside her, Sliver spoke against her palm, and for once the old splinter's
dryness had nothing in it but ash. *I remember when this craft was for
mending hulls,* it said. *I carried the smith who invented the seam. I am
glad she is dead, and I have never once been glad of that before.*

"Gantry," Ede said quietly, and the word went back down the line, and the
rising went to work.

⁂

The battle for the Welding Halls was short, and grim, and won almost
gently, and Ede would remember all her life that the worst floor in the
tower was the one that cost the fewest lives to take. The Made had not
garrisoned it heavily. It had never needed to. Its horror was its
garrison.

The seam-checker imps came at them down the rack-rows, probe-fisted,
welding shut whatever they caught, and Nix's collar-breakers met them
between the rigs and freed eleven of thirteen, and the two that could not
be reached in time died the deaths that should not have been needed, and
were laid out properly, with their collars broken after, which was all
anyone could still do for them and was done for every one.

The rejected lot came up out of the Scrap-Sink at the fighting's loudest —
botched welds, souls fused wrong and thrown away, melted into one
shambling grief that dragged the living toward the racks — and there was
no freeing there, no collar, nothing to reach; the mercy was the breaking.
Vega's crews broke it with long-axes and the Unrivet word, weeping while
they worked, the way her people wept, silently and without stopping the
work, and when it came apart the scrapped souls in it were freed to rest,
and the halls were quieter by one sound Ede had not known she was hearing
until it stopped.

Seamwright held the gantry. The master engine itself: a Warden grown out
of the welding-rigs, arc-arms and clamp and cruel will, and it fought by
making soldiers — reached into the reserve-racks and sent its own
half-made against them, and reached for the living too, to weld them
standing. It caught a Frosthall spear-thane by the arm and had the plate
half-laid before three crews together drove it back off him with the new
word, the word Sef had carried down the sap-ways in a bundle, the word the
whole floor had been building toward since the first collar broke at the
Red Warcamp.

*Unseam.*

They said it against the Warden until its own work ran backward, seams
opening as fast as its arms could lay them, and then Ulfar Coalbrow put a
forge-maul the length of a roof-beam through its gantry-heart, and the arc
died all down the halls at once, every rig together, and the eviction took
what had worn the engine and dragged it down out of the world, and the
Welding Halls stopped. For the first time since the tower was raised, they
simply stopped.

In the ringing dark, Sef Seamside climbed the Overseer's Gantry alone,
and stood at the master-board a moment with her hands flat on it, and
then ran the rigs the other way.

⁂

The un-making took three days, and Ede stayed for all of it.

She had learned this about herself at the Red Warcamp cages and never
since found reason to doubt it: there is a kind of work a commander cannot
delegate, not because others would do it worse but because ordering it
done and not watching is a coin the soul does not have. So she stood at
the foot of the Cooling Racks while the crews and the craft brought the
fresh-welded down one at a time, and Sef's people found the seams, and
the word was said, and the iron opened.

The morning after the gantry fell, the veil went — untended, the grey
false day thinning and tearing — and down through the high smoke-vents
came the grey-silver of an honest day, lying where only arc-light had
ever lain. Work stopped, one breath, every hand; nobody
needed it named. Then the word was said again, and the iron went on
opening, in daylight.

Not all of it opened. That was the first cruelty of the mending: Unseam
split what had not set. What had set, stayed. A woman came off the racks
with her hands her own again and her back iron forever. A boy — he had
been taken young; the tower took what the press-gangs caught — walked
free with one arm ending at a welded gauntlet no craft could unmake
without unmaking the arm inside it. The healers and the smiths worked side
by side at the racks' foot, one trade now, and Vega moved among them
saying the thing she said, that anything broken can be mended, and Ede
listened to her say it and watched the seam-scarred file past and thought:
mended is not unbroken. Nobody knew that better than Vega. She said it
anyway. Perhaps that was what the word was for.

They came off the racks and they gave their names, and the clerks wrote
them, and Ede stood with her muster-roll under her arm — the rebuilt roll,
carried since the Cindermouth, the habit of it worn into her like a
sword-hilt — and heard the names of six floors she had freed read back to
her by people who had been dragged out of those floors after her army had
marched on and up. Freed at the Frozen Sea, taken again on the Jarl's
Road. Freed at the Frosthall, taken at the feast. The sale's ledger ran in
both directions, and the rising had spent two years reading only one of
them.

On the second day, they brought down the deep rows, the ones welded
farthest back, and a soldier came off the rack who did not wait for the
clerks.

He was iron from the jaw down. The seams on him had set years past —
plate gone dull, rivets rusted at their crowns, a hellknight's finished
body — but the last weld, the one over the will, had been laid fresh; the
tower re-welded its old stock before sending it up to the Herald's muster,
Sef said, the way a cooper re-hoops an old barrel. So the will-seam split.
Everything below the jaw stayed. He came down off the rack on iron legs
that knew how to march, and above the iron mouth were a man's two eyes,
and the eyes found Ede across the racks' foot and stopped, and then his
whole body did the thing his body remembered before his mind can have
caught it up.

He came to attention. Heels together. Shoulders back. Shield-side arm
crooked to carry what was not there.

Ede knew the stance before she knew the face. Third file, shield-side,
the anchor of the push — you built a shield-wall around men who stood like
that. And then the face, above the iron: older, ruined, the same. Wat
Kello. Five years on the Stone of the Climb in Roothollow, the fifth
name from the top of the thirty, cut there by her own hand with the chisel
skipping because she had still been shaking in those years, and she had
smoothed the skip with her thumb and left the name crooked. Wat Kello,
who had held the third file at the Red Warcamp while the trap closed,
whose body had never been found among the caged nor among the dead, and
now she knew why, and the knowing was a whole war's weight arriving at
once. Not caged. Not killed. *Sold on.* Passed up the tower like goods,
held in the dark for five years — the Silk Vaults, the Trophy tiers,
whatever ledger-line he had been entered under — and fed at the last to
these racks, to be finished into the enemy and sent down against his own.

The cages had been the sale's first price. Here stood the full price,
at attention, waiting on her order.

"Stand easy, Wat," Ede said. Her voice held. She had spent two years
learning to make it hold. "Stand easy. You're stood down. You've been
stood down five years, soldier. It's done."

The iron body eased, joint by joint, as if easing were a drill it was
performing from memory. Somebody had run for the banner — it went by
turns among the freed of Shieldwall since Dun Harrow fell, and it was
Ede's whole reserve of luck that it was near — and they brought it up
the aisle, and Wat Kello looked at it a long moment, the old grey cloth,
the wall-and-star gone thread-bare at the fly. He put out one gauntleted
hand and took a fold of it between iron fingers with a delicacy that
undid half the room, and the eyes above the iron mouth shone and spilled,
and the mouth itself could do nothing; it was the tower's mouth; the
tower had made it and made it badly, for the tower had never needed its
soldiers to do this.

Then he worked the mouth anyway. It moved like a rusted hinge, and the
voice that came out of it was scraped hollow, a voice off an anvil, one
word wide.

The word was not his name. It was not her name.

"Bread," said Wat Kello.

The racks' foot went still. Ede did not move at all.

"Again," she said, gently.

The hinge worked. "Bread." His free hand rose, shaking, and touched his
own jaw, his nose — *smell*, the gesture said, the man who came smelled —
and Ede stood in the blue-lit halls and was five years and eighty-four
floors away, in a burnt hall on floor sixty hearing a freed prisoner rasp
about a hooded courier at the Warcamp who smelled of Roothollow bread; on
a trophy floor with her own shield on the wall and toll-marks on its rim;
at a watch-fire telling a splinter of crystal the one thing she could
tell no one alive. He was giving testimony. Five years welded shut, and
the first act of his freed will — before his own name, before hers — was
to report to his commander the last thing he had learned in the field.
The man who sold us came to the cages to count us. I marked him. Bread.

"I know," Ede said.

She said it very low, for him alone, and she watched the two human eyes
above the iron read everything her face had spent a year hiding from an
army. She let them read it. Five years he had carried his one word up
through the dark, and he had earned the truth ahead of every living soul
but one: that his commander knew the courier's name, had known it since
the Pale Court, and had climbed beside the man since, and eaten his bread
at every muster, and said nothing, waiting on proof that would hold in
open council — because the courier was beloved, and the accusation would
shatter what the proof must carry whole.

"You're the proof, Wat," she said. "You and a serving-list and a ledger.
Hold my secret five floors more. Then I'll ask you to say your word
again, where it finishes this."

The iron hand closed careful as a jeweler on the fold of the banner. Above
the finished mouth the eyes changed, and it took her a heartbeat to
understand what they were doing, because the mouth could not help them do
it. He was smiling. Third file, shield-side. Give that man a wall to hold
and he would hold it till the hills wore down.

*The price of the sale,* Sliver said against her palm, *is standing in
front of you, and it is still hers to spend. There is nothing in my long
memory crueler than this floor. And nothing braver in it than that man.*

That night Ede sat late with Mistress Calyne over a slate and a
cipher-book, at a shielded lamp in a corner of the halls where the rigs
stood dead, and between them, piece by piece, they laid the trap that had
been two years in the gathering. The serving-list from a Herald's boot.
The toll-ledger's payment, moved up the tower the week of the sale. The
granary dates. And now a witness with an iron mouth and one word in it,
worth all the rest together.

"Before the Herald's Gate," Calyne said. Her grey eyes were the same
temperature they had been the day she arrived at the Broodthrone accusing
everyone and trusted by no one, the coldest and least-loved and most
constant hunter the rising had. "In open council, staged in sight of the
gate, with every banner watching. It must be there. Do you see why?"

Ede saw. Five floors above them, past the warrens and the drill-lines and
the iron chapels, Malgrim's muster-gate stood at the top of the Made like
a keystone, and behind its arch sat the owner of a certain willing collar.
"Because there," she said, "he has nowhere left to run but upward. Into
the arms of the thing he serves. In front of everyone he fooled."

"In front of everyone *I* could not convince," Calyne said, without
bitterness, which was the only way she said anything. She closed the
cipher-book — her own hand, her life's hunt, blood not yet on its margin —
and looked at it a moment as one looks at a tool that has one use left in
it. "Five floors, general. Keep him warm. Keep him baking. Let the whole
rising love him for five more floors."

Above them, in the dark of the racks, the freed of the Welding Halls
slept their first sleep as themselves, and the halls that had made the
King's army lay silent, already being rebuilt by Vega's crews to run
backward forever. And up the long iron miles, five floors and a gate
away, a quartermaster who smelled of warm bread was tallying loaves for
an army he had been selling for twenty years, item by item, to the
patient man behind the drums.

The rising would come to him presently. It was growing as it climbed.
