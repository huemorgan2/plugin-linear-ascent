# Chapter 17 — Calyne

The word came up with the ride-keepers, the way all word came up now:
a grey rider on the Grey Rides, climbing alone.

Ede was at the chasm-mouth camps when the report reached her, standing
on the black moraine above the tier-gate with the first true cold of the
wintering in her teeth. Below, on the floors they had freed, the rising's
road ran like a lit wick — gate-towns waking, wagons climbing, the freed
coming up behind their own liberation in numbers Berrin's clerks could
barely count fast enough. Above, past the boulder-fields and the wheeling
glare hawks, the tier went on into ice. Forty banners now. A nation
moving, and stopping soon to breathe.

"Alone," the ride-keeper said again, as if the word itself needed
guarding. "Aline sighted her at the Wayshrine. Grey cloak, grey horse,
no banner. She rides the middle of the Rides, captain. Not the verge.
The middle, like she owns them." A pause. "The crows follow her. Aline
says they've followed her three floors."

"The Wake," said Rhovan, from behind his cup.

He had come up from the Broodthrone a fortnight past, thin as a
bowstring, smelling of silk under the woodsmoke, with a winter of quiet
folded away behind his eyes and news that had kept the inner council up
three nights running. Now he looked down the tier the way a man looks
at weather coming.

"You know her, then," Ede said.

"Mistress Calyne. My handler." A fraction, offered like coin. Then, when
she only waited: "My examiner, my paymaster, and the nearest thing the
Wake keeps to a blade. She has run its eyes on this side of the tower
since before I was recruited. If she has left her web and come up
herself, she is not carrying a letter." He set the cup down with great
care. "Ede. What I paid at the Broodthrone, I paid from the Wake's
purse. She's coming to collect."

"From you?"

"From the rising. I'm merely the receipt."

She came into the camps on the evening of the second day, and Ede,
watching her dismount in the muster-yard, understood at once why the
crows had followed. Calyne was small, grey-cloaked, past the middle of
some long elvish age, with a face like a folded letter, and she moved
through the crowding, banner-hung, cook-fire chaos of the rising's camp
as if it were an audit she had already completed and found wanting. She
did not look at the banners. She looked at the picket-lines, the wagon
tallies, the sight-lines to the tier-gate. Ede had known one other
person to enter a strange camp that way, and she had buried the man who
taught him, at the mound's edge, by the forms.

The Wake's mistress did not ask for the council. She walked to the
council fire as if she had dug its pit, stood in the light, and said,
without raising her voice: "Rhovan of the Grey Rides. Stand up."

He stood. He had the sense not to speak.

"Item," she said, in a voice like a door being locked. "One founding.
Item: one purpose, whole. Item: the name — the *name* — of the
Leaf-Ledger, in the hearing of a general, a giant, a barrow-queen, a
goblin, and a beast that files what it hears and never dies. Fourscore
years of silence, spent in an afternoon, by a field man, for
*road-toll*." Her eyes went around the fire then, deliberately, and Ede
felt them stop on her and weigh her like freight. "You will want to know
if I have come to kill him. The Wake does not spill what it can still
pour. And you will want to know if I have come to take the Wake's bows
home. General Stonhal, whatever this one has told you of us, we are not
sentimental, and our bows serve where our interest lies."

"And where does your interest lie tonight?" Ede said.

"Here," said Calyne. "That is the misfortune of it. What was spent
cannot be recalled; it can only be *audited*. From tonight the Wake does
not lend the rising its eyes from below. From tonight I am the eyes,
and I sit on this council, and I read what it reads, and I count what
it counts. What I cannot trust, I watch." She unclasped the grey cloak,
folded it once, and sat down at the fire in the manner of a woman who
had been a member for years and merely stepped out. "Proceed."

Around the ring, Ede's captains looked at one another. It was Berrin who
recovered first — Berrin, who could make a welcome out of two crusts and
a bench-end — already signalling a cook for a bowl, already shifting
down to make room the way he had for every stray the road had sent
them. "There's barley and there's what the clerks are calling mutton,"
he said comfortably. "Eat first, audit after. Nobody counts honestly on
an empty stomach."

"I count honestly on every stomach," Calyne said, and took the bowl, and
did not thank him, and Ede watched her not do it, and could not have
said why the small cold of that stayed with her the rest of the night.

⁂

It was Nix who said it out loud first, because it was always Nix who
said the things everyone else was keeping in.

"Right, so, the counting," he said, three days on, in the small hours,
by the wall where the wintering rosters were pinned. "One: she writes in
cipher. All the time. In council she writes, boss — you're talking, Vega's
talking, and scratch scratch scratch goes the little grey book, and I
looked over her shoulder, on account of being short enough that it isn't
rude, and it's not letters, it's *lace*. Two: she goes out at night. Not
sometimes-nights. Every-nights. Pickets seen her by the water-side going
down, and back before dawn with peat on her boots — that's down-floor
peat, boss, we're on gravel up here, I checked, I stole a boot. Three:
she asked Harrow's clerk for the muster-roll. The whole roll. Names,
floors, who came up when. Four: she asked which wagons carry the mail.
Five" — he had run out of fingers on one hand and looked at it with
disapproval — "five, she doesn't drink, and I don't hold it against a
body, but it does keep the memory sharp, doesn't it, for a person taking
an interest in everyone's comings and goings."

"She's a spymaster," Ede said. "You've described a spymaster doing her
trade in the open."

"That's just it, boss. Openly's the strange part." Nix hugged the
standard closer; he had taken to sleeping with it since the Broodthrone,
and nobody teased him. "When I was cup-bearer, the ones as skimmed the
cup always poured with both sleeves rolled. Look, nothing hid. It's the
rolled sleeves I don't trust."

Ede did not trust them either, and hated that she didn't, and went
looking for reasons the way you probe a tooth. They were not hard to
find; that was the trouble. Calyne's little grey cipher-book never left
her body. Her night walks went down the freed stairs, always alone,
always unlogged, and when Ede set a quiet watch on the tier-gate, the
watch reported the Wake's mistress had gone down by the water-stair
instead, as if she had counted Ede's pickets too. She would not say
where she went. She would not say where she had been the week the
Warcamp fell, either, when Ede asked her plainly across the ledger-table
— she had folded her hands and said, "Below," with the whole insolent
economy of her people, and Ede, who had watched Rhovan spend a year
telling the truth one coin at a time, knew a guarded purse when it was
shut in her face.

And she was Roothollow-born. That surfaced by accident, at the fire,
when Mereth Wakesend remarked on the grey lace-cipher and Calyne said,
without looking up, that she had learned her first letters chalking
tallies on the Stone of the Climb like every Roothollow child. Ede had
kept her face still. She had needed practice at that lately, and was
getting it. Roothollow. The word had not stopped ringing since a freed
prisoner in the Warcamp's wreckage had said *the courier smelled of
Roothollow bread* — since the whole hunt had turned toward home like a
weathervane. Berrin, who missed nothing at a fire, had only nodded
along: he'd known her people a little, he said; ropewalk folk, off the
market square; she'd left young. "Before my time on the granary books,"
he added, apologetic, "so don't ask me the year," and passed the pot,
and the talk had gone on, warm, ordinary, and Ede had sat in it hearing
nothing but the crackle of her own suspicion.

Then came the night the inner council heard the Broodthrone's second
story, and everything got worse.

They were six around the map-table in the command tent: Ede, Vega,
Rhovan, Dun Harrow, Nix, and the Wake's mistress, self-seated,
scratching lace. Rhovan told it the way he had told Ede — the engines
under the world, the underside of a stolen country, and then the
courier. The toll-marked silver. The hood. The tread that had crossed
the Matriarch's web twice, once going up, and once, four seasons later,
coming down. The only soul in the age of the tower to walk the road
both ways.

The scratching had stopped.

Ede watched Calyne without watching her — an old skill from card-tables
in a worse life — and what she saw she could not afterward unsee. The
Wake's mistress had gone still. Not surprised-still; *checked*-still,
the stillness of an abacus mid-sum, every bead in the air. When she
finally spoke there was something new under the grey of her voice, and
it took Ede a heartbeat to name it, because it had no business there.

It sounded like hunger.

"The tread," Calyne said. "She kept it. Twenty years, and she could
name it again — you are certain? Those were her words? If that
creature's web were brought a *step* to compare — if a man could be
walked across silk under her lamps — she could say *this one* or *not
this one*?"

"She keeps what she keeps," Rhovan said. "She would know him among ten
thousand. She said so, and she does not overstate; overstatement voids
warranties."

"Then the Broodthrone is not a floor," the spymaster said, half to
herself, writing again, fast now, the lace running off the pen in long
grey loops. "It is a *witness*." And she asked question after question —
the courier's years, the seasons between his passings, which Tollmen's
marks were on the silver, whether the Matriarch had smelled bread on
him — no, Ede realized, listening with her skin: whether anyone had
*asked* her about bread; who else knew this story; who had been in the
tent each time it was told — and every question was the question of
someone measuring a net, and Ede sat there in the lamplight unable to
tell, for her life, for her army's life, whether the net was being
measured for casting or for slipping.

*"You are grinding your teeth,"* Sliver observed, from her palm, much
later, when the tent was empty and the lamp was guttering out its last.

"She fits, Sliver. Every seam of her. The ciphers, the nights, the
questions. She was born in Roothollow and she won't say where she's
been, and the one story that should have frightened a loyal spy to
death lit her up like a lamp instead."

*"I have carried forty-one climbers,"* the splinter said, *"and I will
tell you the only thing four ages taught me about hunting hidden things:
the creature you can see circling the trap is rarely the one that has
been eating from it. Rarely,"* it added, dry as old paper, *"is not
never. I make no defense of the grey woman. I merely decline to make
the accusation while the ground is dark. So should you."*

"Then what do I do with her?"

*"What the Barrow King did with grief, and Vyx with an army she could
not afford to fight,"* said Sliver. *"You cannot expel her; the Wake's
bows hold half your left flank. You cannot try her; you have lace and
peat and a birthplace. So keep her where the accounting is. Seat her
close. Read what she reads. And Ede — count her nights, but count
everyone's. A camp that watches one back only is a camp with its own
back bare."*

⁂

So the false winter's-peace settled over the chasm-mouth camps: forty
banners under the black moraine, the cook-fire smoke standing straight
up in the cold, and at the council fire each evening, in the seat she
had taken and no one had dared take back, a grey woman writing lace,
watching everything, owed by nobody, trusted by nobody, going nowhere.

On the last night before the freeze closed the high road, word came up
from the tier below with Berrin's wagons: Vyx's court had stood aside at
the Broodthrone's stair for the season's last trains, correct to the
clause, and the Matriarch had sent the rising a gift outside all
contract — a bolt of websteel thread, enough to string every bow in the
host, with a line in a clerk's fair hand: *For the party of the first
part. A kept promise warms better than fires. Inquire of your hostage.*
Ede read it twice and put it away with the other things she did not
understand yet and would.

Then she called the captains and gave the wintering order the whole camp
had been waiting for.

"We stop climbing at the freeze," she said. "We winter here, at the
chasm-mouth, and while the ice holds the high road shut, we do the thing
this army has never once had the breath to do." She looked down the
fire, at Vega and Rhovan and Dun with the banner, at Nix asleep against
the standard-pole, at the grey woman writing, and past them all at the
lit wick of the road running down through forty freed floors into the
dark. "We *count*. Every banner. Every people. Every name, every loaf,
every arrow, every soul this rising has carried up out of the fever —
we set it all in one book, and come the thaw we will know for the first
time what we are.

"Nine peoples and forty banners," said Ede Stonhal, and the cold
took her breath and made it a standard over the fire. "Come the
wintering, we find out how big a world we've stolen back."

Across the fire, without looking up, Mistress Calyne turned to a fresh
page.
