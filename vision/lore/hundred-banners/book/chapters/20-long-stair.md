# Chapter 20 — The Long Stair

There were one hundred and eleven bridges in the Hanging City, or one
hundred and nine if you did not count the two that were only a single rope
with ideas above its station, and Nix had walked every one of them twice
before the army was allowed to cross. That was the arrangement he had made
with himself. He was the lightest thing in the rising that could carry a
lantern and an opinion at the same time, and so he went first, and counted,
and came back, and the counting was for the army and the coming back was
for him.

The city hung over a chasm that had no bottom anyone had ever heard hit.
Its streets were cables. Its plazas were woven silk, wide as threshing
floors, swaying a little always, like a ship that had forgotten the sea.
Lamps hung out in the dark on long lines, so that standing at the chasm's
lip you looked out at a town of low stars and no ground under any of them.
The Deep-folk had built it with their spider partners, back when the
spiders were partners, and the fever had ended the partnership the way the
fever ended everything — by making the builders eat the building.

Fenn Skeinside met the vanguard at the first anchor-post with one hand on
a line, which Nix would learn was where the man's hand always was. He was
a city-warden with rope-burn scars laid over rope-burn scars, and he
talked the way his bridges hung: no slack anywhere.

"Your army crosses in file, on the lines I say, in the order I say," he
said. "The silk holds soldiers. It does not hold argument. Leave the
argument on this side."

They left the argument on that side. Vega's forge-crews wanted to sling
the wagons on doubled cables and Fenn allowed it, after walking each
splice himself. His people taught the Threadwalk to the few who could
learn it, elves mostly, and to Nix, who learned it in an afternoon and
was insufferable about it for a week. For everyone else there was the
vertigo-draught, mixed in Skeinside by the barrel, tasting of cold
stone and courage. You drank it and the drop under your boots became a
fact instead of a voice.

The crossing took four days. The spiders came drifting on their balloon-
silk out of the black, sails up, knives of legs, and were cured one by one
by the archers on the anchor-towers — each great wrong shape collapsing to
an ordinary spinner that reeled in its sail and anchored to the wall,
harmless, done. The husks were worse. Silk-bound
figures walking the cables behind the living, cutting lines with no more
malice than scissors. Broken, they fell, and the falling drained out of
them before they landed, and what landed in the great net of the
Undercatch was only wrapping.

On the third day a drover from Hedgeharbor stepped from a platform to a
bridge exactly the way he had done two hundred times, and a cut line that
had held all morning chose that heartbeat to give. He did not scream. Nix
was near enough to see him go and not near enough to do anything but
count him. The Undercatch took him, and the Undercatch was old, and there
was a rent in it no wider than a door.

His name had been Tam Yoler. Nix learned it afterward, on purpose. He had
a cord he kept under his shirt, a plain tally-cord, and that evening he
put the first knot of the high floors in it, and told nobody, because it
was not for anybody.

The city gave as well as took — everything did, that was the horrible
arithmetic of the climb. Fenn's cable-walkers went down into the
Undercatch with lamps and came up with eleven living souls who had fallen
in the fevered years and survived on dropped cargo and net-caught rain,
including a woman who had been down there so long she asked, climbing
out, whether the lamps were still lit in the Gossamer Market, and wept
when they raised the market that same night for the answer.

After the city, the stair.

That was what the army came to call the nine floors of that season — the
Long Stair — though it was lifts and gates like any other stretch of the
Ascent. It felt like a stair. It felt like climbing a thing that had
noticed you.

The Cloudline was ridges over white nothing, and the squall-drakes came
sideways out of the weather; a kite of Voss's supply-train went off the
edge with two of its handlers, and the cliff-goats watched them fall with
yellow uninterested eyes. The Ember Dunes cooked the host by day and
froze it by night, and the dune-adders took three sleepers before anyone
thought to sift the sand around the bedrolls. The Wreckfields were a
forest of dead sky-ships where the salvage-folk of Hullside showed them
which hulls were sound to camp in and which ones breathed. On the Troll
Bridges the army crossed crevasse after crevasse on packed snow, and on
the second day a span that had carried two thousand soldiers let go
under a string of nine mules and the farrier leading them, and the
raven-haunted gorge did not even echo.

Nine knots. Nix retied the cord twice to be sure.

That was the thing his hands understood before his head did. The rising
had fought since the Fencerows — wolves, Wardens, Cullers, wights, its
own quarrels — and every loss had an enemy attached to it, something with
a face, something you could cure or evict or, worst and truest, kill. On
the Long Stair the army bled and there was nothing to hit. A rope. A
sandbank. A snow-bridge. The tower was done sending soldiers for a while.
The tower had remembered that it was itself a weapon, a hundred countries
of edge and drop and cold welded into one tall trap, and it had all the
patience in the world.

Rest came strangely, where it came. The Rookeries deafened them with
fledglings and stole their buttons. On the Shade Walks the lamplit
avenues were quiet and wrong, and everyone's shadow was advised in
writing not to be trusted, and one poor watchman of the rearguard
followed his own shadow around a corner and was not found. Then the Giant
Steadings, and that floor was kindness: freehold farms sized for giants,
fences a wagon could drive under without ducking, and the freeholders of
Thanesrest putting up the whole footsore host in barns the size of
market-squares. Vega walked among her own folk for three days with her
shoulders down for the first time since the Frozen Sea. The freeholders
sent forty scythes up with the army when it moved. Nix counted them too.
The cord was for the dead but the counting was for everything; counting
was how he held the world still enough to look at.

Then the lift opened on the fifty-ninth floor, and the world went flat
and grey and silent, and every hair Nix owned stood up and pointed back
the way he had come.

The Trapdoor Fields were nothing. That was the first thing you saw: a
cavern-plain floored in fine grey dust, dead level, dead empty, running
out past lantern-reach in every direction, quiet as a held breath. The
second thing you saw was Wend Latchlight's face, and then you knew the
nothing was a lie.

"The whole floor's a lid," she told the war-council, up on the Watch-
Mound, the one honest rise in all that flatness. She was a small hard
woman with a delver's lamp-squint, and she had scars on her forearms in
neat rows, like tally-marks, which Nix respected and did not mention.
"Every third step is a door, and under every door is a mouth. The lids
are hinged spring-iron and dust-lichen; you will not see them. I have
maps of the proven lines, and the lids *move*, so the maps are wrong
somewhere, and I don't know where. That's the truth of it. Walk where I
say, exactly, one behind the other, and be quiet doing it. Sound brings
them up."

Ede asked how an army of nine thousand crosses a floor in single file,
in silence.

"Slowly," said Wend.

They made the rules that night and the rules were these: one line, one
pace, three arms' lengths between souls. No voice. Orders by hand-sign,
passed back rank to rank like a bucket at a fire. Wagons unloaded, the
loads carried, the wagons drawn on skids by hand — hooves drum, and
drumming brings them up. The Surestep draught to the point-walkers and
the flank-wards, because there was not enough for nine thousand. And at
the head of the whole column, in front of the vanguard, in front of Ede
herself, one goblin with a delver's pole, because the lids were sprung
by weight, and Nix weighed less than the army's kettles.

He did not volunteer. He wanted that understood by history. Wend
Latchlight looked over the war-council, keepers and captains and one
giant, and her eye came down on Nix like a lid, and she said, "Him," and
that was the whole argument. You cannot volunteer for a thing you were
always going to be given.

The crossing took three days, and Nix remembered it afterward the way
you remember a fever — in pieces, all of them too bright.

The pole, tapping. Two taps for a true step, laid soft as a moth. The
dust taking his footprints and keeping them. Wend a spear-length behind
him, reading the ground over his shoulder, her hand-signs small and
calm: *left, left again, hold.* Behind her the army, two miles of the
gathered world — men, elves, giants, delvers, freed kobolds, Tollmen,
Cullers, thanes — walking one behind the other in a silence so complete
he could hear the lamp-oil move. A hundred banners furled and carried
flat, because a banner snaps, and snapping brings them up.

Once an hour, somewhere out on the plain, a lid would rise of itself —
a hand's width, to look — and settle again, soft, patient,
like a man tucking a letter back in a drawer. Nobody had to pass a sign
for that. The whole column felt it through its boot-soles and kept
walking.

On the second day, in the middle of the middle mile, the floor ate Mole.

Mole was a kobold of the Broodwarrens, freed at the surrender Nix had
talked into being, one of the ones Nix had taught to say a name again in
the winter camps below the Hanging City — had taught, and then watched
him pick a name for himself, which is a thing you never forget watching.
He had picked Mole because he liked digging and was honest about his
eyes. He carried kettle-loads without complaint and sang under his
breath when he forgot the rules, and he was seven hundred and forty
places back in the column when a lid that was on no map breathed open
under his left foot and took him to the hip, and the rules held, that
was the horror of it, the rules *held* — the soldiers either side of him
had him by the arms in a heartbeat, and the sign came sprinting up the
column hand to hand, *sprung trap, seven hundred, hold the line* — and
Nix stood at the head of the world's army with his pole in the dust,
forbidden to run, forbidden to shout, doing the only thing he had, which
was counting, while a half-mile behind him a thing under a lid was
stronger than four men and then stronger than six, and then the sign
that came up the column changed, and the line walked on.

They did not even have him to carry. That was what Nix could not put
anywhere. The Warcamp had left bodies; the Barrows had left bodies; you
carried them out and named them at the mound and the naming closed over
the wound like water. The Trapdoor Fields left a closed lid in a flat
plain, one among ten thousand, already re-dusted, already a lie again.

Four more knots went into the cord on that floor. A flank-ward who
stepped one stride off the line to steady a skidding wagon. Two carriers
at the dust-funnels, where the ground was honest about wanting them and
swallowed slow, and the ropes came up empty. A Culler of the rearguard
whose name Nix had to ask three people for, and the third one wept, so
Nix carried the name and the weeping both.

At the King-Burrow they finally had a thing to fight, and it was almost
a relief, and Nix hated that it was a relief. They gave Latchjaw a
wagon of quarry-stone rolled down-slope on skids, and the great lid at
the plain's heart threw itself open to take the gift, and the trick of
it was done — out of its throat and into the open, where a thing built
of hinge and hunger met the rising's bows, and Vega's hammer, and the
end it had been owed since the fever made it. The lids fell still, all
of them at once, edge to edge across the plain, like a held breath let
go. Out of the king-burrow's throat the delvers brought up nineteen
packs, six lid-maps that were not Wend's, and one living climber of
some company nobody knew, who had been down there in the dark keeping
himself by counting, and when they lit a lamp near his face he asked
them please to say the number he had reached, because he had lost it,
and Nix — who understood completely — told him a good one.

At the far wall the stair-lift stood open, and beyond the gate the air
came down cold and clean, and there was light in it.

Nix went up with the vanguard and stood in the snow of a high country
under carved eaves, and looked across a frozen yard at the biggest
building he had ever seen that wasn't a floor — a mead-hall like a
timber mountain, every window bleeding warm gold onto the snow, its
doors thrown wide, and out of the doors, rolling down across the yard
like something spilled, came firelight, and the smell of roasting, and
a skald's song that never stopped, and the sound of a hundred voices
raised in welcome.

Sixty floors of the Ascent had taught Nix exactly one lesson without
any exceptions in it, and the lesson was: nothing on a Warden's floor
is glad to see you.

He stood in the snow with his cord under his shirt — thirty-one knots
now between the chasm and the dust, thirty-one, and not one of them
taken by a blade, not one of them anybody's kill to answer for; the
tower itself had done its own work this season, and would again — and
he listened to the warmest sound in the world pour out of the
Frosthall's open door, and he counted the doors, and the shadows on
the gold, and his own slow steps forward, because counting was how you
held a thing still enough to see what it really was.

"Well," he said quietly, to the standard on his back, to the cord, to
nobody. "Either it's supper, or it's us."

It was both. But that is the next part of the story.
