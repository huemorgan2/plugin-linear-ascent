# Chapter 45 — The Stolen Years

The stair out of the reactor country climbed for half a day and came out
nowhere.

Ede had the tower's grammar by heart now, the way you learn a language
you are marched through: a stair ends at a gate, a gate gives onto a
country, a country has a sky. This stair ended on a landing of riveted
iron broader than Roothollow's whole market, and past the landing there
was no sky and no country — only the dark going up and the dark going
out, rib after colossal rib of anchor-iron dwindling toward a vanishing
point the lanterns could not buy back. Not a floor. The space between
two floors. They had climbed out of the world's rooms and into its
timbers.

*Between the heart and the King's waiting-rooms,* Sliver said against
her breastbone, low, as it had spoken ever since the scram. *Every great
house has such a space. The builders ran the binding-seams through here,
and the counterweights, and the warm water off the core. You are inside
the wall, general.*

"How far across?"

*Four days, at a host's pace. There is a road; there has always been a
road.* A pause, dry as a shut book. *Hood the lanterns when you find it.
You will see why.*

The quiet came with them. It had come with them since Ulfar's hand on
the bar — the great new silence of a tower with its heart stopped, no
hum in the iron, no voice under the world, every collar cold — and in
the galleries of the between it was so whole that the host made all the
sound there was: boot-leather, cart-axles, the litters of the burned
crews creaking, the hundred and forty saved out of the Fuel Pens
breathing carefully in their wagons. An army is never quiet. This one
came near it. No one ordered the hush; the place collected it, the way
a church does, or a sickroom.

On the second watch of the first day, far down the run of the road, Ede
saw green light.

Not the Yoke's dead white. Not lamp-gold. Green — the low green of a
summer evening under leaves, standing in one patch of the dark like a
lit window in a dead town; and beyond it another light, smaller,
grey-blue, the color of snow falling at dusk; and a third, warm as an
open door. The vanguard stopped without an order being given. Ede
walked out ahead of the column, and the smell reached her before the
understanding did, across four hundred paces of dead iron.

Cut grass. Blossom. Rain on warm ground.

"Boss," Nix said, very softly. "I can hear bees."

The tenders called them the Years.

Their keeper came out to the road, as keepers had come out to roads the
whole height of the world: a small dry woman with soil in the creases
of her hands and a dead collar at her throat. She waited while a
collar-breaker said the word over the cold iron; she caught it as it
fell, looked at it the way you look at a tooth that has ached for
years, put it in her apron, and got on with her office.

"Pell," she said. "Of the Years. You'll be the rising; we felt your work
below." She turned her face down the road, to the lights standing one
after another in the dark, out to where the ribs of the world swallowed
them. "Come and see what the tower kept."

The road ran between garths. That was the tenders' word, and it was the
right one: garden-closes under long vaults of glass and iron, each a
field or two across, each holding its own light, its own weather, its
own hour — and inside the glass, growing, a piece of the old world's
last night.

"When he took the countries," Pell said, walking, "he took them living,
and living things are mid-task. You don't steal a farm the way you steal
a ring. There was hay half-cut, blossom half-set, dough proving in a
hundred thousand crocks. The countries went up and were made floors, and
the floors kept their weather — you'll have marched through it; the
sixtieth is always snow — but a floor's weather is a wheel stopped.
Nothing has finished up there in eighty years." She set her hand on a
rail of black iron before the first great garth. "The binders cut one
close out of every country the night it rose. The year itself,
mid-breath. They bedded them down here in the warm off the heart, under
glass, and bound them to burn slow. Eighty garths; one for every stolen
realm. My folk have tended them mother to daughter since the Theft. Fed
them, trimmed them, kept them —" and here the dry voice found the word
it had plainly used all its life, without ceremony — "unfinished."

Beyond the rail stood an orchard in flower.

It was night everywhere in the between and would be night there forever
now, but the orchard hung in its own early morning: a soft light with no
lamp in it anywhere, dew standing grey on the grass, twelve apple trees
in first blossom, and bees — real bees, old-world bees, an age old and
one morning old — working the rows as they had worked them for eighty
years, the same morning, over and over, never once reaching noon.

Ede stood at the rail a long time. Behind her she could hear the hush
arriving as each rank came level with the glass, like a wave running
backward.

"He never came," she said at last. It was not quite a question. She had
stood in the trophy-mind of this tower before.

"Never once." Pell looked up at her, and the look had eighty years of
mothers in it. "He kept them because they were his. He never needed to
see a thing to own it."

The Wyrm had said it of him, a hundred floors ago: *he built the one
tower tall enough to see the whole world from, and he has never once
looked.* Ede had not expected to find, one floor under his door, the
garden of everything he never looked at.

"You should know what your lever did," Pell said then, with no blame in
it at all, which was worse and better at once. "The hold on the garths
was the heart's hold, and the heart is out. The beds are cooling. The
years are off the leash, general, and they're finishing. Eldest first."

"Finishing," Ede said.

"Running." Pell spread her earth-dark hands, a woman laying down a
burden and showing it to you at the same time. "A year that has stood
still eighty years spends itself quick, like held breath let go. That
orchard has a night or two left in its morning. Then it'll run — noon,
fruit, leaf-fall, all of it, a year in a night — and go dark, done."

"Can they be kept?" Vega asked it from the road, low, and Ede heard in
the asking all the mender in her.

"They were never kept, smith. They were stopped." The old woman said it
gently, keeper-fashion, one hard true thing at a time. "Eighty years my
family kept the world's last day from ever getting to its evening. Now
it gets its evening. We're not grieving down here. We're sitting up with
them. You'll know the difference by the far gate."

The crossing took the four days Sliver had promised, and the host made
it in silence, and no one ordered that either.

The lanterns went hooded, because the garths were the light: eighty
small skies standing in the dark of the between, each burning the hour
of its own stolen night, so that the road ran from a midsummer evening
into falling snow, and out of the snow into warm dark rain, and the
army of the hundred banners walked it the way you walk the aisle of a
sickhouse where every bed holds someone's mother. Tenders moved among
the glass with baskets and ladders, sitting up with the Years. The host
watered its oxen at their cisterns and lent them its surgeons, and
nothing else changed hands the whole width of that country except
quiet.

Ede walked it as she had walked every floor, up and down her own
column, and saw the garths take her army apart gently, one soldier at a
time.

She saw the giants of the Frosthall's muster stop before a close of
deep midwinter — a steading corner, a sledge half-loaded, a doorway
hung with juniper for a feast-night eighty years deferred — and stand
in a row with their helms off, snow falling on the other side of the
glass, not one flake of it ever reaching them.

She saw Rhovan stand a long while at a garth of night rain: one acre of
sunken lane and hazel, warm rain falling straight down onto leaf-mold,
eighty years of one shower that never wet the road twice. She did not
ask an elf what he was doing in the rain. When he came away his face
was the same as ever. His hair was not, and he let it dry as it liked.

She saw Nix at the first-taken's garth, and that one she stopped for.

The goblin warrens had gone up before every realm in the world, the
foundation-stones of the whole theft, and their garth was the eldest and
nearest spent: a cellar-mouth and a mushroom bed and one tallow lamp
over a stone table laid for a feast — the new-lamp night, Nix said,
when the warrens light the year's first wick off the year's last. The
lamp had burned eighty years on the last of the old year and never been
let touch the new. It stood very low now. Nix leaned on the rail with
the standard against his shoulder and looked at the low lamp for the
time a lamp takes to gutter, and counted nothing at all; and Ede, who
had learned from a whole climb what his counting was, understood the
not-counting too, and stood beside him and let it be.

*The rain-garth,* Sliver said that night, unasked, in the dry old voice
that had gone drier since its kin went dark. *I knew that sky. I
crossed under it twice before there was a tower, riding in a scholar's
ear. It rained like that the day I was cut.* A silence. *Every light
down here is the last of its kind. I am in good company. I had wanted
to say that word again before the end of things. Company.*

Ede closed her hand around the splinter and walked on, correctly.

On the third night the orchard finished. Word came back down the column
soft as word of a death, and the whole held morning let go at last and
ran. Noon came to the orchard for the first time in eighty years, and
the bees rose all together into the heat of the day they had been
promised so long, and the blossom went over to fruit while a soldier's
supper cooked; by the middle watch the tenders were harvesting at a
run, gently, weeping and working both; by false dawn the leaves had
turned and the first apples of the stolen world were going down the
column hand to hand to the litters, to the burned crews and the hundred
and forty out of the Fuel Pens, who ate them slowly, in the dark, while
twelve bare trees stood down into their first honest winter and the
garth's small sky went out. Done, Pell said. Not lost. Done.

The Fencerows garth stood last before the far gate, and it was the
largest of the eighty, and it was barley.

A close of it, half-mown, cut off mid-swath on the night of the Theft:
stooks half-built along the hedge, a whetstone lying in the stubble
where a hand had set it down, and the standing rest leaning heavy-
headed under a harvest evening's light — that long gold hour when farm
country races the dark, held at the racing, eighty years. Ede heard the
murmur go down the column and come back, because half the low-floor
musters had Fencerows folk in them, and they had walked four days
through other countries' held nights to find their own floor's stolen
evening standing ripe at the end of the road.

It was finishing. You could see it now that the orchard had taught
them what to look for: the gold going long at the edges, the light
leaning.

A Fencerows serjeant of the second muster came to Ede and asked what
none of them would ask the tenders directly, and Ede took him to Pell,
and Pell heard him out and looked a long time through the glass at the
mowers' scythes — still hanging in the hedge where the mowing had been
meant to start again at first light, eighty years of first lights ago —
and said, in the voice of a woman handing over a patient: "It wants
finishing. Go in and finish it."

They mowed the last field of the old world by its own last light.

Forty of the Fencerows' freed went in with the dead mowers' scythes,
and the tenders showed them the rows, and the only sound the width of
that whole dark country was the hush of blades laid into eighty-year
barley while the garth's gold hour finally spent itself over their
shoulders. No one sang. Harvest is sung home in hedge country, the
serjeant told her afterward, every year of the world — but you sing to
make the work go, and this work went by itself, and besides, he said,
and did not finish the sentence, so Ede finished it in her own head:
you do not sing over a deathbed. You sit up.

She bound sheaves in the second row until her hands learned the work,
which took a while, and nobody excused the general her crooked knots,
which was its own kind of gift. Nix walked the stubble counting sheaves
in a whisper, once for the floor and once for the dead, the way he
counted everything now. The light went amber, then ember, then the
color of a banked fire; the last swath fell as the first honest stars
any of them had ever seen came out inside the glass, over one acre of
the world, for the length of ten breaths; and then the Fencerows'
stolen year was over, and its garth stood dark and stubbled and
entirely, quietly finished.

The grain went into sacks, and Pell chalked each one herself, in a
plain hand, and what she chalked was not an inventory. SEED-CORN, the
sacks said. FENCEROWS. BY THE FIRST GATE. Eighty years old and one
evening old, the harvest never brought in, going home at last to be
planting instead of bread.

"Nobody living has seen a floor's second spring," Pell said, watching
the sacks tied off. "My grandmother held that the years below would run
again when the garths were done, and my mother held it, and I hold it,
the way you hold what you can't stand at a rail and look at. Either way
the seed goes home. And when the last garth ends, we'll close the glass
and come down after it. A freed world will be wanting almanac-keepers.
Somebody has to teach a hundred floors their seasons back; they've had
no practice."

⁂

The far gate stood open on a rising stair, and above it, Sliver said,
lay the King's own waiting-rooms, the last patience of the Made.

The host came up out of the country of the Years in column, in the
dark, in its own lantern-light again, and it did not smell of lead
anymore, or of ash. It smelled of straw and cut barley and apples; it
carried a dead world's seed-corn in its baggage-train; and at the head
of it, furled on Marn's shoulder, the oldest banner in the world went
up the stair trailing a last thread of harvest-dust from a field that
no longer existed anywhere at all, and never would again, and had, at
the very end, been brought in.

At the head of the stair Ede paused once and looked back down the road
of small skies — fewer already than four days ago, going out one by one
behind the host like lamps down a village street at bedtime — and drew
one long breath of the green dark, and held it, the way you hold the
last of anything.

Then she climbed toward the doors of the man who owned all of it and
had never once come to see, and the rising came up the stair behind
her, quiet as snow.
