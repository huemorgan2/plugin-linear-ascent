# Chapter 36 — Breaking the Hunt

Rhovan had been born under the Huntsman's law and had spent three hundred
years getting out from under it, and now he knelt in the Pale Kennels
learning it back by lamplight, because the law was the only weapon on this
floor that would loose.

Nael Hornsend taught him. The old horn-caller had kept the calloff-lore
alive for eighty years the way the keepers kept everything on this
climb — alone, at night, against the day someone would come who could use
it. The lore was not long. That was the terrible economy of it. Recall,
Houndcall, Mark, and over them all one word that gathered the rest the way
a fist gathers fingers: Calloff, the merciful capstone of the elf-hunt's
arts, the word that ends any hunt. A child could learn the sounds in an
evening. What could not be learned in an evening was the meaning them.

"You cannot call off a hunt you would rather win," Hornsend said. "The
word reads the heart it leaves. That is why the tower could not take it,
and why I could not use it. A thousand years his horn-caller. I taught
half his pack their names." The old elf's hands were steady on the master
horn — pale-silver, plain as a shepherd's, the Master's own. "I cannot
want his hunt ended more than I want my Master back, and the word knows
it. It must be sounded by someone who wants nothing from him at all."

"I am of the Grey Rides," Rhovan said. "I grew up wanting nothing from
him except distance."

"I know what you are," Hornsend said, not unkindly. "You are the one who
broke his own token over his knee two floors down, and answers a plain
question with a third of an answer, and walks wide of anything that could
be a leash. The word may like that in you. Loose things call off cleanly."
He set the horn in Rhovan's hands. "Or it may find the fraction you keep
back, and fail. We will learn together, in front of everyone."

Ede came to the kennels at middle-night with the trophy-keepers, and they
brought the collar. Rhovan had thought there would be more argument about
that — the taking down of anything from that wall had the feel of a
sacrilege — but the keepers carried Bell's collar folded in pale-grass like
a relic going to war, which was what it was. A hunt is worth what its
mercy is worth. The man who had said that was welded to a crown two
hundred strides away, and everything that loved him was arming to break
it.

"The army stands ready below the court," Ede said. "Say the shape of it
again. All of it. No fractions tonight, Rhovan."

He gave her all of it, which for her he now did, a change two years and
one disavowal in the making. "The Hunt must ride out — the stair opens
only past its Master, and its Master will not sit his stand while an army
walks his floor; the crown will make him course us. When the pale hunt
comes, no one runs and no one charges. Every soul in the rising turns and
walks toward the horns. The law receives what walks; the pack cannot take
a single one of us and the crown cannot make it — his own first law,
nailed into him deeper than the weld. That is the breaking. Not one law
against another. His law against his collar."

"And what can be taken," Vega rumbled, from the kennel door she filled,
"is the four welded dead who serve him — the whip, the horse, the horn-
wight, and the hounds' pale fever. Those we may fight. Those we will
fight." She had her hammer, and her arm was sewn and fired, and she had
asked for the remount herself: she had shod horses for four hundred
years, she said, and would not see a good horse ridden dead a stride
longer than she could help.

"And I have the small folk on the kennel-roofs," Nix said, "with sixty
buckets of kennel-broth, on account of the pups." He said it like a
battle-order, because it was one; the unblooded hounds, the pack's young,
frantic for a first kill they had never been given — the cure for them was
not steel, the quarry-folk swore, but the smell of the kennels at
feeding, the puppy-memory under the fever. "Sixty buckets, twelve
ladles, one goblin. I've held worse doors with less soup."

The Hunt rode out at the hour the horns woke, and it was the last time,
and some part of everything on that floor seemed to know it.

Rhovan stood in the front rank with the horn slung at his hip and
watched the pale court empty itself at them: the pack in its rings
unwinding into a flood of moon-white, the riders, the whips, the pale owls
dropping from the antler-branches, and last, from the high seat, the
Master of the Hunt on his dead horse, antler-crowned, coming down the
bone-white ground at a walk that was worse than any charge. The horns
sounded the drawn covert. Seventy banners stood on the pale-grass to
receive it — Men and giants and goblins, snare-folk and quarry-folk and
road-folk in wyrm-scale, the freed of thirty floors — and Rhovan heard,
rippling down the line in forty languages, the order Ede had given at
dusk like a mother teaching a prayer:

When the horns come, walk.

They walked. Gods and graves, they walked — an army turning as one thing
into the teeth of a full course and going forward at a footpace, and
Rhovan walked in it, and learned what Ede had bought alone in the Quarry
Runs and then given to every soul who followed her. The pack came on like
winter water. Every animal law in the body said run. Nobody ran. An old
Shieldwall pike-woman beside him was weeping straight ahead of herself
with her spear at rest; on his other hand a kobold matron had her whole
fist in her mouth and her feet going steady as a drum. Ten strides. Five.
And the flood of moon-white broke — parted, flowed round the walking army
hackles-down, ten thousand years of law holding against ten years of
weld — and the pale hunt came apart on its own mercy like a wave on a
sea-wall.

The welded dead did not part. The first whip came through the checked
pack with its silent lash taking heads of pale-grass, and the line met it
with bills and rivet-shields; it broke on the third rank and drained into
the white ground. The horn-wight rose on the Master's left and set its
horn to sound the kill-drive that would have burned the pack's law out of
it — and Rhovan, who had been given one task and a horn, sounded first.

He had wondered, kneeling in the kennels, what the word would find in
him — which held-back fraction, which walked-wide loyalty, whether a
name-broken elf three centuries out from under this law had standing left
to speak it. The word took him entire and truthful as an arrow leaving:
the Rides at lamp-lit evening, his mother whipping-in, every hunt he had
ever fled ending in one breath of pale-silver sound. Calloff. The
horn-wight's call died unsounded in its throat. Across the pale ground
ten thousand snares went slack in sympathy two floors down; the stand
crows lifted from their stands and were only crows; the riderless
remount, mid-gallop at the line, lost the fever's rein — and Vega stepped
out of the third rank and took its pale tack in her two hands and held,
as a farrier holds, till the welded dead thing shuddered dry out of the
harness and the tack fell empty across her arms. She folded it. Even
then, even there, she folded it.

Which left, in the ringing quiet, one rider.

The Master of the Hunt sat his dead horse in the middle of his broken
hunt, and the crown fought him. Rhovan saw it plain — the antlered iron
grinding at the seam, hauling the gloved hand toward the great horn at
the saddle-bow, the kill-drive, the last command the tower had left. And
up the pale ground toward him, between the seated hounds, unarmed, at a
walk, went Ede Stonhal with a cracked leather collar in her open hands.

She said nothing. This was his court; his court had taught her; speech
was spoor and mercy was the only word he had left. She stopped at his
stirrup and held up the little collar, name-tag worn to a shine, and the
pale rider looked down at it for the space of ten heartbeats, while his
hand strained against his own crown at the horn's throat.

Bell. She heeled to his hand before he ever carried a whip.

The hand came off the great horn. It came down, slow, shaking with the
iron's whole weight against it, and took the collar off her palms with a
gentleness Rhovan would see behind his eyes till he died. And the crown —
which the tower had nailed to a law of mercy and then bet against it —
cracked at the seam like frost-split stone, and the antlered iron fell
away in halves onto the white grass, and the pale drained out of the
court like a tide going home.

Color came back first to the hounds. Brindle and tan and wheaten, a
whole pack's plain dear colors flooding through the moon-white, and the
pack went mad the way dogs go mad at a homecoming, swarming the stirrups
of a man waking from fourscore years. The Master of the Hunt looked at
his hands. He looked at his kennels, his court, his people standing in
the wreck of his hunt wearing the colors he had forgotten. He did not
speak. He never spoke, and Rhovan understood at last that this had
stopped being the wound and become the dignity: what he did instead was
raise the master horn — Hornsend ran to him and gave it over, weeping like
rain — and sound one long call that Rhovan's blood knew before his mind
did, the call written under every other call, the hunt's true ending,
going out across the pale court and down the beaters' ways and floor by
floor through the whole wood-realm. Every hunt, ended. Every horn,
answered and released. In the Snare Woods the last snares fell slack; on
the Runs the lanes forgot the chase; and in the Gibbet Glade, the
snare-folk were already cutting their caught kin down at last and laying
them in the green ground.

Then the Master of the Hunt dismounted, hung the great horn on the
kennel-post the way a laborer hangs a tool at the day's end, for good,
and knelt into his hounds.

The muster before the Made took three days to count, and Rhovan, who
counted for the council now that the council trusted his counting, walked
the whole of it on the last evening with the tally in his head like a
psalm.

Seventy banners. He counted them again from the Vantage Stand as they
went up the pale sky, which was pale no longer but a plain kind evening
grey: the wall-and-star of Shieldwall, oldest and most mended; the
Forge-Court's hammer; Voss's crossed keys, which nobody loved and
everybody fed; the fair-folk's bunting, the Broodwarrens' knot, kobold
tally-marks on cut tin; the horn-and-heel of the pale court, newest of
all, sewn in three nights by the trophy-keepers from a thousand years of
pale-silver thread. Thirty-one peoples. He said their names to himself,
all of them, the way Nix had taught the freed to say their own: Men and
elves and giants and goblins, kobolds and the fair-folk and the road-folk
and the rest, thirty-one kinds of stolen, standing in one camp under one
charter that had been signed at a mead-hall and burned at a feast and
carried up sixty floors since like a banked coal. One army. The thing the
tower was built to prevent, fed and mustered and singing badly around
four hundred fires, at the top of the stolen world.

At the camp's north edge the green ended.

He stood a long time at that line, where everyone stood a while that
evening, the whole army taking its turn to look. Behind: the wood-realm
falling away tier under tier, lamplight in Bayhollow and Brambleside,
hunting-horns — sport-horns now, mercy-horns — calling clean and far away
down the runs, a hundred greens breathing in the dark. The stolen realms,
every floor of them walked and freed and left living. Ahead, above,
where the next stair rose: iron. The Made. Black outworks welded to
black ramparts, floor eighty-one going up into eighty-two like a fist
into a forearm, no floor-file keeper alive up there to hand them a map,
nothing up there the tower had stolen. Only what it had built. Rampart
and rivet, forge-light in the seams, and — faint, when the camp-singing
lulled — drums. Not Skarn's drums, nor the mine-dark's. Patient drums.
Manufactory drums, keeping a rhythm no heart kept, the sound of the
enemy's own country getting ready to receive them.

Ede came and stood at the line beside him, wrapped in her plain cloak,
with the splinter's faint light in her closed left hand. She had not
slept again; he could read that much, and had chosen — his own law now,
and the hardest he kept — not to read further, though something rode her
that was heavier than the Made, and had ridden her since a white hall he
did not let himself think about too exactly. She would tell him in
fractions, when she could. He was the last one entitled to complain of
that.

"The last stolen floor is behind us," she said. "Everything we freed
stays freed. Whatever we are after tonight, we aren't rescuers any more."

"No," Rhovan said. "After tonight we are the siege."

The drums turned under the singing like a millwheel under water. Along
the north edge the army looked up at the iron a while longer, and then,
being an army, went back to its fires, because the bread was coming
round again — and in the whole camp only the two of them stayed to see
the forge-light in the outworks flare, once, twice, like a smith testing
his fire before a long day's work.

The tower had stopped losing things it had stolen. What it owned, it
would spend. Rhovan went down to the fires and began, quietly, to count
arrows.
