# Chapter 12 — The Out-Mourned King

The third assault died on the causeway like the first two, and Ede called
it back before the moat could keep any more of her people.

They came off the stones in good order, shields up, dragging their hurt,
and behind them the barrow guard re-formed. It always re-formed. A rank of
drowned housecarls stood across the causeway where the line had broken them
a hundred heartbeats before — water running out of their mail, spears
levelled, patient the way only the dead are patient. Beyond them the mound
rose against a heavy sky: one grassed hill the size of a town, ringed by
nine black yews and a moat of water so dark it gave back no faces.

"Count," Ede said.

"Eleven hurt, two bad." Nix had the tally before she finished asking; he
always did. "Four spears in the moat. One ram. Well. You saw the ram."

She had seen the ram. Vega's forge-crews had built it on the Tithe Barrows
out of gate-timber and stubbornness, and the moat had opened under it like
a mouth. The water had not flooded. It had *risen* — the way a man rises
when someone enters his hall — and taken the ram, and the causeway's third
span, and a Tollman boy whose name Ede did not know yet and would have to
learn. Fire had done no better. The mound drank fire. The wights her line
broke drained into the black water, and the black water gave them back.

And through all of it, over all of it, the horn. The king's herald stood
on the mound's shoulder with a silver horn that had rotted into something
else, and what it blew was not a charge and not a warning. It was a
summons. It got into the knees. Ede had watched a Culler serjeant of
twenty years' service kneel in the causeway mud with tears on his face and
no idea why, and she had felt her own legs argue with her, and won, and
known that one morning soon she would lose.

Berrin had bread and dry stockings moving down the line before the last
shield came off the stones. He didn't ask how it had gone. He never asked
what a face had already told him; he just made sure the face got fed. Ede
took the heel of a loaf from him without a word and stood chewing it,
looking across the moat, while the barrow ravens went up off the yews in
one black breath and settled again, and the horn, satisfied, went quiet.

*"You cannot dig him out,"* Sliver said against her palm, dry as dust in a
reliquary. *"You are laying siege to a funeral. I have seen it tried
once. That army is in the moat."*

"Then tell me what takes a funeral."

The splinter was quiet a long moment.

*"A better one,"* it said.

Mereth Wakesend received the war-council in a turf-roofed watch-house at
the causeway's landward end, because she would not leave the causeway, and
after three days at the Kingsbarrow nobody asked her to. She was the last
barrow-queen of Wakesend, hereditary warden of the royal grave, and she
was old the way a fence-post is old: grey, checked, and not going
anywhere.

"You can't kill him," she said. "You've spent three mornings learning
what I could have told you the first. He isn't a beast the fever rode.
He's a *king* the tower woke, and the mound is his hall and the moat is
his wall and the court is his oath-men, and every one of them is made of
the same grief. Steel goes into it like steel into water."

"Everything the tower made can be broken," Vega said. She said it slowly,
the way giants say everything, and she finished her sentence, the way
giants always do. "I have broken things I forged myself. I only need to
find where the weld is."

"The weld is grief, mistress," Mereth said. "Go on. Put your hammer to
that."

The council was crowded and it was quarrelsome, because it was three
armies pretending to be one. On the Tithe Barrows below, not a week gone,
Cullers and Tollmen and the Wake's hired bows had been killing each other
over gate-rights when the mounds opened, and only the wights rising under
all three banners at once had taught them the arithmetic — die separately
or stand together. They had stood. They did not have to like it. Hale sat
at the table's end with his hands flat in front of him and said nothing at
all; he had said very little since the Broodwarrens, since he had carried
his own dead out of the silk.

"There is a rite," Mereth said, when the arguing had burned itself down to
coals. "Older than the tower. Older than my king, and he was old when the
yews were saplings. The mound keeps the funeral-forms of Men, and the
forms have a law in them, and the law is this: *precedence of grief.* Two
mournings cannot hold one ground. The lesser yields to the greater. That
is why no second king was ever howed on this floor. The mound would not
have him. It was already full."

"You're saying we mourn at him," said the Tollmen's captain, and laughed,
and stopped laughing when nobody joined him.

"I'm saying he cannot be out-fought, because fighting isn't in the forms.
And I am saying he can be out-mourned, because yielding is." The
barrow-queen looked around the table, at the banners, at the mud, at the
scarred hands. "You are the largest company of the bereaved that has ever
stood on this floor. Set it down at his moat, all of it, by the forms, with
the names said aloud — and his court must answer the question the horn has
been asking you for three days."

"Which is what?" Ede said.

"*Who mourns?*"

Ede sat very still. She was thinking, unwillingly, of a stone at the foot
of the tower with thirty names cut into it, and of who had cut them — with
a chisel, at night, sober only technically — and of how cutting a name is
a thing you can do instead of saying it. She had not said her dead aloud
in the years since the Warcamp. Not once. Not sober.

"The forms," she said. "Teach them to us."

They began at dusk, because the forms began at dusk, and Wakesend's
barrow-keepers went down the line with rushlights until the moat's whole
landward bank was a thin river of fire under the heavy sky. The army knelt.
It was not ordered to. The order had been only *stand and keep silence*,
but kneeling came off the mound like weather, the horn low and long, and
this time no one fought it, and so it did not take the knees. It asked.

The herald stood on the mound's black shoulder. The horn spoke — that
long, drowned summons — and Mereth Wakesend answered it from the causeway
stones in a voice that carried like a bell over water.

"The living," she called. "The living mourn."

The horn again — shorter. A question.

"For our dead," she answered. "And we will name them."

Then she turned, and looked at Ede, because the forms said the greatest
grief went first, and everyone on that bank knew whose that was.

Ede walked out onto the causeway alone. The barrow guard stood a spear's
throw away, dripping, still. Behind her, three thousand rushlights and not
one cough. She had led charges that frightened her less.

"Corran Vael," she said, and her voice did not carry like Mereth's, and
so she said it again, louder, and the name went out over the black water
and did not come back. "Corran Vael. My first serjeant. He died on the
gate of the Red Warcamp so the third file could get behind the drums. He
sang badly and knew it and sang anyway.

"Bel Tarrow. Little Bel. She lied about her years to carry my banner, and
I let her, and she was seventeen. Osric Pound. Marta of the Weir. Dun
Fallow — the old one, not his nephew. Hewet. Cass Redmane, who owed me
three coppers, and I forgave the debt on the stair and she said she'd pay
me at the top anyway…"

Thirty names. She had cut them into the Stone of the Climb with her own
hand, and she found that her hand remembered them better than her mouth
did; twice she had to stop and stand in the silence with the rushlights
burning until the name came, and the silence waited for her, and the moat
waited, and even the horn waited. Nothing at the Kingsbarrow hurried
grief. It was the first place in the tower where there was time enough.

Then the freed Shieldwall came out onto the stones behind her — the ones
who had lived, whom she had marched into the massacre and left in Skarn's
cages, half of whom had spent a season believing she had sold them — and
one-armed Dun Harrow planted the banner on the causeway and named the
cage-dead. The ones who had not lasted. The winter names. His voice was a
standard-bearer's voice and it did not break, but it came close enough
that no one on that bank was untouched, and when he finished, others were
already walking forward, unbidden, because grief keeps its own muster.

The keepers named their floors' dead — Fairstead's, Cairnside's,
Wakewater's, counts that had not matched for years finally read out in
full. Vega knelt on the bank, too big for the causeway stones, and said
nothing for a long while, and then, slowly, finishing every sentence,
named the ones who had died wearing steel with her forge-mark on it, and
asked their pardon — which was not in the forms. The mound allowed it.

Hale came last of the captains. He stood where Ede had stood and named
his twenty — the Cullers the brood-silk took at the warrens — plainly,
like a man reading a duty-roll. And then he did not step back. He stood
there with his jaw working, and
what he said next he said quietly, so that only the moat heard the whole
of it: he began to name the warren's dead. The surrendered ones. The ones
his own doctrine had cost. He did not know their names — kill-all keeps
no rolls — and so Nix went out and stood at his elbow on the cold stones,
a goblin beside the man who had spent twenty years killing goblins, and
supplied them, fast and low: "Threadfoot. Old Ochre. The midwife — they
called her Two-Lamps, on account of — just Two-Lamps. Say it." And Hale
said it. All of them. It took until the middle of the night.

And the mound listened.

Ede felt it turn — not the moment the court broke, because the court did
not break; the moment it *conceded*. The moat went still first, still as
a held breath, the drowned lilies motionless on the black glass of it.
Then the barrow guard grounded their spears. Rank by rank, the sound of
iron butts on stone rolling around the moat's whole ring like slow
thunder, the dead housecarls set their weapons down and knelt — to the
living. The tribute-bearer came down the mound with its arms full of
grave-gold and laid it at the causeway's far end, all of it, a king's
ransom set down like an offering at someone else's funeral. The herald
raised the silver horn one last time, and what came out of it was not a
summons. It was a lament, long and falling, the note a horn makes when
the hunt is over and everyone is coming home. Then it, too, knelt.

The mound's lych-face opened, and the Barrow King came out into the
rushlight.

He was drowned and colossal and crowned, grave-gold on his war-gear and a
thousand years of moat in his bones, and the fever's wrongness hung on
him like a borrowed cloak, too small for what wore it. He stopped at the
causeway's end, above his kneeling court, and when he spoke it was in the
funeral-forms and nothing else, because nothing else was left in him.

*"Who mourns?"*

"All of us," Ede said.

*"For whom?"*

Ede looked back once — at the rushlights, at the banners of three armies
that had been killing each other a week ago, at Dun's standard and Hale's
bowed head and Nix small and straight-backed on the stones — and then she
looked up at the dead king and gave him the answer the whole climb had
been carrying since the night of the Theft.

"For a world."

The king stood a long time. Rushlight moved on the black water. And then
the Barrow King, who could not be beaten with steel, reached up with both
hands and lifted the crown from his own head, and set it down on the
causeway stones, and yielded.

*"Precedence,"* he said, *"is given. My grief kept one man's house. Yours
keeps the world's. Bury us well."*

He went back into the mound, and the mound went with him. It did not fall
the way a wall falls. It *folded* — the whole grassed hill sighing inward
with a sound like a breath let go after a thousand years of holding, the
nine yews bowing until their crowns touched the grass, the moat pouring
itself into the closing earth, the kneeling court sinking rank by rank
into ground that received them like a bed long since turned down. The
wrongness went out of it the way dark goes out of a room. When it
settled, there was a low green barrow under a clearing sky, and a stone
lych-face standing open like a door, and behind the door the stair-lift
burned with its patient light, unlocked, for everyone, forever.

They did not cheer. Three days of assault had not taken the mound, and no
one felt that a victory had now been *won*, exactly; a victory had been
listened for, and granted. That was new. The army moved around it
carefully all the next morning, the way you move around a sleeping child.

Mereth Wakesend took up the crown from the causeway with both hands, and
did not put it on, and never would. Her keepers would climb with the
rising now — crown-breakers, she called them, the folk who knew how to
lay a wrongful ruler down, and Ede thought there might be work for that
craft at the very top of the world. The grave-gold went to the
quartermasters' wagons under seal. The Tollman boy from the moat got his
name back — Petch, a wharf-rat from Roothollow — and got it said aloud at
the water's edge, by the forms, with the whole line standing witness. The
forms belonged to everyone now.

Word came up while they struck camp: Herald-fire on the freed floors —
Voss's toll-houses burned at three gates in the night. Reprisal, aimed at
money. Voss, the report ran, was counting his losses out loud, and
beginning, for the first time in his profiteering life, to count them in
the rising's favor. Ede filed that where she filed all news of Gilder
Voss: useful, and not to be turned her back on.

She stood at the lych-face a while before she climbed, with Sliver warm
in her palm.

*"Three assaults,"* the splinter said. *"Rams, fire, and the finest steel
on five floors. And the mound took a list of names."*

"Say it plainly."

*"You have been carrying that sword since Roothollow believing the sword
was the weapon,"* Sliver said. *"It is not. It never was. The weapon is
what the sword means — who it is drawn for, and who is named after. The
tower has no answer to that. I have waited a long age for someone to
notice."*

Ede looked up the stair, where the fourth tier waited: the Grey Rides
first, by the keepers' telling — dead lamps and a hunt that would not
come home — and beyond them week upon week of floors, drifts and delvings
and a funeral causeway they now knew how to walk. And at the head of the
tier, at floor forty, the thing every keeper lowered their voice to
mention: a throne of silk, and a queen who did not kill what she caught.

She kept what she kept, they said. And she liked to *talk terms*.

"Then we'd best learn what our grief is worth at market," Ede said, and
stepped onto the stair, and went up.
