# Chapter 21 — The Wyrm's Pity

The ash began two floors below the fire. It greyed the frozen boughs
of the Rime Forest; it lay on the Black Meadows like a mourning-cloth;
by the Doorstone Moor it was falling steadily, warm as a lie, and the
host marched under it with cloths tied over their faces and their
banners going grey. Ede Stonhal had never before marched through the
falling of a thing that had been burned. She did not let herself
finish the thought of what, on this floor, got burned. The floor
finished it for her soon enough.

The Cindermouth opened before the vanguard on the third day: the mouth
of a living caldera, a ring of black glass terraces descending to a
lake of slow fire that pulsed below like a heart too large to hurry.
Around the whole rim stood the pyres, hundreds of them, banked and
burning as they had burned for a thousand years, each one a mouth the
floor fed so that the thing in the caldera would not come up and feed
itself.

Sira Lastwater met the vanguard at the rim's cold outer edge. Ede had
met keepers on forty-nine floors and knew the breed by now. She had
never met one who hated the thing she kept.

"I tend the pyres," Sira said, without greeting. "My mother tended
them. Hers carried the wood. Do you understand me, general? A thousand
years, and it was never the wyrm that built the first pyre. It was us.
We taught her that we would feed her, because we were afraid, and the
fever made her glad to learn it. There is a rite for dousing them — the
free-rite, older than the feeding, hidden in my family like a debt. I
have kept it my whole life and never once been permitted to say it
past the first line. The cult will kill you to keep the fires lit.
They are not wicked. They are what a thousand years of being afraid
looks like when it is given a uniform. Kill as few as you can."

*She is asking the right general,* said Sliver, dry against the bone
of Ede's ear. *You keep the count of the ones that could not be
helped. I have watched you cut it in a stone.*

"Kill as few as we can," Ede said, to both of them, and gave the order
to form on the rim.

The battle for the Cindermouth was fought along the rim-walk at
mid-morning, in falling ash, and it was the strangest fight of the
climb, because the enemy was not trying to kill the host. The enemy
was trying to keep the pyres lit.

They came up out of the terraces in their ranks: tribute-ogres,
collared and ash-scarred, enormous — some of them weeping, and Ede
made herself keep seeing it — with the pyre-poles and dragging-hooks
of their office; and among them walked the pyre-wights, figures of
standing flame that had been the given once, aether caught in the
burning. Where a pyre guttered, the wights walked to it and it roared
back to life. Where a soldier fell, the hooks came for the body,
because the cult had a thousand years of habit about the fallen.

Overhead the fevered rim-life joined in for its own reasons:
ash-drakes strafing the line in flat screaming runs, rim-jackals at
the flanks, wyrmlings boiling up the terraces spitting fire. The
shield-line held the rim-walk; that was the whole battle. Hold the
high walk, douse the pyres behind it, and do not be driven down the
glass terraces toward the lake.

Ede walked the line, because that was her post. Where it bent, she
was there, the freed Shieldwall around her and Dun Harrow's banner
overhead, going grey and bright again as the ash slid off the cloth.
Rhovan's archers shot the drakes out of their runs with a patience
that looked like leisure and was not. Vega stood at the third
pyre-field quenching fire as she had quenched water, and where her
craft took a stack the wights around it faltered like lamps in wind.

The pyres were the battle. The free-rite went down the rim from stack
to stack behind the fighting: Sira at the first pyre with the water of
Lastwater's well, and behind her the tribute-ogre deserters Nix had
been gathering at his fire since the Ogre Steps. They knew the rite;
they had always known it; the cult had kept it the way a jailer keeps
a key he prays he will never be asked for. Ogre after ogre knelt at
the stacks they had fed and said the old words and put the fires out —
and the pyre-wights did not fight the dousing. Each one stood over its
dying stack like a man over a grave, and when the last ember went
black, it came apart into ash and warmth and was gone. The soldiers
had watched the burning dead be put to bed.

By noon the rim was a line of cold stacks under a clearing sky, the
ash thinning as the caldera calmed. The cult's ranks broke, not in
rout but in something worse to watch: ogres sitting down where they
stood among the doused pyres, unhooking their collar-chains with
shaking hands, done. The host took them in. The Pressed dead that
morning numbered forty-one, and Ede stood over the laid-out rows and
let the whole line watch her count them, because somebody had to own
the arithmetic — forty-one deaths that should not have been needed,
cut into her memory beside the thirty names on the Stone of the
Climb, in the place she kept the accounts that would never balance.

Then the fire-lake fell quiet in its bed, and out of the caldera —
mildly, the way weather arrives — came the voice.

It did not shout. It was simply large, a voice with geologic patience
and no hurry anywhere in it, and it said: "The fires are out. A
thousand years, and the fires are out. Come down, general of the
walking-folk. I will not eat you. I have lost my appetite for being
obeyed."

She went down with four: Sliver at her collarbone, Nix because the
Pressed trusted no parley without him, Rhovan because he heard the
fractions in what powers said, and Sira, because a thousand years of
her family had earned the seeing of this. The terraces descended in
rings of black glass, still warm, and the light changed as they went —
less of the sky in it, more of the lake — until they stood at the lip
of the Nest.

Cindermaw lay coiled about her hoard, and the hoard was ash. The
treasure of a thousand years of tribute was not gold — there was gold
in it, sunk and unregarded — it was pyre-ash, drifted into dunes, and
the wyrm lay wrapped around it the way an old dog wraps a dead
master's coat. She was a true wyrm, mountain-scaled, her folded wings
like tarred ridgelines, and her eye, when it found Ede, was the size
of a shield and older than anything else the climb had ever looked
at. A campfire from the rim behind them stood reflected in it, one
bright point in an acre of dark.

"I was old when the fields you were born in were a sea-bed," said
Cindermaw. Her voice moved the ash in slow waves. "I chose this
mountain before your people had chosen a name for themselves. I am
not of the tower. The tower came to *me* — built its floors around my
caldera because even a thief knows better than to move a mountain
that is watching him. Sit. Stone does not hurry, and I am mostly
stone by now."

They sat. It seemed the only thing to do, and Nix did it first.

She spoke the way strata lie: slowly, in order. She spoke of the
Theft as a recent event, with a neighbor's mild disapproval for new
people who build ugly. Of the fever, with contempt, and of herself
under it without mercy: "It found the hunger in me and fed it. Do not
make me innocent, little general. The wanting was mine. The teaching
was his. We will divide the shame when one of us is dead." Then she
lay silent a long while, and the ash settled, and nobody — not even
Nix — filled the silence.

"You are the third army I have watched climb," she said at last.

Ede felt Rhovan go still beside her, the particular stillness of a
man memorizing.

"The first climbed angry — no banners on it yet — and reached the
fortieth floor in its fury and fed the tower's cages for a decade.
The second climbed clever —
quiet, patient, well-led. It got higher. Its banners hang in the high
vaults of the Made, behind glass. The tower keeps what it is proud
of." The great eye moved over them. "You climb *heavy*. Nine peoples,
and your dead all named. I have felt you through the rock for a
season. You may be worse to stop than either. That is not prophecy.
Mountains do not prophesy; we only remember forward."

"You watched them fail," Ede said. "Both. And him — you have watched
him longest of all. The keepers say you are the only being in the
tower that pities him."

"And your keepers say it as a wonder," said Cindermaw. "It is only
age. I have watched him eighty years — longer than his Heralds
have lasted, longer than his courts. He is young. He is frightened.
He was frightened first and cruel after; it is nearly always that way
around. He stole a world and stacked it into a tower so that he would
never have to be alone with what it thought of him, and now he sits
at the top of the one thing he made, deaf and watchful, and of every
creature in it — the collared, the caged, the welded, the burned — he
is the only one who can never leave. The tower has one true prisoner,
general, and he built the cell himself, and it is shaped exactly like
him. I pity him. He built the one tower tall enough to see the whole
world from — and in eighty years he has never once looked. Somebody
must pity him. It will not be you; you have work. Mountains have time."

The fire-lake breathed. Ash moved over the hoard in its slow tide.

"Now," said Cindermaw, "I will pay for my fires being put out. I am
old enough to know a debt when one sits down in front of me. Not
gold; the gold is under the ash, take it as you leave, it never meant
anything to me. I will pay in what a mountain has. I will tell you
what I smell."

Her head came down and around, slow as a headland turning weather,
until the shield-sized eye hung level with Ede alone.

"Every creature that has ever crossed my floor is in my nose for
good. Smoke does not keep names, but it keeps *everything else*. And
I tell you, general of the third army: I smell one of you twice —
once going up, once having already been."

The ash was loud in the silence. Ede heard her own blood.

"One of the host," she said.

"One of yours. Fresh on the rim above me this morning, and old in the
deep of my nose from years ago — going up then too, and *back down
after*. Down is the rare direction, little general. Everything climbs
past me; almost nothing returns. The second smell has come down and
joined the third army going up. Who, I cannot give you. Smoke keeps
no names, and eight thousand of you stood in my ash this morning. But
the nose is a mountain's ledger, and the entry is true. One of you
has already been."

"Why tell me?" Ede asked the wyrm. It came out quieter than she
meant.

"Because the first army was betrayed at the fortieth floor," said
Cindermaw, "and the second at the ninetieth, and I am tired of the
smell of it. Climb, third army. The rim is open, the road is yours,
and my reasons go with you. If I pass you on the way up, remember that
of everything you freed on this floor, the largest of it is grateful."
The great body began, tectonically, to gather itself. "And general.
Whoever he is — when you find him, before you do what armies do. Ask
him what they promised. It will be something small. It always is. That
is the other thing worth pitying in this tower, and the discipline is
harder."

She rose. It was like watching a range decide against geology. The
first true wingbeat in a thousand years knocked flat the ash-dunes of
the hoard and bared, glinting, the tribute-gold of ten centuries —
and Cindermaw the Wyrm went up out of her caldera into her floor's
own sky, once around the mouth of it, and was gone, upward,
remembering who freed her.

On the rim above, Lastwater's people stood at their cold stacks in
the clearing air and wept, and the pyre-field's thousand years were
over, and the fifth tier was free.

That night Ede sat late at her fire, and the thing the wyrm had given
her sat across from it like a guest who would not leave.

*Once going up, once having already been.* Set beside what Rhovan had
brought down from the Broodthrone — Vyx's courier, twice past her web
— the two ledgers closed on a single figure. Someone in the host.
Someone at these fires. Someone who had climbed before the rising was
a rising, and come back down, which is the rare direction, and stood
this morning in the ash among eight thousand of her people, grieving
the Pressed dead, cheering the wyrm, counted on the census-wall like
everyone else.

She found she was watching the fires down the terrace-line. Faces she
had bled beside. She made herself stop, and could not, and understood
with a cold thoroughness what had been done to her this day, and that
it could not be undone. The wound the tower could not put into the
rising with drum-fire and cages, it had put in with a sentence. The
hunt was inside the walls now. Inside her.

*Say it,* said Sliver.

"Everyone who has ever climbed before," Ede said slowly, "and come
back down. Veterans of the first two armies, if any live. Old
gate-runners. Tollmen who worked the high spans. Keepers' kin who
went up after the lost. All of them, mustered on a roll — quietly. If
it is done loudly, the guilty one runs and the innocent are ruined.
Quietly, then. A guides' roll: everyone with prior knowledge of the
floors, wanted for the van. It is even true. It is just not the
truth."

It began the next morning, without proclamation, a line in the
quartermasters' ordinary business: the rising sought those who had
climbed in former days, that their knowledge might serve the van.
Names came in slowly, then well — men proud to be asked, an elf who
had run the Grey Rides before the Wake was the Wake, two sisters who
had gone up to fifty-five after their father and come back down with
his ice-axe and nothing else. Each went onto the roll in a fair
clerk's hand.

Berrin kept the rolls. He had kept every roll of the rising's since
Roothollow, and he kept these the way he kept all of them, neatly,
gladly, late into the night, and had the first fair copy on Ede's
table within three days.

*You will want a second copy,* Sliver said, when she set it down.
*And a second keeper. You are building a net, and a net is only as
honest as its knots.*

"I know," said Ede, and looked a while at the neat columns, and the
fire behind them, and the dark beyond that, where her army lay asleep
around ten thousand small lights — her nation — and somewhere among
their breathing, one breath that had been up this tower before and
had sold the going of it. "I have begun to look at my own fires the
way I used to look at a treeline, Sliver. He has made me a general in
my own camp."

*Then be a good one,* said the shard, dry as ever, and ancient, and
hers. *You have always fought best uphill.*

Above them the tower went on. Word came down with the evening meat:
past the caldera the stair opened on the Deep again — a city hung
over a chasm on bridges of spider-silk, lamps in the void like low
stars. And past the silk, the delvers said in whispers, lay a floor
with no name anyone used twice: a country of dust-funnels and hidden
lids, a whole land built as one enormous trap, where the ground
itself listened for footfall. The delvers had one sentence of advice
about crossing it, and the sentence was: *don't be heavy.*

Ede read the scouts' report twice and gave her first order for the
Trapdoor Fields that same night, and it went out to a nation of
sixteen thousand boots, and it was one word long.

*Quietly.*

At his own fire, two terraces down, the lightest soldier in the host
was already walking heel-to-toe along a rope his kobolds had
stretched between two spears, arms wide, tongue between his teeth,
counting his steps out loud. Practicing.
