# Chapter 9 — The Tale of the First Banner

Three days up from the cages, the freed were learning fires again.

The Burnt Common ran black to every horizon — standing char and
gorse-skeleton and bracken stopped forever at knee-high, mile on mile
of it under the
floor's own deep sky. By day that sky was the pale sourceless glare the
low floors kept. At night it banked down to an ash grey that was not
darkness and was not anything else either, and there were no stars in
it, because whatever this stolen country had kept of its nights, it had
not been allowed to keep those.

The host camped in a burn-scar the width of a parish, pickets out to
the char-ridges, and for the first time since the pens opened, the
fires were not sorted. Freed sat with militia; miners of Half-Light
argued mule-loads with muster-folk of Bannerline. It was not
forgiveness. It was only that the Common was very large and very
dead, and people will sit with anyone rather than sit with that.

She walked the lines at dusk with her hood down, because that had
worked once, and found the biggest fire in the camp by following the
sound of a goblin counting.

"— four hundred and six, four hundred and seven — you've gone back,
you're going *back*, that's the same stitch twice —"

"It's a lock-stitch," Dun Harrow said, patiently. "You go back to go
on. Sit down or hold something."

The banner of Shieldwall lay across the standard-bearer's knee, and
Dun was sewing. He had the cloth pinned to his thigh under the flat of
his boot-knife's sheath and the needle in his one fist, and he sewed
the way rivers cut country: not fast, and not stopping. The march had
opened the fly-hem; Vega's silver forge-wire held the hoist, but wire
was for wounds, Dun said, and hems wanted thread. Around him the
company sat mending — boots, straps, gaolers' warplate taken in for
narrower shoulders — because Dun mending drew mending, the way one
man eating draws appetite.

"Captain." He did not look up. "Two hands wanted. The hem, if you
please. Pull it flat and keep it flat."

So the general of the rising knelt down in the char and held cloth
taut between her fists while her standard-bearer sewed, the old grey
field coming flat under her hands, the sewn wall and its one star
lying out across her knees, and nobody found it strange — which was,
she thought, the whole distance the company had come in three days.

It was Wick who asked — a hedger's son from the Fencerows, sixteen,
serious, his first blooding at the Warcamp still sitting behind his
eyes. It came out louder than he meant, the way rehearsed questions
do.

"Why is it a wall?"

Dun's needle kept moving.

"Every other banner on this climb has a killing thing on it," Wick
said, doggedly. "The Cullers' pennons have the axe. Bannerline's got
the horns. Ours is a wall. A wall and a star, and the star's not
even —" he caught himself.

"Say it," said Nix, delighted.

"— straight," said Wick, miserably.

The fire got quiet in a ring, outward, the way water stills. Because
the question was not about cloth at all: three nights ago a baker had
said *he came from home*, and every soul at that fire had climbed
since with the same stone in the boot. If home could sell them, what
was under the banner? What was it a banner *of*?

Dun sewed to the end of his thread, and bit it, and was still a
moment.

"That's a tale," he said. "And I'm a poor hand at tales. I carry the
thing; carrying's not telling. Hetta Marle could tell it — she carried
before me; she'd have done the voices." He looked into the fire. "But
Hetta's on the north face of the Stone, so you'll have it from me,
wrong in the corners, and you'll sit on your objections till I'm
done."

"They won't," said Ede.

"No," Dun agreed. "They never do. That's the correct way to hear it."

"There was a wall at Foldwater," Dun began. "Before the tower. Before
Roothollow was anything but a ford and a bad inn —"

"Shepherd's End," said Mag Weller.

"— it was Foldwater —"

"My gran poured at the Shepherd's End inn forty years, Dun Harrow, and
she called the village Shepherd's End, and she called people who said
Foldwater *upstream folk*."

"Your gran," said Dun, "poured short measures." He let the laugh go
by. "Foldwater or Shepherd's End. It's under the granary row now;
Roothollow ate the village and the name both, and mind that, because
it's a piece of the tale. There was a village, and the village had a
waller. A woman who set stones for hire. Field walls, fold
walls, the in-field dyke — dry work, no mortar in it. Her name was
Aldis Bright."

Ede's hands were full of banner-hem, and so she did not move. But
something in her went and stood at the foot of a standing slab by the
Root Gate — the lit face, oldest names at the base, and lowest of
all, first cut of all, a name worn nearly flat, its glow gone thin as
breath under two generations of thumbs. A. BRIGHT. She had cut thirty
names into the other face of that stone, and never once asked anybody
who the first name was, because in Roothollow nobody asked, because
nobody knew.

"First winter after the Theft," Dun said, "the world was wreckage —
you've had your grandmothers on it; I'll not compete. What was left
on the ground was edge-folk and orphans and whatever the tower
spilled or didn't want, and out of the root-gates that winter came
things. Masterless welded things. Fever-mad beasts. And men gone
raider, which was worse, because the beasts at least were sick.

"Foldwater couldn't run. Lambing, and snow, and you don't run old
folk and children across open country in the dark on the say-so of
smoke. So they stood, and the place they stood was Aldis Bright's
in-field wall, the only made thing between the village and the
night — barn doors for shields, lambing-crooks, one boar-spear, and
her order, the first order of this company, one you've all had from
me on a bad night whether you knew it or not: *hold till the star
clears the wall*."

"What's a star?" said Nix.

It was not a heckle, and nobody laughed: the goblin had been born
under a roof the size of a country, under a meadow's false noon.

"A lamp," Dun said, "that nobody lights and nobody owns. The ground
keeps thousands; you'll see them when we've done the work — that's in
the terms, far as I'm concerned. The morning star is the last one up
before dawn, and it clears a waist-high wall about an hour short of
first light — and she'd sent her sister's boy running for the ford
muster at first smoke, and the muster could be there by dawn, and
she knew it, because a waller knows what's an hour's work. So: hold
till the star. It wasn't poetry. It was the time the relief was due."

"Waist-high," said Nix. "Waist-high. The first Shieldwall in the
world was a *stile*."

"A wall," said Dun Harrow, "is as tall as what's stood behind it. So,
yes. That night it was about the height of a frightened farmer, which
is the tallest thing there is."

"And the relief came?" Wick asked.

"The relief came. First light, ford-men and hedgers, the night's
work lying in the snow on the wrong side of the wall, and the wall
still standing — which a dry wall will do, mind. No mortar. Nothing
holding it up but every stone set right against its neighbors."

"I have always liked a dry wall," Vega said from beyond the fire,
slow, finishing her sentence into the quiet. "The only made thing
that stands by nothing but its neighbors. You cannot weld that. You
can only set it."

"That's the wall," Dun said. "Now the sword. Because there nearly was
a sword. Years on — call it ten; Hetta said seven; every count in
this tale is argued — the wall's famous, the taken are *up there*,
everyone's kin, and the first climb is gathering at the root: every
hedge-company and ford-muster sewing itself a banner at the gate camp
that would grow up to be Roothollow. And a lord among the spilled —
been somebody grand on a floor that was now weather — looks at the
grey cloth her village women had sewed and says: a company wants a
device that puts fear into what's in front of it. Sew her a sword.

"And Aldis Bright looked at him — Hetta would do the look; I can't;
imagine a woman pricing a bad load of stone — and she said —" Dun
stopped. He went at it again, carefully, like a man laying a course.
"She said a sword on a banner tells the ones in front of you what
you mean to do to them. And she'd no quarrel with that, in its
place. But a banner doesn't march facing forward. A
banner is read from *behind* — by the ones walking after it, the
frightened ones, the ones carrying children — and what the cloth owes
them is not fear. So sew what they need to read. A wall. That the
thing they're walking behind will stand between."

He turned the cloth on his knee and found the star with his thumb.

"She sewed this herself. Night before the first climb, by firelight,
with a waller's hands — she could set a ten-ton dyke to a hair's
line, and she could not sew, and she knew it, and she wouldn't give
the star to the women who could. Her wall, her star. It came out
crooked. They offered to pick it out and set it straight, and she
looked at it a long while, and she said —"

"'It's the star as we saw it,'" said Mag Weller, softly, into her
lap. "'A star looks crooked to folk lying under a wall with their
ears to the ground. Leave it.'"

"Your gran had that right, anyway," said Dun. "I've sewn this star
four times. Crooked every time, the same crooked, measured. You keep
the cloth alive. You don't correct her."

It was Serle Danner who asked it. He was at the fire's edge, the way
he took all his places now — half in, half ready to stand — and he
asked it flat, like a man testing a plank before he puts weight on
it.

"Who sold her?"

The fire ticked. Nobody looked at anybody.

"Nobody sold her," Dun said. "Nobody sold anybody, those years. The
world was too poor to buy a company and too frightened to want one
dead. That trade needed peace to invent it — the King's kind of
peace, eighty years of it, fat enough that a route could be worth
silver." He said it without heat. "What took the first climb was
the climb. They went up with the whole root at their backs — first
banners, first lifts opened,
first names on the Stone, hers cut first of all. Past the drum
floors. Past the silk, some say. How high is arguing. Hetta held
she reached the King's own country, the Made, all iron and no
weather. And then —"

He was quiet.

"And then no word came down," he said. "Ever. The tower stood. The
lifts she'd opened closed again over the years, one by one, the way
a burn heals wrong. The songs give her endings — she fell at the
last gate; she took a floor no one's found and holds it yet; the
King keeps her banner in his hall to look at. Songs can't stand a
door left open. I don't sing those verses."

"How much of it is true?" Ede asked. The general's question, asked
plainly, wanting the count.

"All of it's true," said Dun Harrow. "Some of it happened."

The laugh went round low, and died decently. Ede watched him thread
the needle again, one-handed, against his knee, and asked the other
question — the one she had been holding since the cloth first came
flat under her hands.

"Five years in the pens, Dun. You sang the roll every night. Hundred
and twelve names in the dark. You had this tale the whole while."
She kept her eyes on the hem. "You never told it. Why."

The needle stopped.

It was a long time, for Dun. Three breaths, and the whole fire heard
them.

"Because it has no ending," he said at last. "And a cage wants
endings. You can hand caged folk a roll of names — a name's a fact,
the dark can't argue a fact. But hand them a tale where the best of
us goes up and the night just — keeps her — " and there his voice did
not waver, because Dun's voice never wavered; it simply stopped, and
he waited for it, and went on. "I was afraid of the ending. There.
A standard-bearer, afraid of a story. I sang the roll instead
because the roll was the
part I could stand behind. And I'm telling it tonight because of
what Mag smelled on that courier — because half this fire has been
lying awake on it. *Home* sold us. Fine. Look at the cloth, all of
you, and hear this: the cloth is older than home. This wall was
standing before Roothollow had a name to sell anything under.
Whatever home did — *this* didn't."

He drew the thread through and squared the hem and looked, for the
first time in the telling, up.

"And nobody ever stood her down," he said. "That's the piece I hold.
A wall's not like a sword; there's no losing one. A wall is relieved,
or it's still standing. Nobody relieved the first wall. So wherever
she stopped, she stopped *standing*, and the watch is still hers,
and it's been a long watch." He folded the banner once across his
knee, gently, the way you settle a sleeper's blanket. "An age is
late for a relief. But I've stood posts where the relief came late,
and I'll tell you what you think in the dark of one. You don't think
*they're not coming*. You think: they'll come. Step at a time.
Someone will come up that stair."

Nix, for once, had no arithmetic. He sat hugging his knees with the
firelight on him, and after a while he said, in a smaller voice than
the camp was used to, "I'd have run. That first night. Waist-high
wall and lambing-crooks — I'd have run."

"You didn't run at the Kingsfield," said Dun.

"I *hid*. Behind this exact cloth. It's good thick wool."

"That's a wall doing its work," said Dun Harrow. "Ask the star if it
minds who's under it."

⁂

The fire burned down to keeping-coals and the company went to its
blankets, and Ede sat with the mended banner rolled across her knees
until only Dun was left, packing his needle-case one-handed.

She held it out to him. For a heartbeat it was the fire-lane at the
Warcamp again — the silence, the held-out pole, the whole camp
watching to see what she was — and then he took it the same way he
had given it, without ceremony, grounding the butt by his boot.

"Yours to hold," he said. "Mine to carry. That's the arrangement,
Captain, and it's older than both of us."

She walked her rounds. At the third picket she stopped, out past the
last fire, where the Common ran black and mute to a horizon you felt
more than saw, and she took the splinter out against her palm.

"You were below in those years," she said quietly. "The first
climb — the wall, the star, the woman. Is any of it so?"

Sliver was a long time answering.

*"The wall is true,"* it said at last. *"The star is true. The rest
is sewing."* And then, unasked: *"Every fire in this camp tells
tales about someone I could not keep, Ede Stonhal. It is the trade
of being old. Let the man have his telling. He told it better than
he knows."*

She stood a while longer than the watch needed her. Somewhere behind
her, small in the dark of a country with no stars in its sky, the
banner of Shieldwall hung still against a cart-frame by the coals —
a grey field, a sewn wall, and above it, crooked exactly as a
waller's cold hands had left it an age ago, the only star on that
whole floor.

Hold till the star, Ede thought, and went to sleep a general, and
dreamed, for the first time in five years, of nothing at all.
