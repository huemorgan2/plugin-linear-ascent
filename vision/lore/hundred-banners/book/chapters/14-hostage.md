# Chapter 14 — The Hostage Chapter

There was no day in the Broodthrone, and no night, and after the first
stretch Rhovan stopped pretending to count either. The vault kept its
own time. The cocoon-lanterns brightened and dimmed together on some
slow tide of the fever, breathing light; the brood stirred in the high
galleries at intervals he came to know; and twice in every cycle of the
lamps, silk sang one long low note as the Matriarch moved upon her web,
and the hall's threads carried her weight like a shore carrying tide.
He learned her tread inside ten sleeps. He could have told her from a
thousand others with his eyes shut and his ear against a single strand.

Later he would understand that this was the winter's whole lesson, and
that she had let him learn it on purpose.

They gave him a chamber in the throne-web's second tier — a room of
white silk with a bed of white silk, dry, clean, and terrible, because
it was a cocoon by any honest accounting; it simply had a door. A
thread-page brought his meals: fair bread, blind cavern-fish, water
without color or taste. He ate what the court ate. That was in the
clauses. Everything was in the clauses. He was permitted any gallery
whose silk bore his weight, and forbidden the Larder, and he tested
neither rule: the first was a courtesy, the second a mercy, and he knew
the difference.

He tried his trade, the first sleeps, out of habit and fear — mapping
anchor-lines, marking which threads were leashes as Wend had taught
him. He gave it up inside a week. There was nothing here to steal. The
Broodthrone was the only court he had ever entered that kept no secrets
from itself: every thread catalogued, every captive inventoried, every
debt entered in the silk-white book. It was the Wake's dream, achieved
by a spider. He did not put that in a letter.

He was allowed letters. Clause the ninth: the hostage may write; the
court may read. So he wrote to the rising, in the flat cipher of facts,
knowing Vyx read every stroke before the ride-keeper courier took the
page: *I am well kept. The contract holds. The fish is better than the
army's.* Fractions of fractions. Somewhere below, the Wake would
already be moving on his first year's story; and there was a letter he
was not writing, to a grey woman who would not read it — every sleep he
composed a line of it, and every waking unwrote it.

Mostly, there was the quiet. He had thought he knew quiet — he was an
elf of the Rides, raised where the wood holds its breath at dusk — but
the Broodthrone's quiet was another element altogether, a sea of it,
with the wrapped court hanging in it like drowned lights. A hundred
living people around him, and no voice. He took to talking to the
nearest cocoon on his gallery, a lantern-glow with the shape of a
stout matron folded in it. He told her the news of the march and
reported the fish. He never learned if she heard. On the worst sleeps
he thought she leaned toward his voice, and on the best ones too.

The Matriarch came down to him on the twelfth sleep, or the fifteenth —
the lamps had bred, by then, and he had lost the count on purpose — and
taught him the game.

"You are dying of stillness," she observed, settling into the web above
his gallery with the terrible gentleness of the very heavy. Her voice at
close quarters was worse and better: silk over glass, but warm glass, a
lamp's chimney. "I warranted you back whole. Boredom voids the warranty.
Attend: this is called cradles. My court has played it since before your
people had lamps to light."

It was a game of knots. She spun him a strand of dry silk, cool as
linen, and tied into it a figure — loops crossed on loops — and passed
it to his spread fingers, and the law of the game was that he must take
it onto his hands without collapsing a single loop, and return it
changed: one knot added, none undone. The first evening he crashed the
whole cradle inside five exchanges and she laughed at him, a sound like
a bow drawn slow across a low string, and spun a new strand, and they
began again.

The stakes, she said, were questions. Whoever crashed the cradle owed
one true answer.

"I am a hostage of the Wake," he said. "You know what my answers are
worth."

"I know what your people think they are worth. Play."

He crashed it again — she had eight hands and patience like bedrock,
and he never won a rally outright all winter, and he came to suspect
the game could not be won, only lost more slowly, and that this too
was the lesson. And the
questions, when he owed them, were nothing his training had braced
for. She did not ask about the rising's strength, or the Wake's roll of
agents, or the Leaf-Ledger he had named at the signing. She asked:

"What does rain sound like?"

He sat with the crashed silk across his palms while his voice went
somewhere and had to be fetched back.

"On leaves," she added, precisely. "Begin with rain on leaves. I have
read of it in four hundred years of tribute and the accounts do not
agree, and it does not fall in the Deep, and it never once fell in this
tower. I am owning to an ignorance, hostage. Do not make me regret it."

So he told her rain, as well as he could — rain on the lamp-woods' broad
leaves, rain on the Rides' grey stone, rain on a canvas fly with a
friend asleep against your shoulder-blade, which is the best rain — and
partway through it he understood that
he was paying the story-clause again, in small coin, freely; and
that she had known he would; and that four hundred years of contracts
had taught her the one truth the Wake had never taught him: that the
things people guard are rarely the things worth having, and the things
worth having, they will give you for nothing, if you only sit still and
ask.

When he owed no answer, she took none. When she owed him one — twice
all winter, twice in hundreds of rallies — she paid in full, to the
letter, like the honest instrument she was. The first time, he asked
about the wrapped knight, her cocooned champion.

"Taken in the Men's wars, four tiers up, ninety years gone," she said.
"Dead before he reached my floor; I do not warrant what I did not
break. His skill is in the lines now, not in him. There is no man in
that mail, hostage. There is only the drill, going on without him. You
are asking whether I would keep *you*
so. You are of rank, warranted, and alive. The clauses forbid it."

"And if there were no clauses?"

"There are always clauses," said Matriarch Vyx. "That is the difference
between me and the tower."

The second answer she owed him, she paid on the coldest sleep of the
winter, when Berrin's trains had stopped for the freeze on the tier
above and the vault's tide of light ran shallow. She had crashed the
cradle herself — he never knew whether by mishap or by purchase — and
instead of waiting for his question she settled lower on the web, all
her weight gathered soft as snowfall, and said:

"I will tell you a story. True, and never told, because no one has ever
been positioned to be told it. We shall call it interest on your
Leaf-Ledger, which was a better coin than my clause demanded, and I do
not like to owe. Attend, hostage."

The lamps breathed. The court hung still on its threads.

"I was old already when the engines came," she said. "This hall was my
mother's, and the caverns under the world were wide, and we heard it
before we felt it: a note in the rock, below the deepest gallery, going
along and never stopping for breath. My brood ran upward. I
went *down*. I have never been able to leave an accounting unread.

"I hung in the dark of the deepest shaft, and beneath me — beneath the
floor of the world, where there is not supposed to be an underneath —
the engines of the Demon King went by. Hours of them. Patient as water.
They were cutting, hostage, that is what I want you to understand; not
burning, not breaking. Cutting, the way a clerk cuts a page from a
ledger he means to keep. And above me through the rock I heard a whole
country's roots let go — the rivers first, then the stones, then the
trees all together — and the world I was born under went up into the
dark like a lamp being carried out of a room, and the cold came in
where it had been.

"I watched the underside of a stolen country pass me in the dark. There
were still lamps burning in it. That is the whole of what I saw, and I
have thought about it for the age of the tower, and I will tell you the
conclusion: I hated him, your Demon
King, not for the theft. Theft I understood; I am a keeper of taken
things myself, and I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. I
hated him because he did not *keep* them. He took a world alive and he
warehoused it, floor from floor, in the dark, and let it starve of
itself. Even I warm what I wrap, hostage. Even I know that a kept thing
kept badly is only a slow kill with a clerk's face on it."

The silk was silent a long while.

"There is a second chapter," she said. "Smaller. You will want it more.
Twenty years gone, or near it, a climber paid my toll — I sold passage
retail then, to the rare mad
soul the Tollmen sent up with their marks on his coin. He paid in
toll-stamped silver and he would not pay the story-price, though I
offered him the discount; he gave me warmth-jars instead, double
weight. A hooded man of Men. Unremarkable, except in this: that four
seasons later, my threads woke on the downward road. And it was the
same tread, hostage. Coming *down*."

Rhovan did not move. Loop by loop, very carefully, the way you hold a
cradle you cannot afford to crash, he kept his hands still.

"Down," he said.

"Down. Paid again, in the same silver, and would not meet my lamps, and
went. The only soul in the age of this tower to pass my web twice. Your
armies pass upward, and the Pressed flee upward, and the dead go
nowhere. Nothing comes down, hostage. The tower is a valve. And once,
just once, it ran backwards. A step on my thread is a name, though not
one your
mouths could say. I would know his tread tonight among ten thousand.
Twenty years has not worn it off my web."

"Who?"

"No clause bought his name, and I do not give what was not bought. He
paid for silence with his silver, and my instruments hold whether the
party is present or absent, or my word is worth nothing and you have
wintered with a beast." A soft note in the silk, almost regret. "The
fact of him I give you freely, as interest. What he was, where he went,
whose bread he ate after — those were never mine to sell."

*Whose bread he ate.* Rhovan heard it, and it went down through him
like a thread through a needle's eye. A freed prisoner in the Warcamp's
wreckage, saying: the courier smelled of Roothollow bread. Everyone in
the rising's council had built the same quiet story from
it: a bought man *below*, a letter passed at a
gate-town, coin moving up through the Tollmen's chains. A traitor who
had never left home.

But nothing comes down. And once, the tower ran backwards.

Whoever had sold Shieldwall had not sold it by post. He had climbed —
past the Rides, past this very web, twenty years of tread unchanged, up
into the dark where the buyers were — and then he had come home, and
hung up his hood, and eaten his bread at somebody's table, and watched
the search look everywhere but *down*. The traitor was not a hand the
tower had reached. The traitor had gone up the tower and asked for it.

"You have stopped playing," Vyx observed.

"I'm thinking about a hood," Rhovan said.

"Mm." Eight hands gathered in the crashed silk and began a new
strand. "A last observation, then, gratis, because it has been a
tolerable winter and you tell rain well. I have taken tribute from
every kind of creature this tower holds, and the accounting is simple:
the ones who pay and go are honest, and the ones who pay twice are
buying something they cannot carry in their hands. He was not buying
passage, hostage. Passage was the receipt. What he bought was the road
itself — the proof it could be walked both ways and no one the wiser.
Someone in your bright climbing world has owned a private road for
twenty years." The cradle rose, loops crossed on loops, and hung on the
air between them. "I would ask myself, were I you and loose in the
daylight: what has been traveling it since?"

The thaw came to the tier above; the trains moved; and on a sleep like
every other sleep, a thread-page stood at his silk door and unpicked it,
stitch by stitch, and the clauses let him go.

She received his leave-taking at the Throne-Web, enormous and courteous,
and had the scrivener enter the hostage-clause discharged, warranty
honored, goods returned whole. He was surprised, and then not
surprised, to find something in him reluctant at the gallery road's
edge. It had been the strangest season of a long strange
life, and the stillest, and no one in it had lied to him even once,
which he could not say of any other winter he had lived.

"Clause the third falls due in a year, hostage," said Matriarch Vyx.
"Bring a better story. You have one; I heard you not telling it, all
winter, every sleep. Knots carry sound, and untold stories knock."

"Perhaps by next winter it will be tellable."

"They ripen," she agreed. "Everything I keep does."

At the tier-gate above, in the first true daylight of the young year,
the ride-keeper courier was waiting for him with the rising's mail: two
seasons of Ede's dispatches, a wax-sealed note in Nix's furious
fist-print — *FISH?? we saved you the good boots. the count is 41
banners. climb faster* — and a grey slip of
paper with no seal at all, edges singed in the Wake's plain way of
saying a channel was burned, five ciphered words long.

He stood in the wind and read it three times.

*I am coming up. — C.*

Rhovan of the Grey Rides looked up the tier, toward the chasm-mouth
camps and the wintering army and the friend he had to reach before his
handler did, and began, at last, to climb faster.
