# Chapter 43 — The Obsidian Stair

The stair was cut from a single sweep of black volcanic glass, and it was
the only light on the ninety-first floor: a cold gleam going up into dark,
polished by captive hands to a perfect mirror, so that every climber went
up it beside a flawless dark copy of themselves.

Wend of Firststep met the vanguard at the bottom tread. She was old and
bent from a life of polishing the thing that tormented her, and she gave
the leadership the mirror-lore the way keepers had handed the rising
their knacks on ninety floors below: plainly, holding nothing back.

"Don't meet your own eye," she said. "That's the whole of it. The glass
learns faces. Look at your boots, look at your neighbor, sing if you have
to. Your reflection has been on this stair longer than you, and it has
learned things. In the Silvered Gallery they step out whether you look or
no. There's a word for breaking them — my folk cut it into the Understep,
where the copies are sourced. But the word only finishes it." She looked
up the long gleam and her mouth went hard. "You finish it. The word just
sweeps up."

The host would cross by companies, eyes down, in the days after. But the
stair took the leadership first, one at a time; whatever else the black
glass was, it was honest, and it knew some accounts cannot be settled in
company.

Ede went first. She had promised it a word.

She climbed with her eyes level, against all the lore, having decided she
would not spend the throne-tiers flinching, and her reflection climbed
beside her in the black glass with her own tired stride for a hundred
treads, and then, where the moss lay deep in the seams, it stopped
matching. It straightened. It walked the way she used to walk before the
Warcamp, shoulders squared to a beloved world, and at the first turning
of the stair it stepped out of the glass and stood above her, and it was
her: sober-eyed, unhaunted, rested. A general. The general.

"You hanged him," Ede said. She could see it in the set of the mouth. Her
mouth.

"At dawn, on the gate," her reflection said, with her own voice, only
certain. "Before the thirty could talk themselves into theater. It was
kind — kinder than parading him down ninety floors behind a goblin with a
list. And the host slept better for it. An army wants a hand that doesn't
shake, Ede. You gave them a shaking one and called it justice." It came
down a tread, moving the way Hale had tried to teach her to move. "I came
back down from the Warcamp too. The difference is I never drank. I cut
the thirty names into the Stone and then I went and cut the man who sold
them, and I have not been tired a day since. Look at me." She did. It was
like looking at rest itself. "You could still be me. The glass keeps
every road a face didn't take. Step in, and the woman who climbs out
sleeps sound, hangs what needs hanging, and her armies never see her weep
over a Herald's carcass like a widow —"

"There it is," Ede said. "That's the flaw in the casting."

"You never wept for Malgrim," Ede said. "You put him down and wiped the
blade. And a general who can't grieve the enemy's dead can't mourn a
barrow-king's precedence, and never wins floor thirty, and never learns
what the sword *means,* and loses the whole war efficiently. I know your
roads. I drink on some of them and I hang my friend on the rest, and
they're the same road walked at different speeds, and I have been
declining it for two years, one morning at a time." She climbed the last
tread, eye to eye with the woman she had been building all her life in
the dark, and did what Wend's lore said no climber could survive: she met
her own eye and held it, and did not look away, and let the glass learn
her whole face — the shake, the grief, the mornings, all of it.

"Learn this too," she told it, quietly. "I would rather he live."

The reflection cracked from the eyes outward, like ice under a boot, and
was glass, and was nothing. Ede stood alone on the treads, and then she
climbed on, and where the black mirror caught her now it showed only a
tired woman climbing, which was all she had ever needed it to show.

Rhovan crossed the Silvered Gallery at the mid-stair, where the mirror-
walls crowded closest and the copies came out whether you met their eyes
or no, and his was waiting mid-span, leaning on a balustrade of black
glass with an elf's patience, and he knew it at once by the token.

The Wake-token hung whole at its breast. His own lay in two pieces at the
bottom of his pack, broken over his knee in front of the whole scattered
army on a wind floor half a year gone, and he had carried the pieces the
way you carry a tooth the farrier pulled — proof against the ache that it
was truly out.

"You kept it," Rhovan said.

"Kept," the reflection agreed. It had his voice, light and unbothered,
and it dealt its words the way he had dealt words for fourscore years:
one card at a time, face down. "Calyne lives, where I am."

"Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it. She lives." A fraction, dealt gently. "Her agent
never defied her on the wind floors. Never burned her secrets to buy a
spider's goodwill. Never gave her cause to spring the trap alone —" it
paused, an elf's pause, letting him do the arithmetic he had done ten
thousand times in the dark, "— so she did not spring it alone."

"And Berrin?"

"Serves the Wake now, unknowing. We hold his collar's ledger and we milk
it. The rising climbs; the Wake steers; the tower will fall in a decade
or three, when it falls to *us.*" It straightened off the balustrade, and
its eyes were his eyes, and they were perfectly restful. That was the
temptation, and it was real: not power. Rest. "Step in, brother. You were
never made for the open. A handled man never has to be right — only
accurate. He tells his fractions and sleeps. You told the whole of
something once, on the wind floors, in front of everyone, and you have
not slept since; I watch you at the fires, not sleeping. The glass keeps
the road back. She *lives,* Rhovan."

There it was, all of it, and the worst part was the accounting held. He
had turned, and Calyne had died springing the trap alone, and the man in
the glass had not, and she had not. A fraction of him — and he was made
of fractions, he had been made of fractions all his long life — reached
for the mirror.

He mastered it with the only weapon the copy could not have learned from
him, because he had used it exactly once.

"The whole truth, then," Rhovan said. "Since you've never told one, and
since it's the one move you don't know. I blackmailed Ede Stonhal at
Roothollow on the first morning and loved her before the Broodthrone.
I burned my
faction because a spider's contract was more honest than my handler's.
Calyne died proving Berrin because I was three floors away holding *her*
faction to *Ede's* war, and if the glass keeps every road, then keep
this: given all of it again, the wind floors, the token, the trap, the
grave —" his voice did not break; elves' voices do not break; it merely
told, for the second time in fourscore years, the entire truth at once,
"— I would turn again. I am not sorry. I have never once been sorry. I
grieve her and I am not sorry, and you cannot say that, and she knew it
of me before I did, and that is why her cipher-book went to Ede and her
last field-order went to me."

The reflection opened its mouth to deal the next fraction, and found,
visibly, that it held no cards at all — for it was the agent who never
turned, and everything it was ended at the turning. It cracked along the
token, and fell out of the world.

Rhovan stood a while in the gallery of black glass, one elf among a
thousand faint climbing shadows, none of them now his. "Field-order
received," he said to no one, and went up.

The stair gave onto the ninety-second floor: the mirror-galleries, halls
of tall silvered glass beyond lamp-reach, where the King hoarded the
stolen faces of the taken. Quicksilver ran in the gutters. The mirrors
were full. Vega Coalbrow, stooping under the arches, walked between
likenesses of people she did not know — some familiar, all wrong,
mouthing — and found Illa of Sheenrest by the Veiled Gallery, where the
draped mirrors stood turned to the wall and the face-robbed hid from
their own reflections.

"The Silvering Vault is below," Illa said. "Where they peel them. Every
face the tower ever took is shelved somewhere under our feet — the dead's
too. We can send them home when the master mirror breaks. But mind the
deep galleries, smith. The glass down there doesn't wear strangers."

It wore Vega. She met it in the last hall before the Vault: a mirror tall
as a forge-door, and in it herself — heavier, gilded, prosperous. Guild-
chains lay across the reflection's shoulders in soft gold loops, chains
worn as honors, and her forge-mark was stamped bright on the steel collar
it held up for inspection the way a smith holds her masterwork. The mark
Vega had spent twenty floors learning to look at, burnished here with
pride.

"Little sister," the reflection said — it was what Ulfar called her, and
the theft of it in her own mouth made her hands into fists. "You look
starved. Come in and eat. I kept the forge, you know, after you ran."

"I didn't run. I climbed."

"You *quit,*" the smith in the glass said, and its argument, when it
came, was not cruel; it was the argument Vega had been losing to herself
since the Adit, the one nobody else knew to make. "I stayed, and I
gentled the steel. My collars ride a finger looser. My welds take at a
lower heat — do you know what a lower heat spares a man at the welding?
I could not unmake the tower, so I stayed at its shoulder and I filed
its teeth, twenty years, ten thousand collars, each one kinder than the
smith before me would have made it. And when you quit, Vega — this is
just arithmetic, you were always the better mender but I was always the
better clerk — when the kind smith quits, a rougher one is hired. Every
collar since your climbing wears *his* iron, not mine. Add it up." The
reflection lowered the collar and its voice went gentle, and gentleness
was the trap, had always been the trap, for here was her own mouth
offering the mending. "Come inside and mend it from within. It's what
you're *for,* mender. Nothing has to break."

Vega stood a long time, the way giants do, and finished her thought
before she spoke, the way her mother had taught both children at the
trough.

"You are not broken," she said at last. "That's my answer. I have walked
up ninety floors mending — locks I forged, cages I marked, my brother,
half an army — and I have learned the one thing our mother's craft never
taught, because on her anvil it wasn't true: some things are not broken.
Some things are *made wrong,* whole and sound and wrong, and a made-
wrong thing in the shape of a mender is the wrongest thing in the tower.
You file its teeth so it can chew longer. You are not my shame, smith.
You're its masterpiece." She unshipped the forge-hammer from her back,
the old one, her mother's, that had mended pack-ice fleets and cage-
locks and never in this whole climb been swung to break; and the hammer,
she found, remembered its other work. "This is an eviction."

The mirror went the way mirrors go. Behind it, stacked in the dark on
shelves of lead, lay stolen faces beyond counting, silvered and sleeping
— and that, below, was mending, and she went down to it gladly, glass in
her braids, to spend the evening giving the dead and the blank-faced
back themselves.

Nix found his in the Grand Gallery itself, under the King's own image,
because of course the tower would spend its best glass on the smallest
target. The whispering courtier had tried him first in the corridors —
it had called him in Gnarl's voice, *cup-bearer, the King's cup is dry,*
and Nix had answered without breaking stride, "The King's dead, I was
there, terrible service at that court anyway," and left it whispering.
But the great glass was ready for him. In it stood a goblin with a
collar on, holding a full cup with both hands, safe.

Small. Warm. Alive. The cup-bearer who never dropped the cup.

"You're still there," Nix said. "In the throne-tent. Ninety floors
and you're still —"

"Still pouring," his reflection agreed. It talked fast, like him; it
counted, like him. "And count it out, go on, you love a count: kings
dead since I started pouring, none of mine. Cup-bearers dead at the
Kingsfield, none. Wars survived by standing at the elbow of whoever's
winning and topping him up: all of them, that's the count, *all of
them.* And your side of the sum?" Its eyes went to the standard on his
back, the banner he had picked up at floor ten to hide behind, and its
voice did the thing Gnarl's used to do, the music-hall pity. "One
banner, three feet of target. One parole, which is a leash with *your*
name on the other end too, don't think it isn't. One south door at one
feast where you counted the cooks — eleven and four, wasn't it, you keep
the fours, I know you keep the fours at night. Put it down, Nix. Come in
and pour. Nobody mourns a cup-bearer because nobody has to; that's the
whole warm arithmetic of the cup."

"That's a real number," Nix admitted. "The four. That's a real number,
I'm not going to stand here and say it isn't." He came closer to the
glass, and the standard's butt tapped the floor as he walked, tock,
tock, like a slow drum. "But you got my count
wrong, which is funny, because you're supposed to be me and I *never*
get the count wrong. You counted what I carried. You didn't count what
carried back. Freed kobolds who can say their own names because I sat
with them at the census-wall: two hundred and six. Floors that
surrendered to talking instead of dying, which is a thing a cup-bearer
knows how to do, it turns out, it's the same trade as pouring only both
ways: one whole warren, first in the history of the war. Collar-breaker
squads, floors eighty-one to eighty-four, collars: I stopped counting at
four thousand because my slate ran out, *my slate ran out.* And one
baker on a list, who is going to feed floor twenty for a year because a
goblin with three feet of target on his back will be standing there
counting the sacks." He looked up — way up — at the King's image hanging
over the gallery, and then at the safe warm goblin in the glass, and his
voice dropped out of the patter for exactly one sentence. "Gnarl died a
king of nothing. I'd rather die a nothing of somebody's."

His reflection had no answer, so it did what the tower always did when
talk failed: it offered. It held the cup out of the glass — truly out,
the silver-lichen letting its small hands and the brimming cup through
into the lamplight, the last warm door of the throne-tiers standing
open.

Nix took the cup. He weighed it, one beat, the way he'd weighed it the
day the banner-meadow burned.

Then he dropped it — the second time in his life, and this time on
purpose, and this time it wasn't fear — and when the cup broke on the
gallery floor the whole glass broke with it, top to bottom, and the
King's image cracked across, and from the Silvering Vault below came up
a sound like a thousand cage-doors opening at once as the stolen faces
flew home.

They gathered at the stair-lift behind the fallen mirror-wall when the
floor was freed: four climbers and no reflections, which was now a fact
about them and not a trick of the light. Nobody said much. There is not
much to say to people who have met you.

The lift-arch stood open on the dark going up, and out of it, faint,
came a warmth underfoot, a hum in the black glass, steady, patient, old
— the sound, Sliver said, of the thing every collar and weld and fever
in the tower was fed from, one floor above.

*The heart,* it said. *You have broken his mirrors. Now comes his fire.*

"Then it's Vega's floor," Ede said, and looked at her armorer, who was
looking up the shaft with her mother's hammer still in her hand, and her
brother's mathematics ahead of her in the dark.
