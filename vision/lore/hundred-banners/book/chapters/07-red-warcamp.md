# Chapter 7 — The Red Warcamp

Ede Stonhal had cut thirty names into the Stone of the Climb with her
own hand, in the winter she stopped drinking, one name a night because
her wrist would not stay true for more. Two years she had lived below a
tower with her company's tomb twenty floors up it. Now she stood in the
mine-dark under that floor, with the drums coming down through a
hundred feet of stone — boom, and boom, and boom, the march that never
departs — and she made herself say the truth of it in her own skull,
once, before she said anything to anyone else.

*Not a tomb. Skarn keeps cages. Some of them are alive, and you are
going to walk into the ground where you lost them, and this time the
plan has to be better than you were.*

*It is,* said Sliver, from her collar-bone, uninvited as always.
*I have audited it twice. It has one flaw only, which is that you
intend to be at the front of it.*

"That's the load-bearing part," Ede said, and went out to the lamps.

Three companies stood to in the assembly-gallery, and she walked the
line of them the way a smith walks a weld, looking for the cold spots.

The Muster of Bannerline held the right: pikes and bills and the good
brass horns, muster-folk who had waited a thousand years to finish
gathering and did not intend to be late for it, Serjeant Mella Rung at
their head with the True Color cased on her shoulder. They would go at
the warcamp's gate — the front door, the loud door — with the rams and
the two ballistae built on the Gear Galleries' beds. Their work was to
be believed. Skarn had to read them as the whole attack, and he would,
because a frontal storm against a shield-wall was exactly the mistake
the tower expected of freed farmers.

The Wrights held the center: miners of the freed adits, machinists of
Sprocket Row, millers of Wheelhouse with their powder-casks slung in
cradles, vault-crews of Half-Light, Grib Sprocket standing among them
listening to the drums the way he listened to bearings. Their war was
underneath. The giants' mine had a throat the orcs had never dug and
did not know: the main downshaft, capped and pitched over with tents.
The Wrights had found the cap from beneath — giants' work answers
giants — and packed the seams with the millers' weir-charge. Their work
was the door in the floor.

And the Van held the left, which meant it held the shaft: Ede's own,
the smallest of the three and the strangest muster she had ever
signed — veterans of nine freed floors, three Red Orc deserters out of
the Grinding Pit, an elf of the Grey Rides with his quiver full and his
opinions filed, a forge-matron with four hammers, and a goblin with a
banner. Their work was the pens. Up the shaft the heartbeat after it
opened, through the tent-lines while the camp's face was at the gate,
and open the cages before any blade in that camp remembered what
hostages are for.

No Cullers. West of her fires, holding the taken gates below, exactly
as ordered — and the lack of them sat in the plan where a reserve
should have been, and she had signed that too, and would sign it again.
An army that kills the surrendered is not a reserve. It is a second
enemy, waiting for your worst hour.

She stopped at the Van's front, where Vega knelt re-wrapping the haft
of the smallest hammer, and Nix stood under the grey banner with the
glow-sick kobold child asleep against the pole, and she said what
captains say, which is nothing anyone remembers after, and then the
thing she had climbed two years to say.

"The people in those cages watched me leave this floor without them.
Whatever they say to me when the doors open — that's mine to stand.
Your work ends at the locks. Understood?"

"Understood," said Vega, "though I will tell you, captain, that you
have described my work also, and we will see whose account is paid
first."

*Wrathsmoke on the wind above,* said Sliver. *The camp's lichen
breathes rage. You have each had the antidote; you will each still
feel it. When your temper rises tonight, count it as weather.*

"Weather," said Nix. "Right. Lovely. I'm counting."

Far above, muffled by the mountain, the Muster's horns sounded at the
warcamp's gate, and the drums above went from march to storm, every
frame in the camp waking at once, the whole floor becoming one
instrument.

"Wrights," said Ede. "Open the mountain."

⁂

The charge went up through her boot-soles first, then through the
world.

The gallery breathed dust. Somewhere over their heads a hundred tons
of cap-stone and tent-ground stood up into the drum-light and sat
back down as a hole, and the shaft drank the war-noise of the camp
down into the dark like a horn blown backward. The Wrights' ladder-
crews were moving before the stone finished falling — up the old
tool-cut wall-stairs, giants first, rigging the winch-frames — and Ede
went up the ropes in the second wave with her shield slung, into the
smoke, into the red.

The Red Warcamp burned its light out of watch-fires and drum-tower
braziers, and the stone of the stolen mine-hall threw it all back
bloody. Tent-lines ran away in every direction, hide and pole, guy-
ropes like a snare-wood; the drum-towers stood over them, four great
frames of lashed timber, each with its team of kobolds beating the
storm; and at the gallery's cut end, lamplit and terrible, the cages:
barred galleries stacked two high into the mine's old workings, and
the shapes of people in them, gripping the bars, because the floor was
shaking and the horns were the wrong horns and something in the middle
of the camp had just become a hole.

Ede came out of that hole with her shield up and her point low, and
for ten heartbeats the plan was perfect: the camp's whole face was at
the gate, the alarm was a gate alarm, and the Van poured out of the
floor behind her unmet, spreading into the lanes, running toward the
pens with the grey banner jogging above them like a gull over a wake.

Then the camp turned around.

Not all of it. Enough. Some serjeant of Skarn's with better ears than
his drums deserved read the new noise in the middle of his camp, and
the lanes ahead of her filled with the honor-guard — Red Orcs in
welded plate, first-taken and hardest-collared, disciplined as
millwork, coming on with their shields locked and no war-cry at all,
which was the frightening thing. The Pressed do not
fight for anything. They fight because the collar leaves no other
door, and it makes them terrible, and it makes killing them a wound
you deal yourself.

"Wedge!" Ede called, and the Van made its wedge, and the two walls
met between the tents.

Shield-work in a tent-lane is millstone work: no room, no light, no
quarter asked because there is no breath to ask it with. Ede took a
blow on the boss that numbed her arm to the shoulder, gave the point
back over the rim, felt it bite. Beside her a Grinding Pit deserter
caught an axe out of the air the way pit-fighters learn to and paid
its owner in chain-scarred iron. An orc's face came over her shield-
rim close enough to share breath — young, furious, terrified, the
fever-smoke riding him — and she killed him, and hated the tower with
a completeness she had not managed even for Gnarl, because this was
the bill it ran up every day it stood: a death that should not have
been needed, dealt by her own hand, again.

*Left lane's clearing,* said Sliver, cold and quick. *The champion is
coming to you. Twenty of its paces. It walks through tents.*

The warframe came through the tent-row like weather through a hedge —
an orc champion once, and none of that left but the height of it,
welded into a walking siege-frame, a mockery in plate, the camp's one
soldier with no collar because it needed none, having no self left to
collar. The lane cleared before it. Her line bent.

"Vega!" Ede called, going back a step, and another, buying the lane
with ground. "Your trade!"

The forge-matron came up the lane already swinging — not at the
frame, Ede saw, never at the frame's strength, but at its joints, at
the welds, hammer-work called in a language of blows: this seam, that
hinge, the knee, the knee, *the knee*. "It is only made!" Vega was
saying as she worked, loud and level, teaching the whole Van in the
middle of the fight the way she'd teach an apprentice-hall. "It is
made, and badly, in a hurry, by the yard — nothing made cannot be
UN-made — " and the last blow went in under the frame's knee-weld
with everything a mountain owes its daughter, and the champion came
down like scaffolding, and what leaked out of the frame as it broke
was not blood but a scrap of the King's will, thin as smoke, draining
away down the shaft as if called home. An eviction. The only mercy
that thing could still be paid.

The lane stood open. At its end stood the cages.

⁂

Afterward, the histories made the locks the quiet part, and the
histories were wrong. The locks were the battle. Everything else was
noise to cover them.

Vega worked the first pen's lock with her eyes shut, hands flat on
the plate, reading her own steel like a letter she'd posted twenty
years before — and swore, once, in the mountain tongue, because it
was good work, because it was hers, because the tower had bought her
best and gotten it. Then she went in through the flaw she had left in
the ward-spring in a leaner, honester year, and the lock gave up its
whole pride at once, and the door swung, and the first of Skarn's
cages stood open on a gallery of staring, starved, unbroken giants.

"OUT," Vega thundered, in the voice that fills doorways. "Dropped
plate at the fires — arm yourselves and turn this camp!"

And Shieldwall came out.

Not her thirty. Ede knew that even then, counting faces at the bars
faster than any battle should have let her — the floor's own folk
first, miners and shield-folk of the seized gallery, Dara Shieldwall
at their head with her fists full of a gaoler's keys she'd lifted in
the first confusion, already running for the second pen. But among
them, threaded through them, in the wrong-colored rags of a company
two years dead: her people. A face from the south wall at the
Warcamp. A face from the baggage-line. A woman who had stood the
rearguard of the rout with her, shoulder to shoulder, the last time
this floor had Ede Stonhal in it.

They came out of the cages, and took up the dropped warplate of their
own gaolers, and the Red Warcamp began — there is no other word, and
the histories for once found the right one — to *turn*. Every pen
Vega and Dara opened was a squad the camp lost and the rising gained,
armed at the enemy's own fires. The Muster's rams had the gate
groaning. The drum-storm was tearing at its own rhythm now, orders
crossing orders.

It was Nix who silenced it. Later she pieced it together from a
dozen tellings: the goblin at the third drum-tower with the banner
planted at its foot, scrambling up the lashed frame to the kobold
drummers collared to their drums — and the working scratched into the
wall of the Cage Pens, the fragment Vega had found with her hands
flat on the steel and read aloud once, wondering: *Break-collar,
written rough, by some caged hand that never got to speak it.* Costly
still — speaking it over the drum-team's collars one at a time took
Vega grey, each collar the price of a day at the anvil — but after
that night it was no longer rare. It was learned. The rising owned
it, and would be assembling the strength to speak it at scale for
thirty floors to come.

The drums stopped. One frame, then the next, then all four — collars
snapping like frost-bound branches, drummers' hands falling still for
the first time in years — and into that silence, enormous, came the
sound the drums had been drowning all along: the gate coming down,
and the Muster's horns, and under the horns a floor's worth of freed
voices finding out together what they sounded like.

*The shield-wall,* said Sliver. *He is still at the stair, Ede. He
has not moved. He is waiting for you.*

⁂

Warlord Skarn stood before the stair-lift at the gallery's cut end,
behind the shield-wall of welded plate that was the last unbroken
thing in his camp, and watched his war turn inside out, and did not
move, because the stair was his post and the post was his collar and
the collar — she understood, walking toward him down the cleared
lane, and the understanding was the worst thing that floor did to
her — the collar was his *honor*.

He was huge even for his kind, first-taken, older than his scars. His
honor-guard had thinned around him; some lay where the pens' freed
squads had met them; some looked at the opened cages, and at the
silent drum-frames, and broke ranks at last, backing away from the
stair with their shields still up, deserting in good order, the only
way orcs ever desert. He let them go. He said something after them in
the Red tongue that was not a curse, and let them go.

"Warlord," Ede said.

"Captain." He knew her. Something in her went through ice at it. Two
years, and the cages, and he knew her at a word. "You climbed short,
last time. The King said you would come again. The King is never
wrong about Men; it is his study."

"Your camp's turned. Your garrison's freeing itself by the pen-load.
It's finished, Skarn." She kept her point low. Behind her she could
hear the Van coming up the lane and stopping, because she had raised
her left hand, because this next part she owed no audience. "The word
that snaps collars is loose on this floor. First-taken, same as the
goblins, same as the kobolds you drummed — none of this was ever
yours to want. Put down the wall, let us speak the working, and walk
out of this camp a free soldier — the first Red Orc in an age to own
his own mornings. Enough has been done tonight that can't be undone.
Not this."

And Skarn laughed — not cruelly, that was the unbearable part, but
the way a veteran laughs at a recruit's honest, hopeless question —
and hefted the great cleaver-blade that was his rank's regalia.

"Free," he said, the way you'd say a word in a language you'd once
heard traders use. "Little captain. The King took me up out of the
mud himself. His own hands, this collar. Do you know what a collar
*is*, below? It is that they will feed you before the uncollared. It
is that your word moves ten thousand spears. It is the King saying:
*you, of all the mud, I will keep.* You call it a chain because no
one ever offered you one. It is a *rank*. It is the only rank the
world ever gave my people — and you would have me lay it down and be
mud again, and call the mud mornings." He brought the cleaver up to
guard, and his voice went formal, parade-ground, almost gentle.
"Come and take my post, captain of Shieldwall. That is how this is
done between soldiers."

*He cannot hear you,* said Sliver, very quietly. *The Yoke is out of
the floor, Ede. What is left is only what he built to survive it.
Some collars we forge ourselves, of what they did to us, and no
working written on any wall will open those.*

"I know," Ede said, to both of them.

She went and took his post.

It was a long fight and she remembered it short: plate and cleaver
against shield and point, his strength against her years of knowing
exactly what her own body would forgive, the shield-wall's welded
plates screaming where his missed strokes met them. He fought like
what he was — a conscript made general, the tower's best pupil,
collared so long the collar had grown a soldier around itself — and
she killed him the way you close a door on winter: braced, both
hands, all the way to the latch. Under the last of it he said
something in the Red tongue to no one — a report, by the shape of
it; a post, handed over — and the histories can argue forever about
what she owed him and whether she wept, because the smoke was thick,
and the lamps were behind her, and it was nobody's business but the
floor's.

Warlord Skarn died at his stair believing his collar had been a
rank, because no one in a hundred floors of the tower's mercy had
ever been given less reason to believe anything else. She stood over
him and made herself say it, silently, the way the shardminds taught:
*a death that should not have been needed.* Then the seam of the
stair-lift split behind the shield-wall with a sound like a held
breath let go, and the second tier of the Ascent stood open, and
Ede Stonhal turned her back on it, because the stair was not the
thing she had climbed this floor for.

⁂

The cages stood open. All of them — Dara's keys and Vega's hands had
finished the work while the warlord held her at the stair, and the
pens' galleries gaped, and the freed were everywhere in the burning
camp, arming, weeping, calling names into the smoke and being
answered, or not.

Ede walked down the line of the pens with her sword still bare,
because her hands had not caught up to the war
being over, and the count she had come twenty floors to take came to
meet her, face by face out of the dark. Shieldwall lived. Not whole —
nothing the tower touches stays whole; there were pens at the line's
end standing empty in a way that had nothing of tonight in it, and on
a post by the emptied galleries a manifest nailed up in the tower's
neat clerking hand, *consigned upward, in good order, to the high
works* — and she would climb with that paper folded over her heart
for thirty floors, and it would cost the tower more than any army
did. But here, now, in the wreck of Skarn's camp: her people, alive,
freed, standing in the firelight in gaoler's plate with their own
company's grey banner — Nix had planted it at the pens' head, and
stood by it, small and smoke-black and counting the freed out loud
because counting was how he wept — coming out of two years of cages
to find the woman who had led them into this floor standing in the
ashes of it with a drawn sword and no speech in her at all.

A one-armed man came out of the third pen last, slow, unhurried, as
if cages were a thing that happened to other people. Dun Harrow. Her
standard-bearer. The arm he'd carried her colors with two years gone,
gone. He looked at her a long moment across the fire-lane, the whole
camp falling quiet around the look, and in it she read everything the
histories would spend a chapter on: that being freed is not the same
as forgiving, that half that silence believed she had sold them, that
the account between a captain and her caged company does not open at
the lock. He looked at her, and at the banner, and back, and said
nothing at all.

Tomorrow this camp would have to decide what she was.

Tonight the drums were silent, the pens were iron scrap, the second
tier stood open overhead, and in the space where the march that never
departs had beaten for a generation, a floor's worth of the freed
were learning the sound of their own gathering — the one sound the
tower was built to prevent, rising through the smoke of the Red
Warcamp like heat off a forge that would not now go out.

Shieldwall lived. The rest was owed, and morning would come
collecting.
