# Chapter 46 — The Last Door

They took the antechambers in two days of walking and one blow.

Ede had feared the ninety-fourth floor the way she had learned to fear all
the Made's quiet, and it was quiet: a honeycomb of waiting-rooms running
back bench by bench to the vanishing point, summons-bells ringing numbers
that were recalled the same breath they were called. The waiting itself had
a body there — a grey chamberlain grown out of a lifetime of tokens and
unread petitions, seated at the last desk before the throne-approach — and
it did not fight the host so much as file it. Nib of Doorward taught the
vanguard the door-art called Precedence, and the rising walked the whole
length of the queue the wrong way, and Vega's hammer broke the desk like a
bad promise. What Ede kept of that floor was not the eviction. It was an
old woman of the taken standing up off a bench she had warmed for
eighty years, looking at the brass token in her fist — her number, her
whole life's wait — and letting it fall. The sound it
made on the marble was small. The sound of ten thousand more was not.
Nib's people brought the taken's unread petitions up out of the Filing
Crypt, the case against the King.

The trophy vaults took two days and cost more. The ninety-fifth floor was
everything Vharuk had ever taken, shelved: crowns of drowned kingdoms, the
standards of freed floors captured back, the arms of heroes labelled like
seed-stock. Down one grim aisle, which Vess of Hoardside would not walk
with her eyes raised, stood the gear of every climber who had come before
and failed, each set named for its dead owner — and at the aisle's end an
empty plinth, dusted, patient, its label blank. Ede stood before the blank
space a long while. Then she took the label off the plinth and put it in
her coat, and Vess laughed, a rusted terrible
sound, the first laugh that floor had heard in an age, and the curators
began to empty the shelves. The rising armed itself out of the King's own
museum. Hero-steel with names on it went back into living hands; the failed
climbers of eighty years were named at last and laid to rest; the
hoard-thing that rose to stop it was evicted with its own exhibits.

The night gardens Ede would not speak of afterward, and no one who crossed
them spoke of them either. The ninety-sixth floor was beautiful. That was
the whole crime of it. Black trees in flower,
terraces, fountains running sweet — and in every pale bloom, if you looked,
a face, because the King had grown his one garden out of the taken,
planted living in the black beds and coaxed to flower. Elys of Bloomside
walked the ranks of the rising with a waking-draught and a spade and taught
them the un-rooting, and for a day and a night the army of the hundred
banners knelt in flowerbeds and dug people out of the soil with their
hands. Vega withered the great flower at the garden's heart with the word
Elys gave her, and the faces in its petals went still, and were freed, and
some of them woke. Ede had not wept at the Warcamp cages. She had not wept
at the Frosthall. She wept there, in the dirt, and was not the only
general who did.

The Kingsway they took singing. Three days' march of white marble
climbed the last tiers, lined with the King's own likeness a thousand
times over, the Theft carved in stone as a triumph — a floor built to
make a climber worship before ever seeing the throne. But the masons
who cut it had hidden the truth in the plinths, low, where the King
would never stoop, and Cord of Wayside read it out loud as the host
came on: no royal line, no god, a usurper of nothing. Then the masons
taught the rising which colossi stood hollow, and brought their own
life's work down across the avenue, statue after statue, with a cry
and a crash and a cheer. The host walked the last mile past broken
idols, unawed.

The court was worst, and shortest. On the ninety-eighth floor there were no
collars to break, no fever to lift, no welds to cut. The courtiers of the
gilded tiers had never been taken. They had chosen him, and fattened on
him, and when Bly's servants stilled the hall's glamour with the truth-art
called Silence, what stood exposed in the cold candle-light was only
people — jewelled, willing, unrepentant people. The Grand Vizier laughed at
the offer of mercy, because he had never been bound and would do it all
again, and so the climb did the thing it had spent ninety-seven floors
learning not to do, and judged. Ede gave the order herself and slept badly
after. Below, everything they had fought was a prisoner wearing a
monster. Here the monsters had worn
people. Bly's servants read their long ledger of the court's crimes aloud
over the ruin, and then folded it, and carried it up — so that when the King
answered, everyone would hear.

Five floors in nine days. Behind the host every gate-town emptied upward,
until the rising no longer swept the throne-tiers so much as rose through
them, the way water rises through a house. Above was one floor of the Made,
and then the summit, and between the two, the Door.

The ninety-ninth floor was one room.

Ede came up the last stair into a hall so vast the torchlight of the
vanguard could not find its walls, and stopped, because everyone stopped;
the whole climb narrowed to this point and every soldier felt it in the
soles of their feet. There was no land here. No hedgerow, no ice, no
gallery of gears. There was dim stone underfoot, and pale vigil-fungus
shining in one long shadow, and at the hall's far end, filling it from
floor to unseen roof, the Door.

It was black. Not the black of night, which is only the absence of lamps,
but the black of adamant and King-steel and welded sorrow, a wall raised to
seal the throne away from all the world below. And the black was not
smooth. Ede walked toward it, and the nearer she came the more the surface
resolved, until she stood at the Weld-Face itself and understood what she
was looking at, and took off her helm without deciding to.

The Door was made of the fallen. Liberators, welded into the metal where
they fell, an age of them, their shapes raised faintly out of the black
like figures under ice, their names still legible where the weld had taken
them mid-stride, mid-swing, mid-word. Rhovan came and read beside her, and
said nothing, which from Rhovan was a whole speech. Some of the names were
in scripts the census-wall had taught her. Some were in no script anyone
living knew.

"You're reading them," said a voice below her elbow. "Good. Most climbers
won't. Most climbers look once and go grey and start talking about seams."

The keeper of Lastward was a small bent man named Hollen, and his people
were a handful — the last of the bound, kept in this hall to tend the Door
that entombed their own greatest hero, kept, he said, as a living reproach.
He led the captains along the Weld-Face, past the doorward imps that sealed
its seams and the grey weld-wights that rose out of the metal to turn back
the living as they had been turned back. And at the Door's very centre,
above the sill, flanked by a faceless sentinel of dead-black adamant and by
one armored figure that stood guard the way only a living soldier stands,
he stopped, and pointed up.

She was in the Door.

Taller than the figures around her, raised half out of the black metal like
a ship's figurehead, a woman in an old harness with her empty hands open at
her sides and her face turned down toward the sill she had died — no. Not
died. Ede looked at the open hands and the bent head and knew, the way she
knew a sleeping sentry from a dead one, that the woman in the metal was
alive.

"The Champion," Hollen said. "The greatest liberator who ever climbed. She
came up an age ago with the whole tower at her back, same as you, and she
reached this hall, and at the last step they took her alive.
He didn't kill her. He made her the lock." The old man's jaw worked.
"Everyone says she's gone. I've kept this floor my whole life on the
belief that everyone is wrong."

"She fights?" Ede asked.

"She fights," Hollen said. "With everything she was. The binding drives
her. But she doesn't wish it — that's the difference, that's everything.
Malgrim clutched his chain. The Vizier laughed at you. Her, they had to
weld." He looked up at the bent head. "A captive who still wishes to be
free always can be. If that's not true, none of this was."

Sliver was warm at Ede's throat, and had been silent since the stair.

"Her name," Ede said. "You keep it."

"I keep where it's buried," said Hollen. "Under the sill. They put her
name and her own arms in the ground when they welded her, so no one could
call her back. Come and dig."

The Threshold Undercroft was a vault beneath the Door's own sill, and the
things in it had been buried with hate and preserved with care, which told
Ede more about the King than the Kingsway had. A harness of plain steel,
old past reckoning, kept oiled. A soldier's kit with a stoppered draught
in it, saved and never spent. A sword — and Ede, lifting it, went still,
because the sword was plain and notched and heavy in the way of a blade
used all its life by someone who knew what it was for, and on the flat of
its pommel was cut a device she had carried on her own shoulder for twenty
years and behind her own banner for two: a grey wall, and a single star
above it.

"Sliver," she said quietly.

The splinter's voice was a long time coming, and when it came it was drier
than she had ever heard it, which for Sliver was grief.

*"Her name is Aldis Bright,"* Sliver said. *"She founded the first banner of
the Shieldwall, an age ago, in a village that is under your Roothollow now.
I have carried forty-one climbers, Ede Stonhal. She was my fourth. She was
the first of them stubborn enough to matter."*

Nix, who had come down uninvited because that was how Nix came everywhere,
looked from the sword to Ede's shoulder and back, said nothing at all for
once in the whole climb, and went back up to fetch the banner.

They gave the fight everything, because the Door gave them no other terms.

The doorward imps sealed the seams and Nix's collar-breakers freed them
one by one, and at every collar that cracked, a seam gave. The weld-wights
rose grey out of the metal, and the host broke them gently as it had
learned to break the Wrongmade, and the freed of them drained into the
adamant and rested. The sentinel of the sill came apart under Vega's
hammer to dead metal that rang for a hundred heartbeats after.

The Champion fought as Hollen had promised, with everything she was, and
everything she was had nearly won this tower once. She fought from the
Door and of the Door, half-freed of the metal and lashed back into it, and
her open hands were empty no longer; the binding had given her a blade of
black weld and it moved the way water moves. She killed no one. Ede saw it
by the third exchange and shouted it down the line: the binding drove her
sword and something under the binding was turning every stroke a
half-hand wide. An age of nights in the metal, and the woman inside was
still fighting the King with the only inch he'd left her.

The armored figure at her side was another matter, and nearly ended
Rhovan, until Nix went in under its shield with the Break-collar word and
the chantry-priests sang Recant behind him, and the Champion's own
lieutenant — taken with her at the last step, collared to fight at her
side forever — came free mid-stroke, dropped guard, dropped blade,
and turned to stand between his welded commander and her own descending
sword. He took the flat of it and held.

"Now," Ede said.

Hollen's people had climbed the scaffold at the Lintel, the last handful
of the bound, and they called her name into the Door. Not
shouted it. Called it, the way you call someone home across fields at
dusk. Aldis. Aldis Bright. The weld took the name and shivered. Vega laid
both palms against the black seam where the woman met the metal, and spoke
the capstone word, the one the whole Made had taught them syllable by
syllable — Unrivet, Unseam, Mirrorbreak, gathered into Unmake — and the
oldest weld in the world let go like frost off a morning line. The priests
sang the Recant of the King's hold. Ede stood on the sill with the plain
notched sword held up hilt-first, and said, herself, last, quietly, into
the opening metal:

"Aldis Bright. You're stood down. Come out."

And the greatest hero of a dead age stepped down out of the Last Door
alive, and put her hand on the offered hilt, and stood swaying on the sill
in the torchlight of ten thousand people who had all, every one of them,
stopped breathing.

She was grey where the songs said gold, and thin as a lath, and the weld
had left its lines on her the way rivers leave lines on a country. She
drank the draught her own kit had kept for her across an age, because
Hollen put it in her hand and glared until she did. She asked for the date
and was given it and took the answer like a blade going in, standing up.
She said the lieutenant's name — Tam — and Tam wept, and she did not,
which seemed to cost more.

Then she saw the banner.

Wat Kello of the thirty carried it now, the man freed at the Welding
Halls, iron from the jaw down, who had carried it since the Herald's Gate
and let no one spell him. He had come to the sill unbidden, because Nix had
fetched him, and the old cloth hung still on its pole: a grey wall, a
single star.

Aldis Bright looked at it for ten heartbeats. Ede watched an age-old face
do sums.

"That is my banner," she said. Her voice was rust and courtesy. "I sewed
the star crooked. It is still crooked." She looked at Wat, at the iron
jaw, at the way he held the pole one-handed and would plainly die before
dropping it, and something in her began, very slowly, to stand back up.
"It never fell, then. All this time."

"It fell twice," Ede said, because the debt did not deserve a lie. "It
fell at the Red Warcamp, when I lost the company that bore it. A man with
one arm carried it out of the cages when we broke them, and bore it up
forty floors, and died keeping the north door of a burning hall so that
others could run. I got the
company back. Thirty of them are in this hall."

"Show me," said Aldis Bright.

So Ede gave her the muster-roll — the rebuilt roll, the one she had written
out of her own memory over a traitor's protests, thumbed and rain-marked
and carried against her heart from the Cindermouth to the Last Door. The
Champion read it the way quartermasters read: fast, and twice. A hundred
banners. Thirty-one peoples and more since. Goblins mustered beside Men,
keepers and Pressed and Tollmen and giants, column after column of the one
thing the tower had been built so the world could never make.

She was a long time over it. When she looked up, it was not at the roll's
keeper but down the hall, at the host, at the light on all those faces
going back farther than light could go.

"Who commands?" she asked.

And it was not Ede who answered, and not Rhovan, and not the keeper of the
floor. It was the thirty — the freed of Shieldwall, the ones who had spent
a winter half-believing their own captain had sold them, standing together
down the sill the way they had stood in the cages, and they answered as
one, the way a company answers at roll:

"Stonhal."

Aldis Bright, founder of the Shieldwall, first soldier of the oldest
banner in the world, turned to the woman who had lost her company and
rebuilt it into the gathered world, and brought the notched blade up
before her face, edge outward, in a form of salute so old that no living
company used it — and every soldier in that hall knew it anyway, and ten
thousand blades and billhooks and hammers and spade-hafts came up in
answer down the whole length of the ninety-ninth floor.

Ede returned it. She did not trust her voice, so she used the one that
carried.

"Shieldwall," she said. "Fall in."

Behind them, unbarred, unguarded, open for the first time in an age, the
Last Door stood on darkness and a rising stair. Somewhere at the top of it
a man who had never once been made to wait was waiting.

The oldest soldier in the world fell in beside Ede Stonhal, and the host
came on.
