# Chapter 27 — The Eye

Nix heard the silence before he saw it, which he would have told you
was impossible, wrong, and exactly what happened.

For three floors the world had been roar — wind like a mill, wind
like a war, wind you chewed before you breathed it — and then the
four of them came through a seam in the eye-wall behind a freed
courtier who knew the way, and the roar did not fade. It stopped.
One step, roar. Next step, nothing. His ears rang, and he counted his
own heartbeats, one, two, three, because when the world did something
impossible you counted what you could.

The eye of the great storm was a bowl of gold light, wide as a day's
walk. The rain did not fall in it; the rain *hung* — beads of bright
water strung on nothing, close as barley in a field, and when Nix
put out his hand and touched one it broke on his finger, warm, and
did not wet the ones beside it. Still trees stood in ranks, not one
leaf turning. Hush-grass grew silver over everything, and no wind
had bent it in a thousand years.

It was the most beautiful place he had seen in seventy-eight floors,
and everything in him that had lived four years under a collar stood
up in his skull and said, small friend, this is a trap.

— A trap, yes, — said the woman who came out of the trees, cheerful,
as if agreeing with a thing he'd said aloud. She was grey and brisk
and small for one of Men, with a rope of little copper bells across
her chest that made no sound at all when she walked. — The calm is
the trap. Out in the wall the wind kills you in a breath, and in
here nothing kills you at all. You just sit down. Rest your eyes.
And never get up again. Mera
Lullside; I keep the eye. You'd be the rising, then. — She looked
them over: the general grey as ash, the giant, the nameless elf,
the goblin with a banner twice his height. — Four of you. Ahead of
the whole host. Brave, that. The eye takes the tired first, and I
never in my life saw anyone tireder.

She gave them the rules on the walk to Lullside, and Nix counted
those too, because they were rules you counted or died of. One: no
one sits alone. Two: hands busy, always — the eye can't lull a hand
that's working. Three: mark the lull-poppies, pale in the hush-
grass, and step wide; what you smell in them is your own funeral,
made comfortable. Four: the Sharpwake draught, morning and evening,
bitter as a debt — Nix drank his and his whole face mutinied, and
Mera said the bitterness was the mercy; sweetness you'd stop
noticing.

At Lullside her folk lived close to the eye-wall on purpose, in the
skirts of the roar, using the noise the way other towns used walls.
They were weather-readers and rain-wrights, who had fought the
drowse by habit for a thousand years, and they took the advance-
party into the Barometer Vault — the one built place the
lull could not reach, cool stone and a great storm-glass taller
than Vega — and showed Ede the eye-wall's turning, and the one safe
seam by which an army could be fed into the calm, stage by stage,
three days hence.

— They can rest here, — Mera said, and tapped the storm-glass. — I
mean that two ways, general. It's the only rest on this tier. And
it wants to be the last one. We'll walk your camps every hour.
You'll hate my bells before you leave. It'll mean you're alive.

⁂

The army came through the seam by regiments, and the eye did to
every one of them what it had done to Nix: stopped their ears with
kindness and buckled their knees.

Nix watched it happen from the top of a supply-crate, keeping his
tally. He had appointed himself to the work because rule two was
hands busy, and no work of his mattered more. Two years he had
counted this army — up chains, across ice, out of cages — and what
came through the eye-wall those three days had the walk of an army
at the very end of its rope. Blown, gathered, marched, grieved.
Soldiers stepped into the gold light and wept without stopping
walking. A whole company of freed kobolds lay down in the hush-
grass in march-order, in one
motion, like grass going down under a scythe, and had to be walked
awake again by Mera's people, gently, with bells.

Ede put the camps where Lullside said: rings within rings, every
fire double-watched, the standards planted at the center where the
suspended rain caught the light and made a slow, drifting crown of
bright beads above the banners. The rising slept in a circle of
standing rain. And for three days, for the first time since the
Frosthall, nobody died.

The eye had its price all the same; it showed them on the second
day what sitting down cost. Off the camps' western ring lay a
garden — the Sitting Garden, Mera called it, and walked them
through it herself, one hand on her bells — where the calm's first
captives had sat down to rest a thousand years ago and never
risen. They were still there. Folk of the old sky-realm, seated in
the silver grass among the hanging rain, breathing. Their clothes
had gone to moss and their hair to lichen and they breathed, one
breath to the army's hundred, their faces peaceful the way carvings
are peaceful, and pale drifting figures moved among them like
hosts among guests — pressure-ghosts, the seated who had finally
stopped even breathing, welcoming the living to lie down beside
them.

And at the garden's edge stood a courtier.

Nix knew the collar before he knew the man — you always knew the
collar first; it was the point of collars. One of Zephyra's own
court, first-taken, silk gone to rags, standing in the hush-grass
with a serene and terrible courtesy, and as they passed he turned
to Nix, of all of them to Nix, and bowed, and gestured to the
grass with a hand like a dry leaf: *sit, small
master. Rest. You have carried that banner such a long way.*

The banner-staff was in Nix's hands. Dun Harrow had carried it up
forty floors with one arm, and since the feast Nix had carried it
because no one else could bear to, and it was twice his height and
he had never once put it down where anyone could see.

— Can't, — Nix told the Pressed man, hoarse. — It doesn't sit, see.
Flags don't. It's the one thing they're for, staying up. I just
hold on and get carried, me.

They broke the man's collar that evening, Vega's hands and a
chisel of anchor-chain steel — and the freed courtier fell out of
his serenity like a man dropped from a height, and wept, and could
say only
that the eye was quiet, so quiet, he had been sent to it for
quiet — and then he slept, truly slept, guarded, with a bell by
his head. Nix sat by him the first watch, holding the banner
upright, counting the man's breaths as they slowed from a
courtier's to a sleeper's. Nineteen to the hundred. Twenty. Rule
one: no one sits alone.

⁂

They buried the dead on the third day.

The gathering had given back eleven hundred living and, at the
last, forty-one bodies, carried up the tier on the skiffs because
Ede would not leave them to the wind floors; and there were the
never-found besides, three hundred and twelve names with nothing
under them at all. Lullside offered the ground: nothing rots in
the eye, Mera said; nothing is troubled; the hush-grass closes
over a grave like a hand over a
coin, and keeps it.

So the rising dug in the silver grass, in the gold light, among
the hanging rain. It dug forty-one graves and three hundred and
twelve small pits the size of a helm, one for each name with no
body, and into each pit went something — a bowstring, a die, a
spoon, a plait of chain-moss, whatever the dead one's mess-mates
had kept. The peoples made their rites in their own tongues, nine
kinds of grief in one garden, and the eye hushed all of them
equally, and the suspended rain hung over the graves like the sky
holding its breath.

It was Nix who started the song. He did not decide to; his mouth
did.

Dun's muster-song. Every soldier of Shieldwall had known it, and
every soldier of the rising had learned it since, in the cages or
on the ice or walking the chains — the counting-song the one-armed
standard-bearer had drummed on his own staff at every muster from
floor twenty to floor sixty, plain as bread, older than anyone
knew:

*One for the gate-post, two for the wall,
three for the shield that shoulders them all;
count off, count off, and answer the stone —
nobody musters alone.*

His voice cracked on the first line. It was a small voice at the
best of times, a cup-bearer's voice, built for announcing wine —
and the eye drank it, the way it drank everything, hush-grass and
soft gold swallowing the notes almost before they left him. He
sang the second line anyway, because you finished a count once you
had started it, Dun had beaten that into the whole army, you never
left a count hanging —

— and the graves' whole company answered the stone.

The rising sang. Quietly — it could not be loud, nothing in the
eye could be loud — but ten thousand voices low is not a sound at
all, it is a weather, and the counting-song rolled around the bowl
of gold light in a slow ring, verse on verse, gate-post and wall
and shield, name after name folded into the counting the way the
song had always taken names, *four for Dun Harrow, who held the
north door* — and Nix, singing, felt the eye change around them.

The stillness pushed back. He felt it the way you feel a hill
under a cart: a great, patient, downward softness leaning on the
song, on the mourners, on every busy hand — the lull gathering
itself at the garden the way it had gathered on sitting folk for a
thousand years, the pressure-ghosts drifting in from the silver
grass, drawn to grief as to tiredness, and among them, rising out
of the Still Point at the garden's heart like cream rising in a
pan, something vast and calm and almost kind — the stillness
itself with a shape on, the thing Lullside's folk would not name,
the welded peace of the whole eye, come to welcome the rising to
rest.

And the song met it, and the song was a *muster*.

That was the whole of the miracle, and Nix understood it even as
his neck-hair stood: the eye had one art, and the art was rest,
and Dun's song was the one human thing built exactly contrary — a
count that demands an answer, a music you cannot sit down in. The
seated of the Sitting Garden stirred. Breath by thousand-year
breath, moss cracking along their shoulders, folk who had rested
since before Roothollow had a name lifted their heads at the old,
old shape of a muster-count — *count off, count off* — and
answered the stone. An arm rose here. A grey head there. The
pressure-ghosts thinned like breath-fog in sun, their peace
cracking off them, and the vast calm shape at the Still Point
came apart last and slowest, not slain — there was nothing in it
to slay — but evicted, dismissed like a servant whose office has
ended, dissolving into a shower of ordinary rain that fell, that
*fell*, the first falling rain the eye had known in a thousand
years, straight down onto the graves like a blessing with its
weight returned.

All over the bowl of gold light, the suspended beads let go.

It rained on the rising for the time a lamp takes to gutter —
warm, real, downward rain — and the seated of the garden rose,
forty-and-nine of them by Nix's count, tottering, blinking, alive,
caught by Lullside hands; and the army sang the count around one
more time for the risen, and Mera Lullside stood in the falling
rain with her bells ringing at last, unmuffled, and cried like a
girl.

They slept that night — the whole host, in the rings of camps,
under a sky rinsed clean — the truest sleep of the climb. Nix
took the middle watch and did not need the draught to stay
sharp; he was too full. He walked the rings with the banner on
his shoulder, counting sleepers the way a miser counts gold.
Eleven thousand and some, breathing. Rule one, kept eleven-
thousandfold: nobody musters alone.

He was at the western ring, by the graves, when the horns came.

⁂

They came through the eye-wall, which nothing came through — not
sound, not wind; Mera had said so, the roar ate everything — and
these came through it the way a knife comes through a curtain.
Three notes, long, cold, courteous, pitched in some register the
storm declined to argue with. Not loud. Clear. The kind of sound
that has never needed to be loud.

Every hound the rising had brought heeled at once, whining, and
lay flat. The roar itself seemed to bend aside to let the notes
pass, and they crossed the bowl of the eye and went on into the
tier above, unhurried, like a rider crossing a room to deliver a
card.

The nameless elf was beside Nix before Nix knew he had moved —
Rhovan, with his bow already strung, listening the way other
folk pray. And out of the graveside dark came one of the blown
they had gathered off the seventy-fourth floor, a snare-folk
elf of Brambleside, wire-scarred, who had gone white to the
lips.

— Coursing-horns, — she said. — The Pale Court's. I grew up under
those horns. They blow the count of the quarry, always, first
call. It's the law of their hunt: the quarry is entitled to know
it is chosen, and to know how many.
— Listen. They'll confirm it.

The horns spoke again through the eye-wall, and Nix, who
counted everything, counted.

Four notes.

Not eleven thousand. Not the army, breathing safe in its rings
of rain. Four — the count of the little party that had walked
ahead of everything it loved, through open sky, for three days,
under eyes that read the gap between shepherds and flock the
way Mera read her glass. Ede. Vega. Rhovan. And a goblin who
held the banner because flags don't sit.

The Pale Court had noticed.

Nix looked at the graves, and the sleeping rings, and the wall
of roaring dark between this floor and the next, and he hugged
the banner-staff to his chest and said, to no one, in a voice
too small for the size of the count:

— Four. It counted us, and it's *polite*. Wake the general.

Above the eye, in the wood beyond the wall, the hunt was already
moving.
