# Chapter 28 — The Anchor Chains

Vega Coalbrow had drawn chain all her life, and she had never feared it
until she walked upon one above the clouds.

The first link was longer than a mead-hall. It hung in a shallow arc
from the anchor-tower behind her to a windlass-house she could not see,
a road of grey iron slung across a gulf that had no floor. Cloud moved
under it, white and slow. Wind moved over it and made it sing — a low
hum, steady as a hearth, felt through the boot-soles before the ears
caught it. She set her hand on the link's worn crown and read the iron
the way another woman might read a letter. Old. True. Tired in the
pins.

"This one is sound," said the chain-keeper beside her. Mera Chainrest
was a woman of Men, small as a child against Vega's knee, grease to
the elbows, a striking-hammer at her belt. "You hear the hum? A
sound link hums. A failing one goes quiet. The moss muffles
it and the rust-flower eats the pins, so we walk, and we listen. It is
slow work. It is the only work there is."

"I understand slow work," Vega said. She said everything slowly, and
she finished it. "Show me a quiet link, and I will mend it."

The rising crossed the Anchor Chains for nine days, and it crossed them
quietly, because it had not sung since the Frosthall. Two months had
not softened that. The army still turned its head at the smell of roast
meat; it still flinched at a hall with two doors. Ede drove it upward
because stopping was worse, and the tower, as if it knew, had given the
grief a road exactly suited to it: a floor with no ground at all, where
every soul went single file along the chains with a line clipped to a
shackle and nothing below the boot but weather.

The chains had held floating isles once. The sky-mooring country of
Men had anchored its islands to the mountain the way other folk moor
barges, and the night of the Theft the isles had torn loose
and drifted away into the dark, and the chains had hung empty ever
since, humming, going nowhere. Chainrest's folk had walked them for a
thousand years regardless. A taut chain, a sound shackle. It was what
they had instead of a country.

Vega walked, and listened, and learned the floor's teeth. Link-rats
ran the moss in packs and fouled the handholds; chain-ticks dropped
from the high links, fat as fists. On the fourth day a harpy that had
been a kite shoved a Frosthall spearman off the third great chain, and
his line held, and the one who went to haul him up was cut instead —
a wind-torn figure walking the links where no keeper walked, a severed
safety-line knotted at its waist. A moss-farmer, Mera said, who fell
in her grandmother's time. They broke it over the gulf and it drained
away into the cloud, and Mera spat after it, and then wept, because
she had known the story of the man all her life and had never had to
see him work.

Vega counted the crossing's cost in her ledger of mendings. One
spearman, cut and living. Two dray-goats, lost. One link gone quiet
under the moss, found by its silence, re-pinned by her own hand. It
was the first thing she had mended since the feast, and her hands
shook when it rang true, and she was glad of the wind, because a
forge-matron does not weep where her apprentices can see.

The Warden held the Windlass House, where the greatest chain came home
to the mountain and the master mooring-brake stood taller than Vega
herself.

Shacklewise had been a drake once, a link-roosting thing that slept
coiled through the great iron. The tower had welded it into the
windlass whole, wound a mooring-chain onto its tail, and set it to keep
the brake it had slept beside. It fought the way the floor fought:
it paid out chain and snapped it back, and a mile of iron road leapt
like a whipped rope, and soldiers went off the links into the sky with
their lines singing taut. For half a morning it kept three companies
pinned in the anchor-towers, and the spans swayed worse with every
heartbeat.

Ede's answer was Vega. The forge-matron went out on the bucking chain
alone, too heavy to be flicked loose, each step placed the way she
placed ingots, and the drake's tail cracked the iron under her and she
rode it like a woman standing in a boat. All the while she
was speaking the keepers' word that Mera had taught her from the brake's
own carving — *Moor*, the art of the floor, the word that holds a thing
immovable against wind and fall and force. She did not cast it at
herself. She walked the whole screaming span and laid her hand on the
windlass and cast it into the brake.

The chains stopped. All of them, floor-wide, one hum in a hundred
voices, fast and still as the day they were forged. Shacklewise heaved
against its own moored tail and could not pay out so much as a link,
and the bowmen came out of the towers, and it ended. Broken,
the welded thing sagged in the windlass like a chain off its sprocket,
and something that had only ever been a drake slid loose of the iron
and glided away down the wind, uncollared, free, small.

Vega stood by the brake a long time with her palm on the lever.

"A brake is a promise," she said to Mera. "Whoever built this meant
it to be kept. We will keep it."

Chainrest kept better than that. With the master brake set and every
chain on the floor holding fast, the keepers ran out the slack chain
to the one isle that had never drifted clear — snagged on its mooring
a half-mile off the span, a hanging garden gone wild, tilting in the
wind — and they hooked it, and rigged the capstans, and hauled their
country home. Giants of the Forge-Court walked the capstan-bars beside
windlass-crews of Men. It took three days. When the Drifted Isle came
to rest against its anchor-stone the whole floor rang like a struck
anvil, and the chain-keepers of Chainrest looked at solid ground
floating in the sky, and did not know what to do with their hands.

That night the rising camped on grass, above the clouds, for the first
time. And for the first time since the Frosthall, somebody sang. It was
a chain-sounding song, not a soldier's song, and half the army did not
know the words. It listened. That was a beginning.

"It is a dead engine," Ulfar said. "That offends me."

Her brother had found the isle's heart by supper: a vault of lift-
stones under the wild garden, the old buoyancy-craft of the sky-realm,
cold now, cracked, spent the night of the Theft. He stood over them
with a lamp, and Vega watched his face and saw the Forge-Court in it,
the way you see weather coming over ice.

"It is a garden," Vega said, "with a dead engine under it. The
garden is the part that matters."

"The garden exists because of the engine." Ulfar said it without
heat. He never argued with heat; that was the worst of him. "Sister.
The folk below us are sleeping on proof that the old world made
things worth keeping. You would break the tower and let all its
craft run out on the ground like blood. I would keep the craft and
pour out only the cruelty. I do not think that makes me the wicked
one."

"No," Vega said slowly. "It makes you the tempted one. Those are
different, and the second is worse, because it can vote."

He laughed, because they were family, and it was not finished between
them, and both knew where it would finish: higher up, in the tower's
own country, where the engines were not dead. She thought of the
feast, of how many arguments had ended that night unfinished forever,
and let her brother laugh. She wrote in her ledger before she slept:
*lift-stones, cracked, nine. Query — what craft raises them. Mend the
garden regardless.*

Below, down the long chains, Berrin Or's supply-trains came up the
links by lamplight, crate after crate swayed across the gulf, bread
and arrows and pitch arriving, as always, exactly when despair would
have begun without them. The quartermaster himself came up with the
last load, one arm still slung from the arrow he had taken at the
feast, and the camp's faces lifted at the sight of him as they lifted
for no one else. He had brought, for no reason under heaven but
kindness, a crate of seed-potatoes for a hanging garden nobody had
known existed a week before.

"How," Vega asked him, "did you know?"

"I didn't." Berrin smiled tiredly and rubbed his bad shoulder.
"But it stood to reason somebody up a tower would be missing a garden.
Somebody always is."

It warmed her, like everyone else. That was what Berrin was for.

Three countries lay between the chains and the fleet, and the army
was the better part of a month in them. It skirted the Reactor Scar
along the cool rim, two days' march of fused colorless glass ringing
under the boots, drinking Grint Scarwatch's palewater at every halt
while the scar-warden walked ahead of the van reading a fire nobody
could see by the prickle of his own skin. It crossed the Updrafts on
Cole Kitegate's re-rigged spanlines, a gorge-country that boomed,
kite-pilots ferrying the baggage up the thermal columns while the
ranks went hand over hand above a wind that wanted them. And it came
up through the Ice Quarries walking canyon-floors of glass-clear
blocks under Hune Hewnstone's locked gantries, house-sized loads
hanging dead still overhead, the quarry-master reading each block's
flaws by the light through it before he would let a company pass
beneath.

Floor sixty-five was a fleet.

They came up the stair-lift into grey light and a forest, and the
forest was masts — an entire sky-fleet that had lost its lift the
night of the Theft and settled upright, hull-deep into the mountain's
flank, so that spar and shroud and rotted sail stood in ranks where a
wood would have branch and leaf. The ground was a tangle of fallen
rigging deeper than a giant is tall. The canopy was crosstrees. In
between hung a thousand ropes, and mast-folk lived in the crowsnests
and crossed their whole country hand over line, and the wind in the
shrouds made the sound of a fleet forever about to sail.

"Don't touch the floor," said Weld Sparside, by way of welcome. The
mast-warden was lean as a stay and barefoot, and he stood on a line
thin as Vega's finger as though it were a quay. "The tangle takes.
Everything you want is up, or down in the Bilge, and the Bilge you go
to only once, with me, on a day I choose."

The rising learned to rig. That was the campaign, more than any fight:
freed sky-folk of Chainrest and Sparside teaching soldiers of nine
peoples to walk a shroud, to trust a knot tied by their own cold
hands, to fall correctly, which is to say onto a line and not through
it. Vega, who could not walk the lines at all, went along the buried
decks below with the shipwrights, and there Weld chose his day and
took her down into the Bilge — the flooded hull-base where the sunken
decks met the rock, black water standing over a drowned hold — and
showed her, by one lamp, the flagship.

Her decks were whole. Her cabins were shut. In her admiral's cabin lay
the fleet's muster-roll and her charts, and deeper, cradled below the
waterline in a chamber sealed with wax and prayer, the thing Vega had
climbed sixty-five floors to put her hands on without knowing it: a
lift-cell, unbroken, asleep — the heart that had once held ten
thousand tons of oak upon the air.

She read it the way she read iron, palms flat, eyes shut. No forge-
mark of hers. Older. Kinder. A made thing with no cruelty in the
making, and the first such she had touched since her own collar-steel
had come back to shame her at the Warcamp cages.

"It can be woken," she said, and her voice was not quite steady.
"Nothing in it is broken. It is only unpaid. Craft like this is a debt,
and lift is how the debt is honored."

"Then you'd best deal with the rig first," Weld said dryly, and
above them, through a hundred fathoms of tangle, something enormous
began to haul on every rope on the floor at once.

Mastwrack fought from the tallest mainmast, a crowsnest drake welded
into the trunk of it, wearing the whole fleet's rigging for limbs. It
did not come to them; it reeled them in. Shroud-lines whipped out of
the dark and took soldiers up screaming into the canopy; spars came
down the mast-lanes like felled pines. The army fought a forest of
rope for a day and a night, cutting and never burning — Weld forbade
all fire — while the topmen worked inward crosstree by crosstree.
In the end the floor's own word won it, *Splice*, the topmen's art
that commands rope: a hundred freed mast-folk speaking it together,
line by line, until the Warden's web answered them and not it, and
it hung in its own stilled rigging,
and the bows finished what obedience had begun. The rigging fell
slack. The forest went quiet as a chapel. Somewhere under it a drake
that remembered being a drake climbed out along a yard and flew.

They raised the lift-cell from the Bilge on the third morning, with
chain-keepers' capstans and topmen's tackle and one giant standing
hip-deep hauling like a windlass made flesh. They set it in the
soundest hull of the wreck-fields, a slim sixty-foot skiff the mast-
folk had kept caulked out of pure stubborn love, and Vega woke the
cell with the fleet's own charts open beside her, and the hull
groaned, and lifted, and stood upon the air.

The Signal-Top was re-rigged by sunset. When its lamp was lit — the
old muster-signal, ship to ship, that no lamp had answered in
eighty years — the mast-folk came out of the whole forest canopy to
look, and the light caught on the skiff riding at anchor above the
wrecks, and Weld Sparside wept without once losing his footing on the
line.

The bridge floors went by like beads told on a string, and every bead
was a country: the Kennel Courts heard half a day before they were
seen, tiered stone yards baying under the mews-yews; the Coolant
Falls a mile-high cliff of stopped white river, its milk-light
showing the pilgrims still standing in the curtain; the Silk Vaults
cold and hushed for two days' walking, aisle on aisle of neat white
bundles; the Lightning Fields an ozone plain of verdigris poles under
a ceiling that flashed and never rained. In the Kennel
Courts the master horn sounded from the Mews-Tower and every ungentled
pack on the floor heeled at last to its own name, and the rising
marched out with hounds at its flanks and owls above its banners. At
the Coolant Falls, Bruna Milkwater opened the source her people had
kept a thousand years, and the frozen river thawed from the head down
and *roared* again, giving up its pilgrim dead for burial. In the
Silk Vaults the crews of Spoolgate read their ledgers by lamplight
and cut free a
vault's worth of the wrapped-but-living, and some of the freed had
been shelved so long they asked after kings dead an age. On the
Lightning Fields the army crossed by the safe lanes under a sky that
aimed, and Mera Copperpole threw the master switch in the Ground-
Vault, and the harvested storm poured into the rising's keeping
instead of down upon its people. Four floors, four gate-towns, four
keepers' hands shaken; and behind the host, at every mooring, the
shipwrights worked.

For that was the turn the sky-campaign made, quietly, while the army
was busy grieving and climbing and learning knots. The wreck-fields
gave hulls. The Bilge gave charts and the craft of lift. Chainrest
gave anchor-chain steel and the word Moor; Sparside gave shroud-cable
and the word Splice; the quarries below had given lenses and the
Fields gave storm-copper for the cells. By the month's end eleven
skiffs rode at the chains above the Mast Forest, rigged, crewed,
provisioned by Berrin's trains — a navy. A small, patched, absurd
navy, the first fleet the sky had carried since the Theft, and every
plank of it mended rather than made.

Vega stood at the rail of the first skiff in the evening watch and
looked up. Above the wreck-fields, above the Fields' flashing sky,
the next floor wore a crown of storm that never moved and never
broke — the Tempest Court, where the seventh tier ended, where a queen
the tower had crowned held her whole court captive inside a hurricane.
The parliament would send an embassy; that was already decided. The
storm did not look like a court that received embassies. It looked
like weather with a will.

*Everything broken can be mended,* Vega told herself, palm on the
rail, feeling the woken cell hum under the deck like a sound chain.

She wondered, watching the lightning stitch the queen's walls, what
it would cost to mend a storm — and whose price it would be.
