# Chapter 34 — The Pale Court

The pale court leached the color out of things, and it did it slowly
enough that you could watch it happen.

Ede noticed it first on Nix, whose green went grey at the edges as they
came up the pale road, the way a leaf goes in frost. Then on her own hands.
The light of floor eighty was a cold moon-pale wash that fell from nowhere
and cast no shadows, and under it the wood's oldest heart stood leafless and
bone-white — birch and hunt-oak branched like antlers, hung with old horns
that turned in no wind. Ahead, ringed by its kennels, the hall: long, low,
white as a skull, a thousand years of hunting nailed to its walls.

"Say nothing you do not mean," Rhovan murmured as they came to the doors.
"In this court, speech is spoor. They will course whatever you let slip."

"And him?"

"He will not speak. He never speaks. The court speaks around him, and
watches what your face does while you wait to learn if it spoke true."

The court received them in the manner of a hunt at rest. Pale hounds sat
in rings on the moon-mossed stones. Whips and horn-callers stood along the
walls — and these were no wights, most of them, but living elves, thin and
colorless, with the eyes of people who had run as quarry when the horn
wanted sport and served as court when it did not. Court-captives, Rhovan had
named them on the road. The Hunt's own folk, held as the Huntsman's
unwilling field.

At the hall's end, on a high seat over the pale floor, sat the Master of
the Hunt.

Ede made herself look at him the way she would read any position: worst
first. Antler-crowned, moon-pale, still as the dead center of a storm. The
crown was welded to him — she could see the seam of it, the tower's iron
under the pale silver — and beneath the crown was a face that had been
gentle once. That was the unbearable part. Skarn had died believing his
collar a rank; this one's face believed nothing at all. The tower had
hunted the Master of the Hunt longer than any lord in the stolen realms,
the court-captives said, until the gentlest hand in the wood was made the
pale hunter of his own people. He watched her cross his floor, and his
eyes were the only fast thing in him, and they never once left her.

An elf came forward from the foot of the high seat. Old, straight-backed,
carrying a horn-caller's baldric with no horn on it — the emptiness of it
plainly a rank of its own.

"I am Nael Hornsend, who was his horn-caller." The old elf's voice was
even, but Ede had stood before enough courts now to hear what it cost.
"You walked to the horns. Four of you, on your feet, at a walk, under a
full course. The court has not received a walked quarry in living memory,
and elves live long. Say what you are."

"Ede Stonhal. Of Roothollow, and of the rising you've watched climb your
runs." She kept it flat, count-plain. "Behind me on the wind floors is an
army the Storm Queen scattered — regiments of it blown down onto your
country, onto the Snare Woods, the Runs, the Wyrmroad. Your hunt is
coursing them now, piecemeal, as it coursed us. I walked to the horns to
say this where the horns take their law: they are not quarry. They are
mine. I have come to bargain for their passage."

A stir along the walls, like wind in a leafless wood. Hornsend glanced
once at the high seat — at the still figure, the unmoving face — and Ede
understood that the glance itself was the court's whole procedure: the old
elf read his silent lord the way sailors read a sky, and spoke the weather
he found there.

"The court hears a claim of quarry-right," Hornsend said. "The walked-to
may ask a boon. It is the oldest grace we have — the Master's own law,
from before the tower, from when a hunt here ended in mercy or did not end
at all. Ask."

"The horns silent over the Hunt's whole country," Ede said, "until the
last of my scattered have come off your floors and mustered below this
court. No course run on any who wear my charter's mark. Guides on the runs
for the lost, and the beaters' ways opened to my provisioners. That is the
boon."

"And what does the court receive?"

She had thought about it the whole pale road up. Voss would have offered
coin, Ulfar engines, Zephyra a throne. She offered the only thing a hunt
that had forgotten mercy could be offered by the people climbing to free
it — though she could not say that part aloud, with the welded crown's iron
listening for all she knew.

"A quarry worth the horn," Ede said. "Above your court the stolen realms
end. What sits up there is the tower's own Making — the thing that took
your wood, welded your pack, and nailed your Master to his crown. When my
army passes your hall, the Hunt is done chasing farmers and cup-bearers
and blown soldiers forever. I offer you the hunt you were made for. We
climb to course the tower itself. Ride with the rising, or stand aside and
watch it done — but the small game is over either way."

Silence, while the old elf watched the high seat. Ede watched too. She
saw nothing move in the pale face — and then the right hand, gloved,
resting on the saddle-bow of a chair built like a saddle, turned palm
upward. Two fingers opened. It was nothing. It was the most speech she
would ever get from him, and the whole hall breathed.

"The boon is granted," Hornsend said, and his even voice cracked at last,
just once, on the next words. "And the court will add what the walked-to
did not ask, because I knew him before the crown, and I say his law is
still alive in there and wants this said: the stair beyond this hall opens
only by his horn. There is no passage past the pale court but through its
Master. When your army is gathered, that reckoning comes, and no boon can
bargain it away." The old elf mastered himself. "Until then: the horns are
silent. The Hunt keeps its truces. It is the last thing about us the tower
could not spoil."

They were the court's received guests for a night, and hunt-law required
that received guests be shown the honor of the house. The honor of the
house was the Trophy Hall.

Ede walked it because refusing was not among her choices. It was the long
gallery of the bone-white hall, and it held a thousand years: horns and
heads, antlers beyond counting, pelts gone pale in the pale light, the
mounted gear of quarry that had run well enough to be remembered. Nothing
in it was cruel, exactly. That was what made it dreadful. Every trophy hung
with its story tagged in fine elvish tally-script, and a trophy-keeper
walked beside the guests and read the stories out, and the stories were
all of respect. *Ran four days. Turned at the water and fought. Spared, and
her line spared after her, for the running's sake.* The far end of the
gallery held older, stranger honors — trophies of the Master's freed peers,
tokens of Skarn and Hrimgar and the Storm Queen from ages when the crowned
captives had hunted each other's borders for sport. And on a peg of pale
silver between two horn-racks, small and out of place among the grandeur:
a plain leather hound-collar, cracked with age, its name-tag worn to a
shine. Ede saw the trophy-keeper's hand hover past it without touching.

"Whose?" Nix asked — fast, curious, counting everything as always.

"The first hound of the pale line," the trophy-keeper said. "Bell. She
heeled to his hand before he ever carried a whip. He hung her collar here
himself, the year she died, and said a hunt is worth what its mercy is
worth." The keeper moved on quickly, the way people move past a grave.
"That was the Master we had."

It was three racks later that Ede stopped walking.

The shield hung between a boar-spear and a wolf's head, and it was a
Shieldwall shield. She knew it before she knew she knew it — knew the
pattern of the rim-studs the way you know a face at a market stall, wrong
country, wrong year, impossible. Round, oak under hide, the wall-and-star
of her own company blistered but plain. The paint of the star had been
laid on in the Warcamp fashion, the star's low point doubled. Third cohort.
One of her thirty had carried it into the mine-dark at floor twenty, and
it had not come out with the freed, because it was here.

"A shield of Men," the trophy-keeper said, reading the tally-tag with a
collector's mild pride. "Not taken — bought. The court bought notable
pieces off the toll-roads in the years the Hunt did not ride low. This one
was sold upward from the twentieth floor. See the rim." Ede saw the rim.
Stamped into the hide binding, small and regular as hoofprints, were
toll-marks — the crossed-key punch she had last seen on the Tithe Barrows,
on ledgers Rhovan had burgled by lamplight, on every crate that had ever
moved through a Tollman gate under Gilder Voss. "Six gates, six marks. A
well-traveled piece. The tally names the consignment: routed through the
gate-town under the tower's root — Roothollow — in the spring of the
sale-year, by a provisioner's factor. Whoever sold it had gate-friends,
to move a battle-trophy the length of the low tower so quick and so
quiet."

"Quick," Ede said. Her voice came out level. Some part of her that had
been a captain before it had been anything else took over her breathing,
her shoulders, the muscles of her face, and did what it had done at a
hundred gravesides. "How quick?"

The keeper consulted the tag, pleased to be asked. "Sold at the tenth
gate within the month of its taking. The spring the Red Warcamp filled
its cages."

The spring the Warcamp filled its cages. Ede stood in the pale light with
her hands loose at her sides and let the arithmetic finish itself, because
it was going to finish whether she let it or not. The spring her company
died, somebody in Roothollow had money moving up the toll-roads. She had
known that much since floor eleven — a payment up the tower, the week of
the sale; Rhovan's stolen ledger-page; a courier who smelled of Roothollow
bread. A signature without a name. But she had been in Roothollow that
spring, drunk and dying of it, and she remembered the season anyway,
because it was the season the granary went up.

The new granary. Raised in a month, timber bought outright, ale stood for
the whole street at the roof-raising, and everyone saying what everyone
always said — that no one in the low world was better to his neighbors,
that Roothollow would have starved twice over in the bad years without
him, that a man like that deserved a good year at last.

The week her thirty went into the cages, his granary got its beams.

She heard Rhovan come up beside her — felt him find the shield, the star,
the doubled point, saw in the pale edge of her sight how still he went.
Elves answer questions with fractions, and ask them the same way. He asked
nothing. She turned to the trophy-keeper.

"It's a good shield," she said. "Third cohort hung their shields
face-out in the wall. It ran well." The words came from somewhere and her
face held. "What does the tally give for the seller's name?"

"No name. Consignments off the toll-roads carry the factor's mark only."
The keeper touched the tag, apologetic. "A wheat-sheaf, this one. A
provisioner's sign, common as rain in the grain-towns. It would be no one
you could find."

"No," Ede said. "No, I don't suppose it would."

She looked at the shield a moment longer, because a captain may look at
her dead company's shield for a moment, and no court in any world reads
more into it than grief. Then she walked on down the gallery, past the
horns of a thousand years, and asked the keeper about a wolf-pelt three
racks along, and heard herself sound interested in the answer.

That night the court lodged them in the kennel-wing, in narrow white
rooms that had been whips' quarters, and Ede sat on the pallet's edge with
Sliver dark in her closed hand and did not sleep.

Say it, she thought, and could not, even soundlessly, even to herself.
Saying it would make it a thing that had been said. So she counted instead,
the old discipline, the one that survived when the rest went. The ledger
payment, the week of the sale. The courier who smelled of bread. The
serving-list at the Frosthall, drawn by someone who knew the seating. The
muster-roll of everyone who had climbed before — the roll with no gaps in
it, kept by the man who kept the rolls. The granary beams. Six toll-marks
on a dead man's rim, and a wheat-sheaf for a signature. Any one of them
was rain. All of them together were a roof, and under the roof, dry and
warm and beloved, stood her oldest friend, and the count would not come
out any other way, and she was done pretending it might.

Below her window the pale court lay colorless under its moonless light.
Somewhere north of it, a day down the beaters' ways, her army was already
moving — regiments taking the opened runs, provision-trains rolling for
the muster, and with the trains, as sure as bread, would come Berrin,
because Berrin never missed a muster, because the army could not eat
without him. He would find her at the picket-line the way he always did.
He would have counted her losses before she said them, and have the
grain already moving to cover them, and he would look at her with the
same steady kindness he had been looking at her with since she was a
drunk woman he refused to give up on, and she would have to look back.

She would have to look back, and say nothing, and climb.

The rest of the way. Beside him. Twenty floors of pretending, while the
collar under his shirt — if there was a collar; there was a collar; she
made herself finish even that — carried everything her face let slip to
whatever sat above them wearing the ninetieth floor like a crown.

In this court, speech is spoor, Rhovan had said. They will course
whatever you let slip.

"Then nothing slips," Ede said to the pale room, in a voice that had
drilled recruits at Roothollow when it was the only thing left she was fit
for. It was almost steady. It would be steady by morning; she knew her own
tempering that well, and hated the knowing.

Her hand ached. She opened it and found Sliver's light seeping between
her fingers, faint and troubled, the splinter warm as a held coal.

*"Your heart has been running for half the night,"* it said. *"You have
not once moved. Ede. What did you take off that wall?"*

She looked out at the bone-white court a long time.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Ask me tomorrow, when the horns can't hear."

The pale light did not change, because it never changed. Morning in the
Huntsman's country was only the hour the horns woke. When they did, they
sounded a truce-call down the runs — the first mercy this floor had been
allowed in eighty years, bought by four walkers and a boon — and Ede
Stonhal washed her face in cold water, and hung her grief where the court
hung its trophies, face-out, and went down to muster her army beside the
man who had sold the last one.
