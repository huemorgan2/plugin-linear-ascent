# Chapter 22 — The Feast

On the morning of the feast of the charter, Mistress Calyne came to Ede
in the Undercroft, among the casks, where the general had gone to be
alone with the lists and had brought, out of old habit, a lamp and no
cup.

The spymaster of the Wake did not waste her own time or anyone else's.
She stood in the lamp-light with her grey cloak still frosted from the
yard, and she laid a strip of paper on the cask-head between them, and
she said, "There is a Herald inside your charter's circle."

The paper was a cipher-strip, short, torn at one end. Ede did not read
ciphers. She looked at it the way you look at a knife someone has set
on your table, and waited.

"It came off a drummer at the Cindermouth," Calyne said. "Malgrim's
hand-code, third form. Most of it is nothing — musters, floors, the
grain-count at the Moraine Gates, which any spy with boots could sell
him. But this line prices something else." Her finger touched the strip
without moving it. "It prices the order of a council that had not yet
met. Who would speak first at the Frosthall, and for what. That was not
carried up by boots, general. That came out of the room where your
parliament was planned — out of the circle that drew it. Someone who
signs what you sign. Someone with a collar under their shirt, and it is
a willing one, and it has been there a long time. I have hunted this
hand for years and it is close enough now that I am telling you at
last, plainly, in daylight: there is a Herald at your table, and they
will be at your feast tonight."

The Undercroft was quiet. Somewhere above, a bench scraped; the hall
was being dressed. Ede looked at the strip of paper, and then at the
woman who had brought it, and everything she knew arranged itself in
the old order, the way a shieldwall forms without a command.

Because here was the thing about Mistress Calyne, laid out fair, the
way Ede would later lay it out for herself a hundred times on a
hundred sleepless floors: no one in the rising had her ciphers. No
one had her absences — gone three days below, back with frost on her
boots and nothing said. No one else had walked out of a Roothollow
childhood into the Wake's grey service with a past so clean it
squeaked. The payment that sold Shieldwall had moved through
toll-ledgers her faction audited. She had arrived at the rising's
council uninvited, embedded herself, survived every suspicion by
being useful — and now, the morning the charter's ink was dry, here
she stood, pointing away from herself, at everyone. At Vega. At
Voss. At Hobb Fennick and Dun Harrow. At Berrin.

That was where the wall closed. The accusation had no floor under it
and it was aimed at a circle of people who had bled up sixty floors —
and it was brought by the one person in the tower whose own account
had never once been opened.

"You've hunted this hand for years," Ede said. "So you have said. It
occurs to me, Mistress, that a hunter is the one soul in a wood whose
walking around at night needs no explaining." She pushed the strip
back across the cask-head. "Everyone in that circle has carried
someone I love out of a fight. What has the Wake carried? Ciphers.
Bring me a name and proof under it, and I will act though it break
me. Bring me one more nameless *someone* on the morning of the first
good day this army has ever had, and I will start to wonder why the
Wake needs my council frightened of itself."

Calyne looked at her for a long moment. Whatever moved behind that
grey regard, it did not reach the surface; it never did; that was the
whole trouble with her.

"You are guarding the wrong door," she said at last, without heat,
and folded the strip away. "I will be at the feast, general. Armed."

She was at the turning of the stair when Berrin came down it — nearly
into her — with a basket on his arm and flour to the elbow. He begged
her pardon so warmly that even Calyne's face conceded something, and
then he was in the lamp-light unpacking the basket onto the cask-head:
a loaf still hot, split, buttered, and a smear of honey, because he had
known Ede since before the Warcamp and knew she forgot to eat on the
days she was afraid of being happy.

"They've let me at the ovens," he said. "Bruna's folk bake big as
they build; I've made them do a proper Roothollow batch for the low
tables. Eat that while it's warm." He was already turning to go —
there was no rest in him on a feast day, there never had been — and
he paused at the stair-foot and looked back at her, flour on his
hands, the lamp catching the good plain worry in his face. "You look
like your watch never ended, Ede. It's ended. Sixty floors and a
signed paper says so. Tonight, for one night, let the army guard you."

The bread did smell of home. That was the fact, and she chose it.

The Frosthall that night was the finest room in the world.

Every lamp in the jarldom was lit, and every door stood open, the
great north door to the yard and the south door by the kitchens and
the little postern doors between, all wide, spilling gold onto the
snow, because Hrimgar had commanded it: the hall had eleven years of
darkness to pay back. The sixty-one banners hung overhead with the
charter itself among them, hung from the center-beam over the High
Seat where all the hall could see it. The tables ran the full length
of the floor, and at them sat the gathered world according to
Berrin's careful pages: men and giants shoulder to shoulder, elves
among the keepers, freed thanes pouring for freed kobolds, Cullers
seated decently far from those they had wronged. Serving-lines
flowed from the kitchens along both walls, laden, endless. The
goblin cooks had outdone the age: Nix had sworn that the freed
Pressed would feed this parliament a feast no giant would forget,
and the boar came in on poles to a noise like a lift-gate opening.

Hrimgar would not take the High Seat. He stood before it the whole
first course, cup in hand, and gave the seat to Ede with the hall
watching — *a jarl who'd give his own seat to a cold stranger* — and
the roar that went up shook soot from the smoke-hole. Ede sat where
the Warden of the sixtieth floor had sat, under the charter, with
Dun Harrow standing his post behind her shoulder, the banner's butt
grounded by her chair, as he had stood behind her in every hall and
every mud-camp since the cages, because he was her standard-bearer,
and he had told her once, in the way he said such things, that a
standard-bearer's post was wherever the standard's general sat, and
that had ended the discussion for two years.

Hale she marked far down the hall at a low bench, in his grey,
drinking little, as he did now. The serjeant had come up sixty
floors to become a quiet man. He had taken the seat nearest the
kitchens, and when the goblin cooks' boys went by under their great
trays he steadied the smallest one's elbow with two fingers, absent,
unthanked, the way he had once corrected a recruit's shield-grip.
Ede caught herself watching him and could not have said why.

The toasts began. Hrimgar gave the dead of the Long Stair, all the
hall on its feet, thirty-one names read slow by a goblin from a
knotted cord, and no one sat until the last. Voss, sleek as a paid
bill, gave *the customers of the freed gates, which is everyone,
forever* — and got his laugh, and even the keepers drank. Vega gave
her mother's forge, and Ulfar drank to that with his eyes wet. Berrin
would give no toast, though half the hall called for him; he was
everywhere and nowhere all night, flour-dusted still at the cuffs,
walking the serving-lines with his lists, chivvying, tasting,
laughing — and it was Berrin, passing behind the High Seat with a jug
in his hand, who leaned in between Ede and Dun and said the last easy
words anyone would ever say in the old world, the world before:

"Look at them, Ede. Look what you fed."

Then he straightened to go on down the line, jug in hand, facing
out over the bright length of the hall the way a man at the High
Seat's back must face, and what happened next happened all at
once.

A mead-horn dropped somewhere down the eastern line and did not
bounce. It rang — struck stone edge-on, a sound too clean for an
accident — and along both serving-lines, in one movement, as if
the hall itself had been rehearsed, a dozen servers set down their
trays and drew from under the linen the knives that were not
carving knives, and above, at the smoke-hole gallery where no
servers went, the first bowstring spoke.

The arrow was for Ede. Kill the general under her own signed
peace, in her own parliament, and the paper over her head becomes
a headstone for the age's great foolish hope. She did not see it
loosed. She saw the table-edge come up at
her as something struck her whole side — not the arrow, Berrin,
Berrin's shoulder taking her out of the chair, Berrin's arms
around her head, the two of them going down together into the
spilled gold of the mead as the arrow meant for her throat went
through him high in the back with a sound she knew from twenty
years of fields, and he made no cry at all, only a cook's small
annoyed grunt, as if a loaf had caught, and covered her with his
body, and would not be moved.

Then the hall was a battle, and it was the battle the tower had
always wanted: not army against army but a feast against itself,
friend indistinguishable from knife, and fire — fire already in
the rafters, pitch-arrows among the banners, sixty-one flags of
the gathered world beginning to burn over the heads of the world
they gathered.

Ede got out from under her oldest friend with his blood on her
neck, and stood up into it, and did what she was for.

"SHIELDWALL," she said, in the voice that carried over drum-fire,
and the tables of the freed company came over onto their sides
down the length of the hall in one crash, oak two fingers thick,
and the gathered world went behind it. Vega was already moving —
a giant among falling lamps, sweeping children and keepers behind
her like a hand sweeping crumbs, her craft hurled up at the burning
center-beam so that frost ran along it and the fire above the
charter died hissing. Rhovan was on a table with his bow, loosing
up into the gallery, elf-eyed in the smoke, and beside him,
suddenly, greyly, Calyne — Calyne who had come armed, Calyne who
was killing the men she had warned of with a short blade and no
expression Ede could read across the reddening hall.

The serving-lines' knives went for the benches where the charter's
signers sat. They knew the seating. Ede saw it and had no time to
see it; she saw a Herald put his blade through a Frosthall steward
who stood between him and Hobb Fennick, saw the old keeper of the
first floor of the world swing a bench-end like a man knocking
apples, saw Hrimgar tear a burning rafter-length down with his
bare hands and lay about him, a jarl defending his guests with a
beam of his own hall, roaring in the deep tongue, weeping another
kind of tears than the night had begun with.

At the north door, they tried to close the hall.

That was the shape of the thing; the fire above, the knives
within, and the doors — the doors Hrimgar had ordered open —
swinging to, barred from without by Heralds in the yard, so that
the gathered world should roast in its own capital. The great
south door by the kitchens was already booming shut. The north
door had Dun Harrow in it.

He had left her shoulder. Standard-bearer's post is wherever the
standard is needed, and the standard was needed in a doorway. He
set the banner-pole of Shieldwall crosswise in the jamb, oak
against oak, and set himself against the pole, one arm, all his
weight, and the door met him and stopped. Through the gap the
night came in cold and the folk streamed out past him into the
yard, keepers and cooks' boys and thanes' children, a river of
the saved running under his braced body, while the Heralds inside
put an arrow into him, and then another, because he would not
fall, because falling was a thing that happened to other men's
standards. Ede fought toward him the length of a burning hall and
was too slow, was always going to be too slow, and knew it with
every step, the way you know the end of a song.

He was still on his feet when she reached him. The door stood
open. The pole stood true. Dun Harrow, one-armed, twice-shot,
looked at his general the way he had looked at her at the cages
of the Warcamp, when he had crossed a silent camp of men who
blamed her and put the banner in her hands, and he said, in his
ordinary voice, the voice that never wavered because it had
never once needed to:

"Go on through."

And that was all. That was Dun. He held his post until the last
child was in the yard and the first shieldmen relieved him, and
then he sat down against the doorpost he had defended, under the
standard he had carried, with the snow of the yard blowing in
past him, and was done.

Across the hall, the south door came open again.

Ede did not see how it began; the kitchens had no witnesses left
standing who could tell it whole. But the hall saw how it ended,
and the goblin cooks told the rest for the rest of their lives:
how the far door boomed shut with all the kitchen-folk trapped
on the hall side of it, how the Heralds turned from the bar to
the cooks with their knives — the Pressed of the serving-lines,
the smallest folk at the feast, the ones the tower had always
spent first — and how the man who came out of the smoke to stand
between was a Culler.

Hale unbarred the door alone. It cost him the time it costs, all
of it paid out of his own blood, and then he stood in the open
doorway with his grey cloak burning at the hem and held it — the
serjeant who had taught half the army's captains their trade,
sword in the old high guard he had drilled into Ede when she was
seventeen and stupid, holding the south door of the Frosthall
against the tower's own doctrine, *kill them all, they cannot
then be enemies* — holding it open, this time, so that everything
small could get out. The cooks went through under his arms. The
last of them, the smallest boy, the one whose elbow he had
steadied, would say all his life that Serjeant Hale looked down
at him going past and said something, and that it was, "Straight
home. Don't count us. Count yourself."

They found him in the doorway after, among six of them.

The Heralds died to a man. Once the first surprise was spent
there was no question of it — forty knives and a dozen bows
against nine thousand of the rising — and by the time the fires
were out there was not one left to put a question to. Each dead
server, stripped, wore the same thing under the shirt: a collar,
light-made, close-linked, willing. No one had forced these. They
had eaten this hall's bread with their own war under their
clothes.

The gathered world spent the night counting, because counting
was all there was.

Sixty-eight dead. Nineteen of them kitchen-folk and servers who
had been true — the goblin cooks laid their own out in the
Frozen Yard and would let no one else carry them, and then, at
dawn, six goblins carried Serjeant Hale of the Cullers out to
lie at the head of that row, and folded his burned grey cloak
under his head, and nobody in the rising said one word about
it, then or ever. Hobb Fennick lived; the steward who had
stepped in front of him did not, and the old man sat by her the
whole night with his hat off, doing the only thing he knew,
which was keeping count, and for once the counting did not
help. The charter lived. Calyne had cut it down from the
center-beam with her own blade when the rafters caught — the
grey spymaster, first to know, first armed, standing over the
rising's one page in a burning hall — and it had cost the
document a scorched corner, and Ede, taking it from her hands,
could not meet her eyes, and Calyne, being Calyne, said nothing
at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

Dun Harrow they laid on the High Seat's table with the banner
of Shieldwall over him, and the freed of the cages stood watch
in turns, all night, one-armed men and hollow-eyed women who
had been two years in the dark of the Warcamp because a route
was sold, standing over the man who had carried their name up
sixty floors and put it in a doorway.

And Berrin lived. The barber-surgeons drew the arrow before
midnight and called it a clean wound, high through the meat of
the back and shoulder — an inch of wind the other way, they
said, and it would have found the general's throat. Ede sat by
his cot in the Undercroft among the casks where the morning had
begun, and held his flour-scuffed hand while he slept, and did
the arithmetic of it over and over: he saw nothing, knew
nothing, had no shield and no sword and no reason, and he put
his body over hers between one heartbeat and the next, the way
you reach for bread before you know you are hungry. Whatever
else the night had taken, it had shown her the bottom of one
man, and the bottom was solid, and she had built on it half her
life.

When he woke, near dawn, the first thing he asked was whether
the cooks had got out.

The second thing, when she told him about Dun and about Hale,
was nothing at all. He turned his face away and cried like a
quartermaster: silently, briefly, and then he asked for the
lists of the dead, because someone would have to strike them
from the rolls, and it should be done kindly, and by someone
who had known them, and he was already reaching for his coat.

She took the lists from him and blew out the lamp and ordered
him, as his general, to sleep.

Then she went up alone into her burned capital, in the grey of
the morning, past the row in the yard and the watch at the
table, and stood under the black center-beam where the charter
had hung, and understood what the tower had bought at her feast
and what it had failed to buy. The paper was safe. The paper
would be re-hung by noon and the parliament would sit again by
week's end, harder and colder and wiser; the rising would
climb; none of that had died. What had died was the room where
no one watched the serving-lines. Sixty floors of war had not
managed to teach the gathered world suspicion, and one supper
had. From now on there would be tasters, and guards at the
gallery, and lists checked against lists, and every open door
would have a man remembering the north one.

Somebody in this hall had known the seating, she thought. And
the thought did not finish; it only stood there in her, cold,
patient, the first stone of a thing she would be building for
a long time.

*You are still wearing his blood,* Sliver said quietly, against
her palm. *The baker's. You should sleep, Ede.*

"After the names," she said. "Bring me ink."
