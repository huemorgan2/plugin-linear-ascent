# Chapter 1 — The Trainer of Other People's Recruits

The recruits came to the yard at first light, because Ede Stonhal had
told them the tower kept no honest mornings and they had better bring
their own. They came yawning out of the lodging-rows with their borrowed
shields on their backs, past the aether-forge already breathing blue and
the horse trough already skimmed with dust, and they made their line in
the cold without being told, which was the first thing she had taught
them and the last thing most captains bothered to.

There were eleven of them that season. A giant lad called Brom, sixteen
and the size of a door, who spoke slowly and finished every sentence he
began. The ferry twins, Lark and Fell, marsh-line stock, quick-handed,
raised on punts and eel-traps by grandmothers who remembered a drowned
country. A delver girl named Pike who could climb anything with a rope on
it and most things without. Seven more of the usual Roothollow mix — a
smith's daughter, two hedgers' sons, an elf boy who had never seen the
woods his mother sang about. Every people the theft had spilled, drilling
in one yard, under one voice.

"Shields," Ede said, and eleven shields came up, and she walked the line
and put two of them a hand higher with her fist.

She drilled them the way she had been drilled: hedge-lane work first,
because the first floor was hedges. How to take a wall of blackthorn on
your shoulder and not your face. How a lane fights — narrow front, no
flank, everything decided by whoever holds their feet. Then the counts.
She made them say the counts back to her until they could say them asleep,
because the counts were what came home and everything else was luck.

"Brackjaw," she said.

"Circles once," the line answered. "Always once. Then the head goes down
and it comes straight."

"So where are you when the head goes down?"

"Not in front of it," said Lark.

"Beside it," said Brom, "with my shield on the servo shoulder, because a
straight line cannot turn. I have finished."

"You're all dead a breath early," Ede said. "It circles once. You count
the circle. You don't spend the count admiring it." But she let them see
the nod, because they had earned the nod, and a line that is never fed
starves the same as any other living thing.

Two years she had run this yard. Two years she had drunk nothing the
horse trough couldn't give her, a ladle at a time, morning and noon, and
if her hand still went looking for something else at dusk, that was
between her and the dusk. Six companies she had trained in that time, and
walked to the Root Gate, and watched go up. Four of them had put names on
the Stone of the Climb — you could see the glow of the new cuts from the
yard on a clear night, floors broken, lifts opened, letters lit from
within like coals under a grate. Two of them had put names on the north
face instead, where nothing glows.

She trained other people's recruits and other people climbed with them.
That was the arrangement. Nobody in Roothollow said it to her face
anymore, which is how a town tells you it has given up on you politely.

The elf was watching her before she saw him, which she would later learn
was the usual order of things where Rhovan was concerned.

He was leaning at the yard's edge where the forge-light didn't reach,
grey from boots to collar, riding clothes cut for the high bridleways,
with an unstrung bow across his back and the stillness of a man who has
made a profession of being furniture. He had been there long enough to
watch the whole shield drill. When Pike's arrow-stance sagged on the
practice butts he crossed the yard without being asked and moved her back
elbow two fingers' width, and the next three shafts went where she meant
them, and he was back in his patch of no-light before Ede had finished
deciding to be annoyed.

She dismissed the line at noon and he was still there.

"You're either buying or begging," she said. "The yard fee's posted."

"Neither." He came into the light like something deciding to be seen.
Close up he was older than elves usually let you notice. "Rhovan. Of the
Grey Rides."

"That's a place, not an employer."

"It's both, at a distance." He looked at the trough, at the forge, at the
Stone standing off by the gate with its lit names and its dark ones, the
way a surveyor looks at ground he has already bought. "I keep other
people's secrets. It's steadier work than yours. I have one of yours in
my coat, and I'd rather hand it over than keep carrying it. It's heavier
than it looks."

"I don't have secrets. I have a record. Everybody knows my record."

"Everybody knows a story," Rhovan said. "The record's better."

She took him to the tack-shed she used for an office, because whatever
this was, it wasn't for the yard. Harness on the walls, a plank table, the
season's pay-ledger, a stool she didn't offer him. He laid a folded page
on the plank and set one finger on it and left the finger there, the way
you hold down something that might blow away or bite.

It was heavy paper, toll-house ruling, folded in three and sealed with
lead-grey wax that carried no sigil at all — which in Roothollow, where
every guild stamped everything down to its firewood, was its own kind of
signature. The fold was old. The wax was not the first wax; someone had
sealed this page more than once, in more than one place.

"Where's it from?"

"A ledger."

"Whose?"

"That," Rhovan said, "is the wrong end of the question. Read it."

She broke the wax. A clerk's fair hand, ruled entries, the ink gone
tea-brown. Dated at the top in the tower's own reckoning — she did the
sum against her own calendar twice, because the first time her mind
refused to hold the answer still. The week before she had marched. The
very week.

*Item. For the road of the Shieldwall Company, gate to gate, floors nine
to twenty, with hours, fords, and watchwords entire: paid in full.*

*Item. Delivery to be taken at the Red Warcamp, at the drums.*

*Item. Goods: one company, standards, stores, and persons, thirty and
some.*

*Received against the above the sum of —*

The page was cut. Not torn — cut, clean, with a knife and a straightedge,
the way a clerk trims what a clerk means to keep elsewhere. Whatever sum
had been paid, and whatever hand had taken it, lived on the half that was
still in the ledger this had been cut from.

The tack-shed went away for a while.

She stood in the mine-dark under floor twenty with her company breathing
around her and the drums starting up, and heard, for the first time
truly, what she had heard that night and buried under nine years of
asking the wrong question: the drums had been beating Shieldwall's own
march. Their own signal-cadence, the beat she used to move the line in
the dark, played back at them out of the black like a landlord jingling
your own keys. She had taken it for mockery, improvised, some drummer's
cruel good ear. It was not improvised. They had the watchwords. The gates
had barred behind the company, not before it — she had cursed her own
scouting for nine years over those gates — and Skarn's honor guard had
already been formed up on the spoil-heaps above the road, in the dark, in
silence, torches shuttered, waiting the way you wait for something whose
hours you have in writing. The whole floor had not fought them. It had
*received* them.

Not a defeat. She had led thirty-one people into a mine and it was never
a battle at all. It was a delivery, and she had carried it, on time, by
the marked road, to the drums.

She had spent nine years asking what she had done wrong. Here was the
answer, in a clerk's fair hand, with the important half cut away: nothing.
She had done nothing wrong, and it should have lifted something off her,
and instead it set down something far heavier in its place, because a
captain who fails can drill recruits and drink trough-water and call it
penance — but goods that were sold are owed a different debt entirely,
and the debt had a seller's name on it, and the name was on the half of
the page she didn't have.

"Who sold us," she said. Not a question. A requirement.

"The page doesn't say. Pages rarely do." Rhovan's voice stayed level as a
toll-bar. "Ledgers do."

"Then where's the ledger?"

He pointed one finger at the roof of the tack-shed, and through it, at
the whole stacked weight of the world. "Up."

He gave her the rest standing, in the fractions that she would come to
know as the whole of his conversation. The page had come to the Grey
Rides folded inside a dead courier's boot-lining, and who the courier was
was a second question, and who had killed him was a third, and Rhovan
answered neither. The people he worked for — he never once that afternoon
said the word *Wake*, and she noticed the not-saying — wanted the tower
down. They had wanted it down for a long time, and they had concluded,
in whatever grey room such things are concluded in, that towers do not
come down for secrets. They come down for armies, and armies gather
around a spine, and spines were scarcer than secrets.

"You want me to climb," she said.

"I want to hand this back to the person it belongs to and be done," said
Rhovan. "The people I answer to want you climbing. They are prepared to
be unpleasant about it. I'd sooner not be. Consider that my one virtue."

"Unpleasant."

"The page could go up the tower with you," he said, "or it could go to
the criers. Half this town already half-believes the captain sold her own
company and drank herself kind about it after. The page doesn't say she
didn't. Read it again if you doubt me. It names no seller. Papers like
this hang whoever's standing nearest when they're read aloud, and you
have been standing nearest for nine years."

Her hand had closed on the edge of the plank table. She watched her own
knuckles whiten with a sort of distant interest, the way you watch
weather over somebody else's fields. Two years dry, and she had never
once wanted the other thing so precisely, so much like thirst.

"Get out of my yard," she said.

"I will. One thing more." He said it with his hand already on the door,
his fraction of mercy, or his fraction of craft; she never did learn to
tell them apart. "I'm paid to ask three times. That's the term of my
contract. I'll be at the Root Gate at dawn for the first answer, and I'll
take whichever one you bring — but bring one." He looked once around the
tack-shed, at the harness and the pay-ledger and the stool she hadn't
offered, and then at her. "You count well. Everyone says so. The counts,
the recruits, the ladles from that trough. Count this: thirty and some,
sold by the head, and the seller's name cut clean, and kept. Somebody has
been carrying that name for nine years, the way you've been carrying the
blame for it. Of the two of you, I'd say the name weighs less. Its owner
has never once looked for the bottom of a cup."

Then he was gone, grey into grey, and the yard was just a yard.

She walked the town that evening the long way, which she had not done in
a year. Roothollow at lamplighting: every race the theft had spilled,
shoulder to shoulder in the wreck-built streets. Giants stooping under
lintels made of ship-keel. Elf children playing knucklebones on the steps
of a chapel built from four kinds of salvage and roofed in stair-lift
crating. The gate-queue for tomorrow's climbs already forming along the
wall, cook-fires, oiled shields, some captain's hired piper practicing
the same eight notes over and over, hopeful and thin. Past the granary
row the air went warm and brown — Berrin's ovens were awake, working the
night shift so the gate-queue could carry fresh bread up at dawn — and
she stood in the smell of it a breath longer than she meant to. That
smell had fed her through worse years than this one. It was the one thing
in Roothollow that had never once asked her how she was.

The Stone of the Climb stood by the Root Gate, a standing slab the height
of a giant, cut on every face. On the gate side glowed the names of the
breakers — every climber who had ever put a Warden down and opened a lift,
lit from within, oldest at the base, so that the whole stone stood in its
own shallow light. On the north face, in plain dead letters, the lost.

She had cut thirty of those letters' names herself, one each night, the
first winter she was sober, with a mason's chisel she returned every
morning and borrowed again every dusk. The mason had never charged her.
The town watched her do it and said nothing, which is how a town
forgives you for something it still believes you did.

Her thumb found the fourth name down without her eyes' help. DUN HARROW,
STANDARD-BEARER. The thumb had worn the letters faintly smooth already,
two winters of this, and she stood in the Stone's low light and did the
arithmetic Rhovan had left her with, and hated him for the neatness of
it.

If it was a sale, there was a seller. If there was a seller, there was a
buyer, and buyers keep what they pay for. Nine years she had mourned her
thirty as dead because dead was the worst thing, and she had trained
herself to believe the worst thing the way you train any recruit: daily,
until it holds its line. But the page said *goods*. The page said
*delivery*. You do not write *persons, thirty and some* on a bill of sale
for a massacre.

At her throat, under her shirt, hung a small tin on a leather thong, and
the tin was empty, and had been empty two years. She had carried a
splinter in it once — a sliver of smoky crystal no longer than a finger,
old Aldervale's own thinking aether, the shardmind that had chosen her at
this gate when she was younger than Brom. It had gone up with her every
climb. It had come down with her from the Warcamp, the only thing that
did. The day she cut the first name into the north face she had walked it
back to the shard-house and laid it in the rack with the unchosen, and
the keeper had said nothing at all, and the splinter had said one word,
one only, dry as a seed in the dark of the tin:

*Later.*

She had never repeated it to anyone. There was no one it could be
repeated to who would not ask what it meant, and she did not know what it
meant, and a captain does not hand out questions she cannot drill an
answer to.

She stood a long while at the Stone, until the piper by the wall gave up
on his eight notes and the cook-fires burned down to keeping-coals. Then
she walked home past the yard, and checked the trough's skim of dust out
of pure habit, and did not sleep.

Rhovan would be at the Root Gate at dawn, wanting the first of his three
answers. She knew the shape of the word already. It was small, and it fit
her mouth, and she had said it to herself so many years running that it
sounded like her name.
