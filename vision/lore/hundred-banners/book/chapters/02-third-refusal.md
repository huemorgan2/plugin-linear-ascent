# Chapter 2 — The Third Refusal

Rhovan was at the Root Gate before the light was, standing against the
wall with the patience of a man being paid by the day. Behind him the
gate-queue was waking: captains kicking their people upright, shields
being oiled by feel, somebody's kettle, somebody's prayer. The great arch
of the stair-lift stood over it all in the half-dark, black iron carved
by no hand anyone loved, and the queue kept its backs to it the way men
keep their backs to a cliff-edge they work beside every day.

"No," Ede said.

He didn't move. "You're sure."

"I train them. Other people climb with them. That's the arrangement, and
the arrangement holds. I led thirty-one people up that stair once and I
came down alone, and I will not stand at the front of a line again while
there's breath in me. If the Wake wants a spine, buy one. The queue's
full of captains."

"It is," Rhovan agreed, looking at the queue, and managed to make the
two words do the work of an insult with no part of them you could
answer. He straightened off the wall. "That's one, then."

"That's the only."

"That's one," he said again, mild as milk, and walked into the waking
market, and was grey, and then was gone.

She ran the yard hard that day, and harder the day after. If the
recruits noticed that the counts came faster and the nod came never,
they had the sense to keep it among themselves. The page she kept folded
in the lining of her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel the
corner of it when she breathed deep, and so she breathed shallow, and
told herself that was drill discipline, and knew better all the while.

*Goods. Delivery. Thirty and some.* She woke at the hour of the theft's
old thunder every night that week, with the drums in her ears, beating
her own march.

Gate-day came at the week's end, as it came every week: the morning the
lifts took up whoever had signed the climb-roll, and Roothollow turned
out to watch its bravest and its most foolish walk under the arch. Her
eleven were signed to go up under a hired captain named Corvel — a
southern-wall man with good teeth and a bought company, who had inspected
her recruits the way you inspect a horse's mouth, and paid the yard fee
in full, and called her *the trainer* to her face without once asking if
she had another name.

On the eve of it, when drill was done and the others had gone to their
suppers, Brom stayed behind in the yard. He stood in front of her with
his shield still on his arm, and she watched him build the sentence first
in his head, stone by stone, the way giants do, so that it would stand
up on its own when he set it down between them.

"You taught us the counts of the first nineteen jailers," he said. "You
taught us where Brackjaw's shoulder is, and what the flooded drift hides,
and how a lane fights. Tomorrow we climb behind a man who paid a fee, and
you will stand at this trough, and count ladles. I would rather climb
behind the counts' own keeper. So would all eleven. We took a vote, which
Captain Corvel does not know about. I am asking you to come and stand at
the front of us. I have finished."

The yard was very quiet. Out past the wall the gate-queue's fires were
being lit, one after another, little answers to the dark.

"No," Ede said.

It came out of her flat and fast, before anything gentler could get in
front of it — the second refusal, and the first one that cost her.
Brom's face did not change; giants' faces don't; the hurt goes down into
them like frost into ground, to work all winter where you can't see it.
He nodded once, and thanked her for the trough water — for two seasons of
it, formally, the whole courtesy, while she stood there with the page
against her ribs — and shouldered his shield, and went to his supper.

She heard the tale had traveled by the way the lodging-row went silent
when she passed it. A town the size of Roothollow has no secrets, only
kindnesses, and it was being kind to her again, and she could not bear
it.

She did not go home. She went where she had gone through every bad night
of the drunk years and half the good ones since, down the granary row,
into the brown warmth, through the door that was never barred to her:
Berrin's bakehouse.

⁂

He was at the night ovens with his sleeves rolled and flour to the
elbows, a big man gone comfortably heavy, grey in the beard and quick in
the hands, sliding loaves for tomorrow's gate-queue off the peel in
ranks as neat as any shield-line. The whole room smelled like the one
good memory everyone in Roothollow had in common. Grain-ledgers stood
shelved along the wall by the door, spines cracked with use — he kept
the counts of half the town's eating, did Berrin Or, and had since
before she'd owned a shield.

He looked her over once, the way he'd looked her over across that
threshold a hundred times, and reached down two bowls, and filled them
from the pot that lived at the back of the oven-top, and set hers on the
flour-barrel she always sat on. That was Berrin entire: he fed you first
and found out what was wrong with you after, on the sound theory that
half of what was wrong with anyone was supper.

"Eleven good recruits," he said, tearing bread for them both, "one hired
captain with teeth like a fair-day pony, and my second-best flour going
up the lift with them at dawn. And you've eaten nothing since noon, by
the look you're wearing. Sit."

"You've heard, then."

"I've heard six versions. Roothollow's a wonderful town; you can hear
your own supper argued about before you've cooked it." He settled onto
the sacks across from her with his bowl. "I've also heard about your
grey visitor. That elf drinks at the Long Trough and says nothing so
loudly you can hear it two streets off. There's paper involved, they
say. Old paper."

So she told him. All of it — the page, the fair hand, the cut edge, the
week of the date; the drums beating her own march; Rhovan and his three
askings. It came out of her in drill-order, flat, itemized, because that
was the only way she could carry it across a room, and Berrin listened
the way he did everything, entirely, wiping the bowl with his bread and
watching her over it, and when she had finished he was quiet for a long
breath.

"Well," he said at last. "First thing. Whoever sold a company by the
head and let the page out of his sight was a fool, and I'd tell him so
to his face, and then I'd hold him while you settled up." He said it
easily, the way he said everything, and reached to top up her bowl.
"Second thing, and look at me while I say it, because it's the thing
you walked here not to hear. Nobody pays warcamp prices for the dead,
Ede. The dead are free. It's the living that cost."

"Don't."

"*Goods*," he said, not letting her off. "*Delivery. Persons, thirty and
some.* You're the counter, girl, not me — but I've kept ledgers since
before you could lift a shield, and I'll tell you what that paper is.
That's not a butcher's bill. That's a bill of lading. You don't write
*persons* on what you mean to spoil."

"They've been nine years in cages, if they're anywhere at all."

"Then they've been nine years counting doors," Berrin said, "and nobody's
come. Suppose Dun is breathing. Just suppose it, one breath's worth,
you can put it down after. He's kept your standard and your counts nine
years in the dark, because that's what that man is. What's his count of
*you* at, do you reckon, by now?"

It was the cruelest thing anyone had said to her in two years, and he
said it with such plain love that she couldn't even stand up to leave.

"I'd get them killed," she said. "Whoever follows me — that's what
follows me. You know what I am now. I train. I don't lead."

"What you are now," Berrin said, "is hidden. And hiding's had two good
years, I'll grant it — it got you sober, it got six companies trained,
I'll not sneer at it. But grief you tend like a garden isn't grief
anymore, it's comfort, and I've sold enough comfort across this counter
to know it by smell." He set his bowl aside. "You want to go up alone
and settle it and die of it tidily — no, don't trouble to deny it, I've
watched you want things across this room for ten years. That's the
drink talking in a new voice, that's all that is. The other road is
worse and better: take the children. Stand at the front. Teach them from
the front, where it costs — you've been teaching from the safe end so
long you've forgotten the front is where the trade's honest."

The ovens ticked. Somewhere in the back a prentice was stacking wood.

"And there's a last thing," he said, getting up, brushing flour, "the
practical thing, so you know I mean it. Sign the roll at dawn and I'll
victual you to floor twenty myself — flour, boots, lamp-oil, the good
wax against the wet floors, all of it on the granary's book. You'll sign
for every sack, mind; friendship's friendship and a ledger's a ledger."
He stood over her, and for a moment all the ease went out of him and
what was left was old and steady as the Stone. "And when you find the
book that page was cut from — and you will, you find everything you've
ever gone looking for, it's the rest of us that lose things — bring me
the name that signed it. Just the name. I'll bake for the hanging."

She looked up at him — the flour, the grey beard, thirty years of other
people's suppers — and felt the *no* she had carried for a week come
apart quietly, like a knot that had only ever needed one honest pull.

"The good boots," she said.

"Take the good boots," Berrin agreed, and his grin came back, and he
went to see to his loaves.

She was at the Root Gate before the light was, and this time it was
Rhovan who found her already standing there.

He did not gloat, she gave him that. He watched her sign the climb-roll
at the gate-warden's table — *Ede Stonhal, of Roothollow, and company* —
in the grey before dawn, with the queue stirring and craning all down
the wall, and all he said was, "That's a strange spelling of *no*."

"You're still owed a third refusal," she said. "Keep it. I may want it
someday."

Word ran down the queue faster than the light did. Corvel came up with
his contract in his fist and his teeth on display and got as far as
"the fee" before Brom stepped out of his line — unhurried, complete,
like a wall deciding to move — and set himself behind Ede's shoulder.
"I had not yet signed your roll," he said. "Now I will not. I have
finished." Lark and Fell were already there. Pike came over the baggage
rather than around it. The rest followed the way the rest always
follow, and Corvel looked at his eleven recruits become none, and did
the one wise thing she ever saw him do: he pocketed the contract and
kept his teeth shut.

The last of it happened at the shard-house.

It stood beside the arch as it had stood all her life: a dry stone vault,
older-built than anything else in Roothollow because the shards abide no
damp, ranked inside with a hundred waiting splinters on their felted
racks like a choir that has been holding one long note since the Theft.
Every climber signed and then walked the racks, and mostly nothing
happened, and once in a season some crystal woke for a stranger and the
town had a feast. Mother Cail kept it, an old delver woman with a lamp
on her brow at all hours, who had kept it since before the Stone had
names.

Ede had no intention of walking the racks. She was past the doorway,
head down, making for the arch and the assembling line, when the light
changed.

It came through the vault door sideways, thin and white, and the queue's
murmur died from the near end to the far one like a sluice shutting. In
the racks, in the third row, in the place it had lain for two years
among the unchosen, a splinter of smoky crystal no longer than a finger
had kindled from the inside — and then it rose, unhurried, over the
felt and the sleeping ranks of its kin, and crossed the vault, and came
out under the lintel into the morning, and hung in the air before her at
the height of her throat. Exactly at the height, she understood dully,
of a small tin on a leather thong.

"They don't re-choose," Mother Cail said from the doorway. Her lamp was
shaking. She said it to the queue, to the gate-warden, to forty years of
her own keeping. "Write it down, someone. They have never — in the whole
record, back to the first gate, a shard set down has never once—"

*I told you: later,* said Sliver, dry as a seed, ancient as the lamps of
drowned Aldervale, and not quietly. It spoke the way the Stone glowed,
from the inside of things; every soul along the wall heard it in their
own bones, and she watched the nearest captains take their caps off
without knowing they did it. *It is later.*

"You could have said more," Ede managed. "At the time."

*Forty-one climbers I have carried. Nine were stubborn enough to matter.
You were the ninth. You handed me back like a borrowed knife, Ede
Stonhal, and I have spent two years in a drawer full of children,
listening to your counts across the yard through six feet of stone. They
have improved. You have not. We will work on it as we go.*

Her hand came up, and the splinter settled onto her palm with the weight
of nothing at all, of a name, of a debt — warm, familiar, hers. The tin
was open at her throat before she had decided to open it.

*One more thing said publicly, since we are being public.* The light in
it steadied to a working glow. *I do not re-choose. Let the record
stand: she set me down. I never set her down.*

The queue made a sound then, all down the wall — not a cheer, nothing so
tidy; more the sound a town makes when it puts something down after
carrying it for years. And Ede Stonhal turned from the shard-house with
her splinter lit at her throat and her eleven at her back and Rhovan
falling in grey and silent at her shoulder, and walked under the black
arch of the Root Gate, onto the stair-lift her name had been absent from
for nine years, and climbed.

The lift ran up its chain into the dark of the first floor's underside —
roots, hanging stone, the old severed veins of a stolen country — and
then through, into a flat white glare that was no sun's, where the
hedges of a farm valley ran their straight green lines to the horizon
under the Yoke's false light, hung where a sun should hang, which never
set, and never moved a shadow.

Somewhere out in that stillness, she knew, an old farmer was already
counting the lift-chain's links by ear, tallying the climbers coming up
into his fields against the ones who would come back to the gate.

The counts were about to change.
