# Chapter 23 — The Reckoning of Names

Rhovan of the Grey Rides had spent a long life sorting what people said
from what was so, and he had learned that the second thing lives in
small rooms. Proclamations are for halls. Truth keeps to pantries,
stair-feet, the space between a question and its answer. So in the
days after the feast, while the Frosthall was scrubbed and re-beamed
and the parliament talked bravely of sitting again, Rhovan did what he
was made for. He found the small rooms, and he stood in their corners,
and he watched the rising learn to be suspicious.

The first small room was the annex off the hall's west wall, where the
muster-rolls of the whole gathered world had been racked in their
cases, company by company, floor by floor — two years of Berrin's neat
quartermaster's hand, every name that had ever eaten the rising's
bread — and where the fire had done its most thorough work. The
rafters of the great hall were scorched; the annex was ash. A
pitch-arrow through its one window, the wardens judged, in the first
breath of the attack. Of everything the Heralds burned that night,
nothing had burned so well as the names.

Ede stood in the ash of it on the third morning and said she would
rebuild the rolls from memory.

Rhovan was in the doorway and heard the whole of what followed, and
counted it, as he counted all exchanges, in the private ledger he
kept behind his eyes. Berrin — up from his cot against the surgeons'
word, arm slung, grey-faced, moving like a man twice his years —
protested, and his protests were all of them sound. There were fair
copies of the older rolls in Roothollow, he said; send down for them,
six weeks and the bulk of it is back, why spend yourself. The new
musters could be re-sworn company by company; let the captains carry
it. She had not slept three nights; the rising needed its general,
not its clerk. And when none of that moved her, the last protest,
the true one, the one that made half the annex turn and look at him:
"Ede. I kept those rolls. Every name in them went through this hand.
Let the man who lost them be the man who digs them back."

"You didn't lose them, and you have one arm to dig with," Ede said.
"I am not spending you either, old friend. Sit down before you fall
down."

And then — Rhovan watched it happen — she called for trestles, and
ink, and the captains of every company in the muster, and she began.
Not from the Roothollow copies. From her own head, and from
everyone's: each captain called their company from memory, each
soldier named their bench-neighbors, each keeper their floor-folk,
cross-sworn, witnessed, written fair by three clerks at three
separate tables so that no single hand held the record. It took nine
days. It became, though no one had planned it — Rhovan was never
sure whether even Ede had planned it — a thing the whole army passed
through, a mustering of the living to answer the burning of the
names; folk queued in the yard past the row of graves to swear to
one another's existence, and wept doing it, and came away steadier.
The Frosthall's skalds already had a song about the feast. By the
ninth day they had a quieter one about the lists.

Berrin sat where she had put him, and could not write, and so did
what he did: he fed the work. Broth to the clerks' tables, warm
lofts for captains who had queued past midnight, and his own
prodigious memory laid open to anyone who was short a name — he
knew every soul's floor and trade and mess-line, all of it given
freely, checked and re-checked against the sworn testimony like
everything else. *A ledger of him,* Rhovan thought, watching him
help an old Fencerows widow recall her dead husband's company, and
meant it in admiration, and filed the thought away in the manner of
his trade, which files everything.

The second small room was the Undercroft, past midnight, nine days
after the feast, among the casks. Calyne had chosen it. Rhovan
understood the choosing; it was where the general had refused her
warning, and Calyne, who was owed an apology from the world and
would die before requesting one, took her satisfactions in
geography.

Two lamps. One barrel-head. On it, side by side, for the first
time in the twenty years he had served her, the whole of what each
of them knew.

"Ciphers, then," she said. "Mine first. Since half this army has
me buried under the traitor's cairn already, we may as well begin
with the accused."

It should be said plainly, because Rhovan said nothing plainly
aloud in those years and someone must: agent and handler had never
once opened their books to each other. That is the grammar of the
Wake. A handler knows what her agent is for; an agent knows what
he is fed; the space between is the faction's fortune, and you do
not spend the fortune on trust. What happened on that barrel-head
was, by every law Rhovan had lived under since the Grey Rides,
treason twice over — his fractions and her ledgers, laid flat in
lamplight for a colleague to read whole. He gave her the last of
what he had held back from the Broodthrone year, the pieces he
had kept even after his leak to Vyx: the courier who had passed
the spider-queen's web twice, once going up, once having already
been. She gave him five years of intercepts in Malgrim's
hand-code, third form, and her workings beneath them, and he
began, strip by strip, to see the shape she had been walking
around alone all this time.

The shape was this. One hand, appearing in the traffic across
twenty years, always in fair-copy — a clerk's trained script,
Roothollow-schooled, the kind every counting-house south of the
tower taught its apprentices, no flourish, no error. Rhovan set
the strips in date-order down the barrel-head like a man laying
out a hand of cards. The hand had priced a mercenary company's
route the season of the Red Warcamp. The hand had moved payment
through the toll-ledgers — his own burglary, the page from floor
five, matched two of Calyne's intercepts to the week. The hand
had requested extraction once, years back, and been refused —
*a ledger entry does not retire,* the reply had said, and Calyne
had kept both halves of that exchange like a pressed flower.
And the hand had written, in the strip from the Cindermouth
drummer, the speaking-order of a council that met behind shut
doors at the top of the freed world.

"Malgrim's willing ones sign nothing," Calyne said. "No names,
no marks. It is the whole craft of the collar — the tower owns
them and never once writes it down. So we will not catch the
hand by its name. We catch it by its reach." She set one pale
finger on the last strip. "Whoever wrote this was in the room,
or was told by someone in the room within a day. I have been
fifteen years narrowing a world to that room, Rhovan. I need
eyes I did not train. Tell me what I have wrong."

He looked a long time.

"Nothing wrong," he said at last. "One thing missing. You've
built it all on paper, and paper is your country, so you trust
it. But the feast wasn't paper. Whoever called that strike fed
Malgrim something no cipher carries well." He tapped the
barrel-head. "Where everyone would be sitting."

It was Nix who found it, because of course it was Nix. The dead
Heralds had been searched by soldiers, and soldiers search for
weapons and papers and gold, and had duly found knives and
collars and nothing else, and the forty of them lay under
canvas in the ice-house yard awaiting the parliament's ruling
on where you bury such men. Nix went down among them on the
tenth day, alone, with a lamp. Asked later why, he said the
count was wrong — the goblin cooks had served the eastern line
with eleven jugs and only ten were smashed or found, and a
missing jug had been itching him for a week, and no, the jug
never did turn up, but you don't find the thing you're
counting, you find the thing that's *there*.

What was there was a fold of paper, flat along the inside of a
dead server's boot-shaft, between leather and lining, where a
man keeps what must survive his pockets being turned out. The
boot had been by the west wall when the rafters dripped fire;
its owner had burned along one whole side. The paper had not.

Rhovan was fetched first, before even Ede, which told him what
the goblin's quick eyes had already made of it. They spread it
on the ice-house floor by lamplight, weighted with pebbles at
the corners like a treaty.

It was the seating of the feast of the charter. All of it —
every table, every bench, the whole gathered world in its
places, copied fair in an anonymous serving-hand onto one
page. And it was not copied from the lists posted in the
kitchens on the feast's eve, because Rhovan walked to the
kitchens that same hour and checked: the posted lists had the
Coalbrows, brother and sister, at the west table, where Bruna
Hearthold had moved them on the last day to honor the
forge-oath, an old giants' courtesy. The paper in the boot had
them at the north table, among the keepers.

They had sat at the west table. But they had been *going* to
sit at the north one — for exactly as long as the seating was
a draft, in council, before it was ever posted. The paper in
the Herald's boot was a copy of the plan, not of the feast.
Marked, in a small careful ring of ink, around the High
Seat: the inner table. The charter's circle.

"So," said Calyne, into the ice-house cold.

Rhovan stood over the paper and did what he and the goblin
both did in extremity. He counted.

Who had been in the room with the draft? The council of the
charter's precedence, called to settle who sat where before
pride could unsettle it — five days before the feast, the
annex, shutters barred, one page passed hand to hand around
one small table. Ede, who had approved it. Berrin, who had
drawn it. Voss, who had disputed three benches' worth of
Tollmen and been overruled. Ulfar Coalbrow, who had stood on
precedence for the Forge-Court. Calyne, seated in council by
right since the parliament's founding, who had said nothing
and remembered everything. And Rhovan of the Grey Rides,
present at his handler's shoulder, seen by all, suspected
already by half.

Six souls. He said the count aloud, and the ice-house held
it the way small rooms hold the truth.

Every soul of the six had signed the charter. Every soul of
the six had bled for the rising, or fed it, or led it. One
of the six had a light collar under the shirt and had walked
out of that shuttered room and written the gathered world's
seating out fair for the tower — and knives had gone down
the serving-lines by that paper, and Dun Harrow had two
arrows by it, and a Culler lay at the head of a row of
goblin graves by it, and none of the six could now be
believed about anything, including him doing the counting.

"Keep all six on your list," Calyne said, watching him.
"Yourself among them. That is the discipline. The heart
strikes names off; the ledger keeps them." A pause; the
lamp guttered; and the grey voice, for one breath, was
almost gentle. "Hers will be the hardest to keep on. She
took an arrow's worth of proof at that table. Keep it on
anyway."

He never asked which name she meant. That was the other
discipline.

The parliament sat again at the month's turn, under the
re-hung charter with its scorched corner, which the clerks
had offered to trim square and Ede had forbidden — *let it
show what it cost* — and voted the climb resumed. There was
no argument. Grief had done what four days of debate never
managed: the gathered world was of one mind, and the mind
was cold, and it pointed up.

On the last night at the Frosthall, Rhovan went out alone
into the Frozen Yard, past the graves with their new stones
— sixty-eight, and two apart under one standard and one
grey cloak, where the snow was kept swept — and stood at
the yard's north wall, and looked at where the army was
going. Above the hall's dark roofline the sixty-first gate
stood open, and beyond it there was no more country. The
world ran out. From here the Ascent climbed into its own
sky: he could see, faint past the gate in the high air's
lamp-dark, the first of the great mooring-chains slung
between the floors like the rigging of a moored world —
a road of iron links, each wide as a wagon, hung over
cloud and nothing, going up. Chainrest, the sky-folk
called the town at its foot. Somewhere past it hung the
wreck-fields, and the harpy-passes, and the storm with a
queen inside it that the Wake's old files spoke of in the
tone reserved for debts.

The rising would walk that chain, all nine thousand of it,
one behind the other over the fall of the world, and
Rhovan would walk it carrying his ledger of six names, and
one of the six would be walking it too, a knife's length
away, all the way up.

He found he was looking forward to the sky. Elves answer
even themselves with fractions, but there it was, whole,
and he let himself keep it: up there, at least, on a road
one link wide, everybody's hands would be where he could
see them.
