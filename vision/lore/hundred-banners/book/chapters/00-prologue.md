# Prologue

They sent the boy down the valley at dusk with the last two sacks of
barley, because the miller ground cheapest for whoever came latest and
his mother would not pay a vain man's price for the sake of an early
night. He was seven. The sacks together outweighed him, the mill road ran
a long way by the river in the dark, and he had been angry about all of
it. Afterward he spent years trying to remember anything else of that
evening — the color of the sky, whether she stood in the door to watch
him go, whether the dog walked him to the gate — and found only the
anger, and the weight of the barley, and the smell of the kitchen he had
walked out of. His mother had set the loaves in before he left. She
always baked on mill nights. Bread against his coming home.

The sound began while he was still short of the mill, and it came from
under his feet, which was wrong. Thunder lives in the sky. This lay down
in the roots of the valley and turned over, and the river jumped in its
bed, and the barley hummed against his back like a hive.

He saw what came next in pieces, the way a child sees anything too big
for him. A line of white light ran along the ridgetops where no road had
ever been. The old boundary oaks tilted all together, courteous, like men
taking off their hats. And the fields went up. Not thrown. Not falling
the wrong way. Lifted — the whole valley leaving the ground in one piece,
hedges and lanes and the stream still lying in its bed, rising as slow
and level as a tray of loaves carried to table by something that did not
mean to spill them.

The boy dropped the sacks and ran back the way he had come, under the
shadow of his own country.

Above him hung the underside of home. Roots first: whole hanging forests
of them, oak-root and hedge-root and the long pale roots of the pasture
grass, combed downward and dripping. The well he had drunk from every day
of his life passed over his head as a severed stone throat, raining. The
duck-pond poured off its broken edge in a silver sheet that fell and fell
and never seemed to strike anything. And in among the roots and the
hanging stones there was lamplight — window after window still burning,
calm and yellow, in the black belly of the risen ground, because nobody
up there had learned yet to be afraid of the dark underneath them.

He found their kitchen by its light. He knew it the way you know your own
door in a town of doors. He ran beneath it calling until his voice went,
and the valley did not stop rising, and the little window climbed up
among the other windows, and the windows climbed up among the stars, and
then he could not tell them from the stars, and then it was quiet.

It rained soil for a while after. Clods, and slates off a shaved roof,
and one bruised windfall apple, which he picked up and held in both hands
and could not eat. High above him, small and growing smaller, he could
hear the dog barking. In all its life that dog had never stopped barking
before somebody came.

He did not leave the rim of the pit where the valley had been. Others
gathered there over the next days: a drover caught out on the road with
half his herd; the miller and his sons — the mill had stood a spear-throw
outside the cut, and had ground on, stupidly, all that night; two
hedgers; a wet-nurse from the far bank; a peddler who wept over nothing
he could name. The left-behind. What the thief had not troubled to take.
They made a camp of what had shaken loose from the underside as the land
rose: half a barn, a length of good fence, a horse trough, and a cold
aether-forge dropped whole out of somebody's smithy, its coals scattered
like a hand of black coins.

On the third night a clerk came walking the rim of the pit.

It was taller than a man and thinner, grey the way paper is grey, with a
lantern in one hand and a book in the crook of its arm, and a fine chain
at its throat worn the way a reeve wears a chain of office. It walked the
whole rim with its lips moving, counting. It did not hurt anyone. That
was the strangest thing about it, and the hardest to forgive. The drover
threw a stone at it, and it noted the stone in the book.

When it reached the boy it stopped. He had not eaten since the barley
went down with the light, and he would not leave the rim, and he was
sitting with the apple he could not eat still in his hands.

"You are not in any of my columns," the clerk said. Its voice was the
finest thing about it — kind, even, reasonable, the voice of someone who
has never once needed to raise it.

It asked him his name, and he gave it, because he was a child and grown
things were asking. The clerk wrote the name low on a page already thick
with names, and what it wrote there the boy could not read, and this
account will not repeat it. Then it reached into its satchel and brought
out a dark loaf, heavy tower bread, and broke the heel off, and held the
heel out to him across the space between them.

"The tower feeds what it counts," the clerk said. "Freely given. You may
throw it in the pit if it pleases you. Some do."

He was seven years old, and every loaf that had ever been baked for him
hung a country over his head, straight up, going cold in a dead oven. He
ate it. It was good — that was the shame he kept longest, longer than any
of the rest of it: it was good bread, and he was glad of it, and it cost
him nothing at all. Nothing is what everything costs, at first.

The clerk watched him eat with an expression he would spend the next
fifty years learning to make himself — patient, kindly, keeping count —
and then it closed the book and walked on along the rim, and its lantern
went away into the dark, and he never told anyone. Not that night, and
not any night after, through all the years when telling might still have
mattered.

In time the left-behind stopped waiting at the rim and began to build.
Others found them — from other pits, other shaved valleys, straggling in
along roads that now ended in air — men and elves and the slab-shouldered
giants of the mountain forges, delvers, marsh-folk, ash-nomads, everyone
the theft had spilled and nobody had counted worth taking. The tower grew
over them ring by ring until it shut out the northern stars, and the camp
grew under it into a town of every people on the ground, hammered out of
wreckage, an aether-forge beside a horse trough. It stood at the root of
the stolen world, in the hollow the theft had left, and the name followed
of itself.

The boy grew up with the town. He learned his letters, and he learned his
sums, and he learned — quicker than the hedgers' boys, quicker than the
drover's sons — the one lesson the clerk had taught him without meaning
to, or by meaning it entirely, and which no one who was fed that night
ever wholly unlearned: that the world can be taken up like a tray of
loaves; that somebody keeps the ledger of the taking; and that the ledger
decides who eats.

On some nights, when the wind came down the black iron and the undersides
of the low floors glimmered with their hanging lamps, he would stand at
the edge of the town and look up. Somewhere above him, in a kitchen he
could have found blindfolded, the oven was long cold, and the loaves were
still in it.
