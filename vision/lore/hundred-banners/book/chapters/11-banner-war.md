# Chapter 11 — The Banner War

Rhovan reached the Tithe Barrows two days ahead of everyone's better
nature, and spent them the way he spent most days: on a height, in the
grey, counting other people's fires.

Floor twenty-nine was grave-country — the old barrow-fields where Men had
laid their dead with a tithe of grave-goods since before anyone now
breathing, mound after grassed mound to the horizon under a still sky,
each great barrow crowned with one black yew. It was the most peaceful
floor he had seen in the tower, and it was patiently wrong. The
grass grew too green over the newest mounds. Beetle-runs riddled the turf
like veins. Pale rings of death-cap stitched themselves around barrow
after barrow, which meant — the keeper had told him within the first
hour — that the mounds were readying to open, and the only thing holding
the floor asleep was that nobody had yet given the dead a grievance.

Three armies were now camped on it, working on that.

From the Great Yew ridge Rhovan read the fires like a ledger, because a
ledger was what it was. Southeast, the Cullers: low fires, straight rows,
grey pennons at half-staff for the twenty silk-wrapped dead they had
carried up three floors to bury in the only grave-earth of Men on the
climb. Northwest, the Tollmen: bright fires, good wine, and a chain —
Voss's factors had come up the freed lifts behind the host like gulls
behind a plough, and they had found the one gate on the tier worth
owning: the lych-gate stair, the arch of standing lych-stone through
which every burial on this floor had passed for a thousand years, and
through which the whole rising must pass to reach the Kingsbarrow above.
The chain was across it. The toll was posted. *Canon law grants free
passage to freed gates*, someone had painted underneath, and someone else
had painted, in a factor's tidy hand, *the lift is free — the road to it
is maintained.*

And between the two, on the low mounds, his own people. Forty bows of the
Wake in ride-grey, faces he had known three hundred years, camped in a
crescent around the Tollmen's chain — because the Wake's masters had
taken Gilder Voss's fee to guard his tolls. It was, Captain Sael had told
him, with a courtesy sharpened on both edges, *a
contract, Rhovan, the kind of thing our employers sign; you used to know
the word.* He had answered with a fraction — that he was the rising's
scout and would remain one — and they had parted like relatives, which
was the trouble.

Threading between all three camps went a small exhausted man with a
lantern and a book: Sef Wakewater, barrow-warden, keeper of the floor,
going fire to fire to fire like a moth working three flames. He carried
the vigil-book, the rolling account of which mounds had stirred and in
what order they would open, and read from it to any who would listen,
always with the same sermon after: *no blood on the grass. Quarrel where
you like, but not on the mounds. Every soul below this turf is owed a
tithe, the fever has soaked into the grave-gold, and the dead are light
sleepers now. Spilled blood is a debt, and this floor* — he would slap
the book — *this floor forecloses.*

Rhovan believed him. Rhovan had been telling the truth in fractions for
three hundred years and knew a whole truth when one walked past him twice
a day with a lantern.

Nobody else believed him at all.

⁂

The spark, when it came, was a toll charged on the dead.

The Culler burial-column came up the gate road at dawn — twenty biers,
forty bearers, Serjeant Hale walking in front with his helm under his
arm, the first time in forty years that any man had seen the top of
Hale's head. They came to the lych-gate; there was no other door the
ground would own. Rhovan, on the ridge, saw the column halt at the chain,
and saw the factor come out of the toll-booth with his slate, and could
not hear the words and did not need to. Voss's schedule of rates was
posted on the arch. It had a line for goods, a line for beasts, a line
for armed men.

The factor — Quist, a careful man — consulted the slate, and the
schedule, and named the rate for twenty biers.

For one heartbeat the whole floor seemed to hold its breath —
the crows silent in the yews, the death-cap rings pale and patient — as
if the ground itself wanted to hear whether the living would really do
this.

Then a Culler corporal named Dree, who had carried his own brother up
three floors of stairs, said something short, and took his axe to the
chain — and a Tollman guard put a halberd through his shoulder — and Hale
bellowed, too late, into the exact silence where his authority used to
be — and from the crescent camp on the mounds, honoring the letter of a
contract signed by people who had never seen this field, the Wake's bows
loosed.

Rhovan watched his own people's arrows go up. Ride-grey fletching against
a grey sky, a sight that had meant *home* to him for three centuries,
rising off the burial mounds of Men in defense of a toll-chain. He did
not remember starting to run.

What followed was the ugliest fight of the climb, and the smallest, and
he saw all of it, because he took part in none of it. The Cullers stormed
the toll-booth and the toll-booth's silver; the Tollmen's guards — half
of them freed men, wearing Voss's livery over Warcamp scars, killing men
who had opened their cages — held the arch; Sael's
bows commanded the mounds and made the open turf a killing-ground,
precise, contractual, each arrow an itemized regret. Three banners. Not
one of them the tower's. The real enemy was a floor up and seventy floors
up and did not need to lift a finger, and on the vigil-ground by the
lych-gate, Tithegrim — the field's welded collector, a barrow-hound risen
and saddled with a rider of grave-gold — stood motionless at the battle's
heart, and watched.

It had been waiting five years, Rhovan understood, cold to his boots. It
was a collector. It never interrupted a payment.

Blood went into the barrow-grass — Culler blood, Tollman blood, and then,
when Dree's maddened friends rushed the crescent, Wake blood too, the
first elf-blood spilled on the climb, and the turf took it the way a
ledger takes a figure.

Sef Wakewater ran into the open between the lines, alone, both arms up,
the vigil-book over his head like a shield, screaming at three armies
that the rings were widening — and it says everything about that hour
that the crows heard him and rose from the yews all together in a black
sheet, and the men did not hear him at all.

That was when the wagons came.

⁂

Rhovan smelled them before he saw them, and so did every soldier on the
field, and that was the whole difference — a smell that went through that
battle like a rumor of another world: baking. Warm bread, wood smoke,
salt pork, the sweet dark ash-barley loaf of the Roothollow gate ovens —
the smell of the market square on a winter morning, of home before the
war made everyone on that field a soldier. Up the gate road from the
freed lifts came a supply-train twenty wagons long, ox-drawn, lamplit
against the grey, and on the lead wagon, standing, reins in one fist and
his hat in the other, bellowing names — not orders, names — came
Quartermaster Berrin Or.

"DREE! Corporal Dree, you're bleeding on good grass, sit *down*, your
mother sold me eggs! QUIST! I hold your paper, Quist, you owe me eleven
crowns and I am not collecting from a corpse, get your men behind the
booth! SAEL! Captain! Strings *down*, my love, whatever he's paying it
isn't worth arrows at grocers —"

He drove the lead wagon straight in between the lines — through the
arrow-fall, at a walk, like weather — and halted it broadside in the
killing-ground, and stood on the seat-board, a stout man of sixty with
flour on his sleeves, and possibly the only person in the tower whom no
faction on that field could imagine shooting. Behind him the train came
on: bread, beer, salt, boot-leather, arrow-shafts, lamp-oil, climbing the
freed tower because one man had spent the whole war making sure it did.

"Right," Berrin said, into the ringing quiet, in the voice of a man
opening a market. "Nobody fights well hungry. It's wasteful, and I've
brought supper. After supper, if you all still want killing each other,
I'll be no further use — but you'll do it fed, in marching order, and
*off this grass*, because the keeper here has been trying to tell you
gentlemen something for two days, and he's the only man on this floor
who's ever paid rent to the landlord you're annoying."

It should not have worked. It worked because there was not a captain in
the three camps who did not owe Berrin Or something older than the
quarrel — the Cullers marched on his flour; Quist's factors banked on his
credit and his silence about their debts; the Wake's own supply-cache
contracts ran through his warehouse-books; and Sael, who trusted no one
of Men, had once been handed by this man — unasked, wordless — her
company's spent arrows, replaced to the exact count. He fed all three
camps from the same wagons, taking no side but supper's, and
by full dark he had the principals under the one roof that awed all
parties equally: the Lych House, the turf-roofed mortuary hall, where
they argued terms across the cold stone washing-slabs of the honored dead
while Sef held the lamp.

Rhovan stood in the doorway and watched his own trade practiced better
than the Wake had ever taught it to him. No cipher, no handler, no blade
behind the offer — just a man who knew what every soul in the room needed
and had most of it on a wagon outside. The terms took shape the way
honest terms did, each party losing the thing it could spare least
easily: the toll-chain would come off the lych-gate, and Voss's factors
would keep their books instead for the host entire — quartermasters under
Berrin's countersign, paid, watched, and useful; the Cullers would
surrender the rear-gate watch — Hale gave this up in one syllable, and
only Rhovan understood he was watching a doctrine laid down, not a duty —
and take the van's flank under joint command; the Wake's guard-contract
would be bought out at cost, and Sael's bows hired forward as the host's
far-scouts, answerable to the rising's council alone.

"And the dead go in free," Berrin said, last of all, writing it. "All
dead. Everyone's. Forever. Gate-law of this floor, witnessed on the —" he
glanced up mildly, "— what am I signing on, keeper?"

"A drumhead," Sef said. "Culler drum. It's what there was."

"Poetic," said Berrin, and signed.

They came out of the Lych House with a peace, into the dark, and Rhovan,
last through the door, heard the first passing-bell note come out of the
lych-stones themselves, a tolling with no bell in it — and saw the
death-cap rings of the Tithe Barrows begin to glow, mound beyond
mound, away toward the horizon.

The blood had gone into the grass hours ago. This floor foreclosed.

⁂

The newest mounds opened first, exactly in the order of Sef's book.

The turf lifted like lids. Up out of the barrow-earth came the
tithe-wights in their grave-gold — tall, deliberate, dressed in the honor
of Men a thousand years old, moving with the unhurried authority of
creditors — and behind them, from the beetle-riddled slopes, the ghouls
came up hunched and wet and wrong, and last of all Tithegrim moved:
mound to mound at a slow parade walk, its grave-gold rider swaying,
waking the field like a serjeant waking a barracks. And down through the
lych-gate arch, from the floor above, from the moated Kingsbarrow itself,
rolled a voice that was not a voice — the pressure of one, in the oldest
funeral-forms of Men, felt in the chest like a tolling:

*The court finds the field in debt. The court will have its tithe.*

The Barrow King had heard the blood, and raised the reckoning.

What Rhovan witnessed next he set down only in his own private book,
because no report he owed anyone had room for it:
three armies that had spent the day killing each other discovering, under
the tolling dark, that the dead of Men do not distinguish banners. The
wights took their due where they found it — off the living, off the
fallen, gold and gear and grave-goods lifted from screaming men with a
courtesy worse than violence — and where a faction stood alone it died
alone, and everyone saw it, and the lesson took one demonstration: a
Culler file that tried doctrine — as if a wight could be cleared — folded
into the dark.

Then Hale's shield-ring anchored the center and *held* it, and held the
gap in it open for anyone in any colors, bellowing them in; and Sael's
bows found work to suit a contract-keeper's conscience, feathering the
ghouls — evictions, clean ones, each unmade thing coming apart and
draining down into the mounds it had robbed; and the factors, whose books
were the best on the floor, did the thing that turned the night: they
opened the toll-chests.

It was Sef who named the law — *a wight walks for its withheld due; pay
it and lay it* — and it was Berrin who turned the law into quartermaster's
work before the sentence was cold. Every mound an account. Every account
payable. The toll-silver went out across the barrows in grocers' baskets,
carried under Culler shields, laid in the mound-mouths with Sef's rite
while the Wake's arrows kept the ghouls off the paying-parties, and
Quist — who had balked exactly once, at the chest's first opening, and
been answered by Berrin, kindly, *you'll be repaid out of a freed tower,
man; the dead bill first* — knelt in the grass by lantern-light, counting
coins into the hand of a thing in grave-gold, the oldest tool of his
trade turned all the way true.

Wight by wight, mound by mound, the field was paid. And the wights, paid,
*lay down* — that was the sight Rhovan kept: the tall figures in gold
receiving their due, and turning, and lying back down into the opened
earth with the contentment of a settled claim, the turf folding over them
like a book closing.

Ede's van came up the gate road at the worst hour, which was the hour
generals kept; Rhovan saw the Shieldwall banner top the ridge with the
host behind it, and saw it find nothing left to rescue — only three
armies working the mounds in mixed parties under one bread-merchant's
tally-lanterns. Tithegrim made its stand at the vigil-ground near dawn,
and it was not a battle, only a foreclosure run in reverse: a Culler
shield-wall pinned the welded hound; a Tollman gate-chain, thrown and
staked, bound its rider's arm; Wake arrows took the joints; and Sef
Wakewater walked up the vigil-mound under their joined shields carrying
the oldest debt on the floor — the collector's own tithe, withheld a
thousand years, kept in the Lych House against a day no one had believed
in — and *paid* it, and the rider bowed, and the whole welded engine came
apart like a settled estate, the tower's scrap of will draining down and
out of a world it had no claim on — an eviction — while every yew on the
floor let go its crows at once into a grey and ordinary and unbelievable
morning.

The mounds were green and shut. The chain was off the lych-gate. Under
it, in the new peace, three armies buried their dead side by side, in
free ground, tithes paid in full — Culler and Tollman and one bowman of
the Wake, and twenty silk-wrapped soldiers whose serjeant went down the
row at the end touching each bier, paying something that did not go in
any book.

Forty-one banners went up the freed stair that afternoon as one host,
under a peace signed on a drumhead, brokered by a grocer.

Rhovan wrote his report to the Wake that night, in cipher: the toll
dispute, the casualties, the new gate-law, the buy-out of Sael's
contract. It was accurate to the last figure, and it was a fraction,
because he left out the one thing his masters would have paid the most to
know — that on this floor the factions had stopped being factions; that
the rising was now *one thing*, and he was in it, and he no longer knew,
when he reached for the word *we*, whom he meant.

He sanded the page, and looked up the dark stair toward the next floor:
the Kingsbarrow, the drowned king's own moated mound, the decade-Warden
that had just raised and settled a whole field of the dead without
leaving its throne.

"The reeve is paid," Sef had told them at the burying, nodding up the
stair. "The King can't be. He's not a debt, he's a grief, and he keeps
the old forms." He had said the rest very quietly, like a man handing
over a key. "You can't out-fight the forms. But they're *forms*. They can
be kept better than he keeps them. Bring your dead's names up that stair
with you, Captain. All of them. You're going to need every one."
