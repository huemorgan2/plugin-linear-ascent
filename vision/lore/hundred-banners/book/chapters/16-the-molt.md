# Chapter 16 — The Molt

The fortieth floor fell without an arrow loosed, and Nix counted the
arrows anyway. None. Naught. Not one. It was his favorite count of the
whole war, and he said it three ways to make it last.

The host went up through the Broodthrone in column, six abreast, under a
ceiling that had eaten armies. Silk hung from the cavern's throat to its
roots — curtains of it, cables of it, swagged galleries thick as a ship's
rigging — and in the silk hung the court: wrapped knights turning slow on
their threads, pale consorts folded like hands at prayer, thread-pages
lying flat along the cables with their eyes politely down. Sixteen
thousand boots went by beneath them and not one strand so much as
shivered. A month ago that stillness would have meant ambush. Today it
meant clause the fourth: *the court shall stand aside, and the host shall
neither burn, nor cut, nor take.*

Nix walked the column's flank, where he could watch the silk and the
soldiers both, because somebody had to count the ones who stared up too
long and strayed out of the line of march. Men, mostly. Giants never
looked up; giants watched their feet, which Nix respected. The elves
looked up and smiled, which he did not.

Matriarch Vyx received the passing of an army the way a lady receives
weather. She sat vast and still upon the throne-web with her forelegs
folded, the tribute of a hundred years hung about her like a dowry, and
she spoke once, as Ede's vanguard passed below the throne, in a voice
like silk drawn over the back of a blade.

"Clause the seventh is observed," she said. "The warmth of your fires
rises and is received. The accounting is fair. Climb, general. And
remember that I remember."

There was one thing in all that cavern the contract let them break, and
it was the thing the tower had nailed into her. High on the crown-cable,
where the anchor-lines gathered, sat a welded ring of black iron the
size of a millstone — the Yoke's seat on the fortieth floor, the voice
that came down the column from the throne and spoke through her web
whether she would or no. Clause the ninth, the last, written at Vyx's
insistence in her own silk, said: *the crown-iron is not of my court,
and the host may do to it what it does to such things.*

Vega's crews went up the anchor-cables with cold chisels, for the
contract barred fire, and they worked the welds the slow way, in shifts,
by hand. Nix counted the hammer-taps until he lost the count on purpose.
When the crown-iron came away at last it fell the height of the whole
cavern and struck the chasm-bridge below with a sound like a bell nobody
had wanted, and then —

Then the Yoke stopped.

It was not a sound ending. Nobody had ever heard it; that was the trick
of it. It sat under hearing, the way a man with a stone in his boot
stops feeling the stone by noon. What ended was a pressure. The whole
cavern breathed. The silk shivered once, all of it, root to throat, the
way a spider's world shivers when she sheds her old skin and stands new
— and ten thousand people who had not known they were clenched came
unclenched at once, and some of them laughed without knowing why, and
some of them wept and knew exactly.

Nix put two fingers to the old scar under his jaw, where his own collar
had sat for nine years, and held them there a while.

They cut down the captives of Threadneedle that afternoon — the wrapped
ones who could still be unwound, cocoon by cocoon, each one carried out
into the lamplight and given soup and a name to answer to. The court
watched from the high silk and stood aside, as contracted. By evening
the stair-lift in the cavern's crown stood open, and the first cold air
any of them had tasted in forty floors came down it like a long drink of
well-water.

Winter lived upstairs. The host camped at the mouth of it.

The cold came down the open stair from the forty-first floor and pooled
in the low galleries, and the scouts who went up came back rimed to the
eyebrows, talking of a country of gravel and balanced boulders under a
hard white sky, with five floors of winter stacked above it and a frozen
sea at the top of them. The tower has no weather but what each floor
kept, and the floors upstairs had all been stolen in winter — winters
with no spring behind them, kept cold since the day they were taken. An
army does not walk onto pack-ice in summer boots. So the rising wintered
where it stood, at the chasm-mouths: the wide cavern-mouths below the
stair, where the Broodthrone's silk gave way to honest stone and the
Deep's old chasm fell away black under the bridges.

The camps grew into a town in a market-week. Cook-fires by the hundred
along the galleries. Tents of bought silk. The heat of eight thousand
suppers rose nightly into the web above, where the court banked upon it
and grew sleek — the tribute paying itself, which Vyx had foreseen when
she wrote the clause.

Berrin Or came up the freed lifts with the winter stores a week ahead of
the frost, which was Berrin all over. He knew without being told that
the elves would not touch seal-fat; he knew which giant companies wanted
their loaves baked black; he knew Nix liked his crusts burnt, and never
once said how he knew. "Nobody sleeps cold on my books," he said, and
thumped the ledger like a drum, and nobody did. Nix kept a private count
of the people in this host worth more than their own weight in bread,
and Berrin sat high on it.

In the winter camps the council ordered its census, and like most things
the council did, it was agreed unanimously for three reasons that had
nothing to do with one another. Voss wanted heads, because rations are
numbers and numbers were the only poetry he read. Calyne wanted names,
for a spy's reasons, which she did not give. Ede wanted musters — who
could march, who could dig, who could draw a bow. So the word went out
through the galleries: every soul in the rising to be counted and set
down.

They made Nix a counter. He counted true, and the kobolds trusted him,
and the quartermasters trusted him, and that was a short list to be on
twice.

⁂

They smoothed a wall of slate at the widest chasm-mouth, tall as three
giants and long as a toll-bridge, and the order was this: every soul to
set their name on it — their own name, in their own hand, in their own
script — and those who could not write to make their mark and stand by
while a neighbor wrote the name beside it, so that the name and the hand
that owned it were at least introduced.

It took nineteen days. Nix was there for all of them.

He counted scripts: forty. The rune-rows of the giants, straight as
girders. The elves' vine-writing, which could not stop being beautiful
even when it was only spelling *Fennick*. The delvers wrote in knots and
had to be issued pegs, and hung them in rows like a harvest. The
Waste-folk scratched theirs into smoked glass and set the panes in the
slate. There were chalk tallies from drovers, and a priest's
copperplate, and one name put down in dye on brood-silk by a woman of
Threadneedle who said she had earned the right to like the stuff again.
Some scripts held a thousand names. Some held three.

One held one.

The clerks brought the count to council on the twentieth day, and read
it by lamplight while the snow-wind hunted down the stair. Souls: eight
thousand, four hundred and six. Banners: forty — Shieldwall the oldest,
then the keepers' lantern-banner, Voss's chain-and-key, and thirty-seven
more named for floors and trades and dead friends. Peoples: nine. And
there the counting had nearly come to blows, cheerful ones. Men. Giants.
Elves. The delvers of the Deep, who were men and giants and elves by
blood and would have none of it — "our nation is the rope and the lamp,"
their foreman said, and the clerks gave way. The borderlanders of the
Waste, ash-nomads and glass-gleaners, who claimed the same and got it.
The kobolds: ninety-one. The Red Orcs: eleven, deserters since the
Warcamp fell, who drilled alone and saluted nobody below Ede herself.
Seven trolls of the Deep who had thrown down the collar together and
would not be parted, counted as a people because no clerk wished to
argue with seven trolls. And the ninth was not on the first draft: the
shardminds asked to be counted, and they asked all together, in one
breath, out of every splinter in the host, which ended the argument
before it began. A people whose country was glass. The clerks gave them
their line. Souls of crystal: two hundred and twelve.

Then the last line, which the clerk read without looking up, because
clerks are kind in the only way their trade allows:

Goblins: one.

Nix went back to the wall that night with a lamp. His name sat small,
between a drover's tally and a knot-row: the hooked running-hand of the
warrens, made for tally-sticks and insults, spelling the one word he had
kept nine years under his tongue. His script, alone of all forty, had no
neighbors.

He thought about the warrens, the first realm taken, the tower's own
foundation stones; about his people collared on every floor of the low
country, handed spears and set to guard the doors of their own prison.
Of all of them, one had climbed. He thought about Gnarl on his
crate-throne in a meadow of stolen banners, who was offered the
Break-collar and chose the crown, because the crown was at least his.
Nix had loved him, and left him, and neither had been wrong.

He stood before the wall a long time, counting the one name in his own
script, and decided the count was wrong in the only way that mattered.

Not *one goblin*. The *first* goblin. A seed is also a count of one.

He never called it a school. He called it his fire, and the fire had
burnt crusts, and nobody who sat at it wore a sword, and that was the
whole charter.

The freed kobolds who had stayed with the rising — out of the flooded
mine and the gear-halls and the smelter floors, ninety-one by the wall's
own count — came to it in the evenings. They were free, and fed, and
they answered to the names the collar had issued them, names handed out
like tools: Ninth Shovel. Grease. Wedge. Tally. Sixth-of-the-Line. A
kobold would come when you called, and what you called him was the work
he had been chained to, and he had stopped hearing the insult years ago,
which was the insult.

So every night, before the soup, Nix told the same story the same way,
because a ritual is only a story with its boots on.

"They called me Cup for nine years. Cup, fetch. Cup, kneel. Cup, the
king's thirsty. And all nine years I kept my name under my tongue like a
seed, and I'll tell you what I told the dark: a name keeps. Yours kept
too. It's down there under nine years of shovel and grease, gone quiet.
Quiet's not gone. Spit it out."

Then he waited. He was good at waiting. He had been a cup-bearer.

The names came slow, and they came crooked. A young digger from the
gear-halls hissed hers all in one breath — *Ssarik* — and then looked
about her like she'd dropped a plate. An old bilge-hand said his —
*Veshk* — to the fire and not to Nix, which Nix allowed, because the
fire wouldn't repeat it wrong. Each name that surfaced, he made the
whole fire say back three times: once to learn it, once to mean it, once
for the count.

The hardest was the one they all called Ninth Shovel, who had worn his
tool-name so long the skin had grown over it. Nine nights he sat at the
fire with his hands around his bowl, digging. On the ninth he looked up.

"It's gone, cup-bearer," he said. "I dug where you said. The collar ate
it."

The fire went quiet. Nix counted his words for once, and spent them
slowly.

"Then make one," he said. "A made name is still a name. Half the best
things in this camp are made things. The bread's made. The banners are
made. This army is the most made thing I ever saw, and it's the best
thing I ever saw. Make one, and we'll wear it in with you."

The old kobold looked a long time down the gallery, to where the great
slate wall stood pale under the lamps with its eight thousand names.

"Chalk," he said at last.

They said it back three times. Then Nix walked him to the wall by
lamplight, the whole fire trailing after, and Chalk wrote it himself,
the first thing he had ever written, the letters wobbling like a new
foal — and the wall gained a name that had never been anybody's tool.
The kobolds' line still read ninety-one souls. By Nix's private count it
now read one people, waking up.

Some nights there were Red Orcs at the edge of the firelight, arms
folded, too proud to sit, listening. Nix counted them anyway. Counts,
like seeds, are patient.

The last market-week of winter, the camps began to fold. The scouts came
down from the Moraine Gates with Bruna Coldquay's ice-road markers
copied fair onto forty map-boards. Vega's forges rang all night,
cold-hammering sledge-runners of rime-iron, and the sound carried up
into the silk where the court's old shed skins hung translucent among
the tribute — for spiders molt, and their whole world shakes when they
do, and stands still when it's done, and what steps out is the same
creature, larger, in armor it grew itself.

On the last night Nix climbed to the high gallery and looked down at the
winter city: fires by the hundred, the census-wall pale in the dark, the
lamps going out in rows as orderly as anything Berrin kept. A company,
he thought, is a thing you hire. An escort walks someone else's road
home. What slept below him owned a wall of eight thousand names in forty
scripts; it had nine peoples and its own laws, a contract kept with a
spider, a school for stolen names, and a census — and a census is a
country's way of looking in a mirror. The old skin hung behind them in
the silk.

In the morning they went up the stair into winter, sixteen thousand
boots on frozen gravel, and the sound they made on the moraine was not
the sound of a company on the march. It was the sound of a nation
moving. Six floors above, at the top of the cold, a frozen sea lay
waiting to measure the size of the word.
