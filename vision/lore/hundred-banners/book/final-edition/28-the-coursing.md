# Chapter 33 — The Coursing

The horns took them at moonset, on the third night in the eye.

Ede had gone forward with three because a general who sends scouts to a
Warden's door has stopped being worth following. That was what she told the
council. The truer reason was smaller and harder: the road to the pale court
had to be walked before the army walked it, and she could not sit in the
standing rain another day listening to Dun's muster-song sung without Dun.

So they were four on the ridge above the eye-wall when the calm ended —
herself, Rhovan, Vega, and Nix — a bow, a hammer, a splinter of crystal, and
a goblin with a kettle he refused to leave with the baggage. Four, and the
army a day behind, still burying, still counting its blown regiments in.

The first horn sounded through the rain-wall like something remembered
rather than heard. The second answered it from the west, where no road was.
The third came from behind them, between them and the camp, and by then
Rhovan had already stopped walking.

"Three horns," he said. "That is a drawn covert."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the quarry has been found." He did not look at her when he said
it. He was looking at the ridgeline, where pale shapes were coming through
the standing rain without disturbing it — hounds, moon-white, running mute.
A hunt that meant to kill would have given tongue. These ran silent, spread
wide, and every gap they left led one way.

"They're herding us," Ede said.

"They are coursing us," Rhovan said. "It is not the same. Herded beasts
arrive somewhere. Coursed ones are enjoyed first."

Vega set her hammer head-down on the stone and leaned on it, and for one
breath Ede watched her decide whether to argue for standing. Two heartbeats.
Three. The hounds on the ridge were forty at a glance and more behind, and
somewhere under the horns was the thing that had welded them.

"We run," Vega said slowly, finishing the thought the way giants do, "and
we choose our running, or they choose it for us. I have shod enough hounds'
masters to know which is worse."

They ran. The gaps in the pale line opened before them, courteous as a
door — down off the ridge, away from the army, away from the eye, into the
dark of the Huntsman's country. Behind them, one glade back, the horns.

The Hunt kept its own ways between its floors — runs collared around
the great stays and anchor-roots, gripping by shape and friction the
way everything built on the tower's bones gripped, winked at for eighty
years because the Hunt was the tower's own. Ede learned that the first
night, when the ground they were driven down was suddenly not the Eye's
drowned terraces but a beaters' stair sunk in the hollow of a black iron
anchor-root — a hidden run, hound-worn, rank with old scent, that went down
when every law of the climb said the way was up. Sliver burned cold
against her palm the whole descent.

*"They are not taking you toward the stair-lifts,"* it said. *"They are
taking you where the running is good. I have carried climbers through this
country twice. Neither time did the country decide the road. It has decided
tonight."*

"Then we're quarry."

*"You have been quarry since the horns found the eye. What you are now is
sport."*

The beaters' stair let out into a wood, and the wood was wrong before she
had taken ten steps into it. Green, quiet, lamp-dark — and strung. Wire
glinted between the trunks at shin height, at throat height, fine as spider-
work. Spring-poles stood cocked in the underbrush like drawn bows nobody had
loosed in years. The leaf-litter lay too smooth in places, the way a pit lies
smooth. Rhovan put out his arm and stopped them all at the treeline.

"The Snare Woods," he said. "Floor seventy-four. The elves here hunted
with wire and patience, and the fever took the wood and multiplied every
snare a thousandfold. Nothing in this wood is a path. Some of it is
pretending."

"How do we cross it?" Ede asked.

"Slowly," Rhovan said, which was a fraction, and then the horn sounded one
glade behind them, which was the rest of the answer. Slowly was not on
offer.

They went in with Nix leading, because Nix was lightest and had walked
point across the Trapdoor Fields and had, he announced, a gift. "Three
gifts," he said, testing the ground with a kettle-hook lashed to a pole.
"Small feet, big eyes, and no pride at all about crawling. Wire here. And
here. And — oh, that's clever, that one's under the other one. Wicked wood.
Lovely wicked wood."

The wood tried for them all night. A noose-creeper took Nix's pole out of
his hands and hoisted it into the canopy quick as a fish taking bait, and
they stood in the dark listening to the springpole thrum and did not speak
for a long while after. Pale flowers glowed in the black ahead, marking
clearings that looked like mercy, and Rhovan said, "Bait-flower. The wood
grows its own lies. Where it looks safest, kneel," and they crawled through
a clearing whose smooth ground was a lidded pit wide enough to take a cart.

Vega suffered worst. The wood was strung for men and elves, and she was
neither, and the third wire she could not fold herself under she took across
the forearm — a spring-blade, wire-flung, that laid her open elbow to wrist
before her forge-skin stopped it. She made no sound. She bound it walking.
But after that the funnel hounds had blood on the air, and the wood's own
beasts began to work them in earnest: dog-shapes ghosting the flanks, never
closing, leaning them always leftward, always leftward, onto ground they had
not tested.

"They funnel us," Rhovan said. "It is what they were bred for, before they
were welded to it. They will bend us onto the wire like water into a race."

"Then we bend back," Ede said, and they spent the strength to fight the
bending — doubling on their own line, crossing a stream to fool nothing that
hunted by more than scent, cutting north when every gap invited them west.
It bought them the night. It cost them the night's strength. And at grey
dawn, when they lay in a bramble-hollow chewing cold meal, the horn
sounded — one glade behind, exactly one glade, no nearer for all the
hounds could plainly have closed, no farther for all Ede's doubling.

Nix counted on his fingers, lips moving. "Eleven times now. Eleven horns,
one glade. I had a master who did that with a whip once. Never hit me. Just
let me hear where the whip was." He put his fingers away. "I didn't love
him either."

They crossed the Gibbet Glade at noon because there was no way around it
that the hounds would allow.

Ede had seen fields after battle. She had seen the cages under the Red
Warcamp opened, and what five years in them had made of her thirty. She
had thought herself past being taught anything new about what the tower did
to the caught. The glade taught her anyway: a clearing of green light where
the sprung snares had kept their catch, game and folk together, hanging in
the trees season past season — and among the hanging dead, things that
moved, wire-tangled figures that had died in their own snares and now
walked the wood setting snares for the living.

"Don't cut them down," Rhovan said, low, as her hand went to her knife.
"Not with the horns behind us. The snare-folk cut them down when this floor
is freed, and give their kin graves. If we start now we finish never."

"You know this wood."

"I know its cousins." A fraction. He gave her a little more, walking:
"The Grey Rides are mine. Floor thirty-one. All the elf-woods in this span
are the Hunt's country — the Rides, the Kennel Courts, the Quarry Runs, and
this. Every one takes its law from the pale court above us. I was raised
under that law." He tested a branch, ducked the wire it hid. "I left before
it could collect me."

"And the law is chasing us."

"The law is exercising us," Rhovan said. "The chase comes when it tires
of the game. Or when we do."

Beyond the glade the wood rose toward a scarp, and on the scarp stood the
tallest elm Ede had ever seen, every trip-line in the wood converging on its
crown like the strings of an instrument. Under it, briefly, they found
people: snare-folk from the gate-town, three of them, light-footed and grey-
eyed, freezing at the sight of armed climbers and unfreezing at the sight of
Nix. What they gave was little and everything — a tested path, a skin of
water, and a warning delivered by their eldest with an elf's economy.

"The Hunt has doors," she said. "It will put you through one tonight,
onto the Runs. Nothing that goes onto the Runs comes off them. The runs
remember the chase." She looked at Vega's bound arm, at Ede's face, and
something in her face shifted — pity, or its harder elvish cousin. "Test
every step. And when you cannot run any more — " she stopped, the way
Rhovan stopped, on the edge of a whole truth.

"Say it," Ede said.

"It is kennel-talk. A child's tale."

"We are being coursed by a child's tale. Say it."

But the horn sounded, one glade back, and the snare-folk were gone the way
elves go, and the door the Hunt had chosen for them stood open in the scarp:
a beaters' way, hound-worn, breathing the smell of run-grass and old blood.

The Quarry Runs were made for exactly this, and that was the horror of
them.

The wood opened into coursing-lanes, broad grass rides walled with run-oak
and covert-thorn, streaming away downhill so sweetly that tired legs turned
onto them of their own accord. The grass itself lied: it laid a clear green
path ahead of them that Rhovan swore was false, a groomed line pointing the
one way in this wood they must not go — down, always down, the way every
country's ground finally went, down toward its own dark middle, toward a
clearing ringed pale with foxglove where every course on this floor had
ended for a thousand years. On the high stands along the lanes sat
crows in their hundreds, and the crows called the course. Ede learned
to hate the crows worse than the hounds. The hounds only followed. The
crows told the whole wood, always, exactly, where the quarry ran.

And the hounds here did not funnel. They paced. Fresh hounds, run-bred,
falling in beside them a lane's width off, matching Nix's trot and Vega's
stride and slowing when they slowed — keeping them, the way a drover keeps
cattle, spending nothing, while the quarry spent everything.

They ran the Runs for a night and a day. Ede kept the count because
keeping counts was the last discipline that survived in her when the rest
went — four of them still; two skins of water, then one, then none until
Nix smelled out a covert-spring; Vega's arm going hot under the binding;
Rhovan's arrows, eleven, then nine, spent on a covert-boar that burst from
the thorn onto Nix and died apologizing to nobody. The foxglove was the
worst of it. Its pollen rode the lane-wind, and it slowed the heart —
gently, courteously, the way everything in this country killed. She would
surface from a grey drift and find she had been walking a lane she never
chose, downhill, on the clear green path, toward the pale ring. Twice Vega's
hand on her shoulder turned her. Once she turned Vega.

They went to ground at last in a covert, a green warren of bramble the
quarry-folk of this floor had kept safe for things that could not run any
more. That was what it was for. That was what they were.

"Count it for me," Ede said, "like a position." Her voice was a stranger's,
scraped thin.

Rhovan did, dry as a sapper reporting a wall. "The hounds hold every lane.
The crows hold the sky, so darkness buys nothing. The wood funnels toward
the killing-ground, and the ground between the lanes is thorn we no longer
have the blood to spare. Vega's arm needs fire and a needle. We have neither.
The army does not know where we are, and if it did, it is a day behind
and would arrive as a second quarry." He put his last arrows in the earth
in front of him, stood them upright in a row, a fraction of a defense.
"The Hunt has run us to the arithmetic. That was always its purpose. It
does not tire. We do."

"Then we stand here," Vega said. Slowly; she finished it. "In the thorn,
where the horse cannot come and the hounds come two abreast, and we make
the freeing of this floor cost the tower a price it remembers. I have been
a debt on this climb since floor sixteen. I would rather end as a payment."

"Two abreast, that's good, that's — " Nix's fast count died in his mouth.
He was looking at his own hands, and his hands would not stop. "Four lanes
into this covert. I counted. Four lanes, forty hounds a lane, and a horn,
and a whip, and a man on a horse who doesn't talk." He laughed, high and
short. "I carried a cup for a king once. Nobody chased me at all. Terrible
job. Lovely hours."

Ede lay against the bramble-root with her heart going slow and wrong under
the foxglove, and felt the old arithmetic close over her like water over a
weir. This was the Warcamp again, thirty names on a stone. This was where
she was always going to arrive: the ground where the people who followed
her stopped living because they had followed her.

*"Your heart is running,"* Sliver said against her palm, very quietly, so
the others got none of it. *"Not from the foxglove. Ask him the thing the
snare-wife would not say."*

She turned her head. "Rhovan. On the scarp. The kennel-talk. Say it now."

For three breaths he was silent, and she watched him fight his own nature
— the elf who told the truth only in fractions, weighing which fraction this
was. Then, flat: "There is one quarry the Hunt does not take."

"Which?"

"The quarry that turns," Rhovan said, "and walks toward the horns. Not
runs — walks. It is the first law of the pale court, older than the
Huntsman, older than the Theft. A hunt is a hunt only while the quarry
flees. What walks toward the horns is not quarry, and the Hunt may not
take what is not quarry. Every child of the Rides has it before they
have their letters."

"And it holds? Even now? Even welded?"

"The tower did not unmake his law. It nailed him to it." He looked away.
"But I have never once seen it tried, Ede. In a thousand years of the pale
court, the kennel-talk counts the quarry who walked on the fingers of one
hand. You know why no one tries it."

"Because no quarry believes it," Ede said.

"Because no quarry believes it."

The horn sounded. One glade behind. It had been one glade behind for three
nights, and it would be one glade behind until their hearts stopped, because
that was the sport: not the catching, the keeping. And Ede Stonhal, who had
been kept by one thing or another her whole life — by drink, by grief, by
thirty names cut in a stone — got her feet under her and stood up out of the
covert into the open lane. Out of an ash-lane fifty floors below, thin and
clear as the fair's children had skipped it, the old counting-rhyme
surfaced in her: *walk to the horn and the horn falls silent.*

"Ede." Vega's voice. "Ede, the arm I would give to stop you is the bad
one. Do not make me offer the good one."

"I'm not spending myself," Ede said, and was surprised to find it true.
She had spent herself down every year of her life and knew the feel of it,
and this was not that feel. "If the law holds, I walk into his court and
his court must receive me, and I bargain for the army from inside instead
of dying out here as an afternoon's sport. If the law doesn't hold — " she
tightened the strap of the shield that wasn't there, an old habit from a
company five years dead " — then I've bought you the length of a hunt's
gloat, and you go through the thorn and you tell Berrin to feed them
whatever he must feed them to keep them climbing."

She said the name and it cost her nothing. Later she would remember that:
the last hour the name cost her nothing.

It was the shut of the day. Low and level through the run-oaks the
gold came — the one minute of true sun even this floor was allowed,
raking in off the world's open edge, a seam of fire far away between
the trunks. On every floor she had freed, folk gathered for that
minute; the darkest floors gathered hardest. She let it touch her
face while it lasted.

She turned toward the horns and walked.

The lane ran uphill, against the wood's whole design, and the walking
was the hardest work of her life. Every stride her legs begged to run —
anywhere, even downhill toward the foxglove, anything but this — and
the horns came on, and the hounds came with them. They broke from the
covert-walls to either side, moon-white, silent, at a full course, and
her body screamed and her step did not change, and at ten strides the
pale line — parted. Flowed around her like water around a post, hackles
down, heads low, and fell in beside her at a walk. Behind her she heard
the others come out of the thorn and do what she did; heard Nix's
breath sobbing with the effort of not running;
heard Vega humming a forge-song slow enough to walk to.

The horns sounded a call she had not heard in three nights — not the drive,
not the drawn covert. Something older. Ahead, the lanes gave onto a glade
lit horn-gold with lantern-light, and it was full of the Hunt: whips and
horn-callers, pale riders, the silent pack seated in rings like a court in
session. And at its center, mounted, moon-pale, antler-crowned, the one
figure that did not move at all.

He did not speak. She had been told he never spoke, and it was worse than
any voice. It was his court that spoke around him, a pale whip-man's words
falling into the hush like coins onto cloth:

"The horn cannot take what walks to it. The court receives you."

The hounds sat. The Huntsman turned his horse toward the pale road that
climbed north out of the glade — toward the bone-white hall she could feel
up there past the trees, hung with a thousand years of trophies, where
whatever a hunt-lord's word was worth would now be tested against the lives
of everyone she had ever led.

Ede spat the taste of foxglove into the run-grass, and followed him up.
