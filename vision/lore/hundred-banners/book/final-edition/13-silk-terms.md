# Chapter 15 — Silk Terms

*The rising makes good pace,* Rhovan wrote. *Nine floors in a season. The
host is fed and its factions hold.*

He did not write: the host walked the Grey Rides behind a fourscore-year
hunt and heard it called home. He did not write what that had sounded
like. The Wake paid him for facts, and the Wake's ciphers had no
characters for the things that mattered.

The Rides had been the tier's first gift and its first lesson. Grey
bridleways running arrow-true through a dark elf-wood — his own people's
wood, a fragment of it, stranded thirty floors from its roots — with the
lantern-crows dead on their grey posts and a spectral hunt coursing the
ways forever after quarry freed long ago. The army had hugged the verges
for two days while Aline Bridlerest, the last ride-master, taught Ede's
captains the shape of the thing: you did not fight a hunt, you *recalled*
it. When Tallyhorn broke on the third morning — a courser welded to its
dead rider, brought down on the open road — Aline had sounded the true
recall from the Ride-Head crossroads, and the whole grey hunt had come
home. Horse and rider and hound, laid down at the wayshrine, a lifetime
late for their own supper. By that dusk the Rides' grey lid was fraying
off the wood, and the wayshrine lamps burned that night in the first
honest dark the Rides had kept in fourscore years. The ride-keepers
climbed with the rising now, its outriders and its fastest word.

After that the floors ran together the way marching floors do. Ran
together, and did not shrink. The Deep Drifts were a week of going
down to go up, a country measured in fathoms, winch-ropes creaking
under loads that would never come up, cold air sighing out of shafts
that swallowed lamplight whole; Orla Ropewalk's crews lowered the army
cage by cage and raised it on the far headframe, reading the ropes by
their pull the way other folk read sky. The Broken Doors sobered even the
giants: a tier of bulkhead-gates the deep-forge had built to hold an
age, every one blown off its hinges in a single season, the corridors
between them crow-loud and scorched where the tower's charges had cut
through; Bruna Lastlantern kept the one door left, and walked the army
through the breaches slowly, so that it would understand what it was
climbing against. The Peat Cuts were two days of black trenches
slotted dead-straight to the horizon, house-deep, ankle-flooded,
whining with midge-fog, Maddox Turfside's cutters walking the banks
above the column and calling down the firm ground — and in the deepest
cut the peat gave up its dead whole and perfect, and the rising, which
knew funerals by then, laid them back down. The rest the histories may
keep: the Troll Delvings' three whispered days, the Rig Graves'
rusting meres, the Vent Country's whistling crust, the Processional
Way's passing-bells, and last the Echo Glades, where the wood gave
three thousand marching feet back out of the trees like a second army
keeping step.

Berrin's supply-trains found them on every floor. That was the season's
quiet miracle, and Rhovan, whose trade was noticing, noticed it:
Roothollow's provisioner was never more than two days behind the van
with flour and boot-leather and the mail from below. "You can plan a
war around that man," Ede said once,
watching the wagons come up the Peat Cuts in the rain, and Rhovan had
written that down for the Wake too, in the flat cipher of facts: *supply
reliable.*

*The tier ends at floor forty,* he wrote instead, that last night on the
Echo Glades. *The Broodthrone. The keepers will not speak of it above a
whisper. We treat with it inside a week.*

He did not write: *and I have heard what it keeps, and I am afraid in a
way I have not been since the Theft.*

Threadneedle was not a gate-town so much as a wound's edge. Its people —
the free ones, the few — lived in the rim-tunnels where the great cavern
narrowed, and their keeper met the war-council at a barricade of anchor
stone with silk-shears at her belt and both forearms scarred to the
elbow. Wend Threadneedle, thread-cutter. She had spent nine years cutting
cocoons open one strand at a time.

"You'll want to see it before you talk about it," was all she said.

They saw it. The Broodthrone was one cavern the size of a stolen city,
and every span of it was spun. Silk-galleries rose tier over tier into
the dark like the inside of some pale cathedral, and hung through them,
soft-lit from within by the fever's own glow, were the cocoons. Hundreds
of them. They hung like lanterns at a festival, and each one held a
living captive — courtiers, servants, tribute, the taken of a dozen
floors. Some of them, Wend said flatly, had been hanging for years and
would greet you by name if you cut them down. Vyx did not kill what she
caught. That was the whole horror of her: she *kept*.

The war-council met that night in the rim-tunnels and planned an assault
none of them believed in. Fire was madness — the silk would burn with
everyone in it. Cutting was slow — nine years had freed Wend a few dozen.
And the Matriarch's court would fight the way it always fought — every
blow landing on the wrapped and the puppeted, the very people the rising
had come to free. Vega sat with her fists on her knees
and said, slowly, that she had broken into every kind of lock the tower
had yet shown her, and that this was the first one made entirely of
hostages, and she did not finish her sentence, which from Vega was the
most frightening thing said all night.

Then the thread-page came.

It was a child. That was the first thing that stopped every hand on
every hilt — a child of Men, grey-faced, silk-collared, walking out of
the dark web with a control-thread running from its neck back up into
the vault. It stopped at the edge of
the council's firelight, and its mouth opened, and the voice that came
out was not a child's. It was silk drawn over glass, cool and faintly
amused, and far away.

"A courtesy, before the parties waste one another," it said. "The
Matriarch Vyx, freeholder of this floor by right of survival, proposes
that the company now trespassing at her rim send its principals to
treat. She observes that you have fought your way up thirty-nine floors,
and she wishes you to
know that on her floor, and on her floor alone" — the child's hand rose,
palm up, in a gesture no child ever made — "*everything is for sale.*"

The thread went slack. The child blinked, and began to cry, and Wend
Threadneedle crossed the firelight and held it, glaring up into the dark
as if her eyes could cut silk.

They went in at noon by the great gallery, under a hundred hanging
lanterns that were people: Ede, Vega, Rhovan, Wend Threadneedle to read
the threads,
and Mereth Wakesend, because after the Kingsbarrow the rising did not
treat with crowned things without her. Nix came too. Nobody had decided
that; he was simply there, walking point with the Shieldwall standard,
counting cocoons under his breath and hating every number.

Matriarch Vyx received them on the Throne-Web, and Rhovan, who had spent
a professional lifetime keeping his face still, needed all of it. She was
vast — not swollen, the way the fever swells its beasts, but *grown*, the
patient bulk of something that had been eating well and thinking long
since before the Theft. Aether burned in her spinnerets like rings on a
magistrate's fingers. Below her, arranged down the web's tiers like a
court at levée, her thralls stood in their silk livery, each moving with
the small borrowed grace of the puppeted; and
among them, armored and motionless, hung the cocooned corpse of a mailed
knight — her champion, Wend had whispered, dead in the silk a long age
and still fencing with drilled skill when the lines pulled.

"The party of the first part," Vyx said, in that silk-over-glass voice,
each word set down precisely, like a clause. "Ede Stonhal, called the
banner-general; her captains; her witnesses. Be welcome under my vault.
You will forgive me if I do not descend. At my displacement, courtesy is
a hazard."

"You asked us here to sell us passage," Ede said. "Name your price."

"Directness. How refreshing, after forty floors of heralds." The
Matriarch shifted, and the whole vault's silk sang one low note, and
every thrall in the court swayed with it. "Then I shall be direct in my
turn. I will not fight you. Understand this is not mercy; it is
arithmetic. I have watched your kind since before the black iron came,
and I know what an army that mourns its dead by name does to whatever
stands in its road. You would win. It would cost you a tenth of your
banners and me the whole of my house, and I have spent nine hundred
years furnishing it. Ruin profits no signatory. Therefore: terms."

"We're listening," Ede said.

"Clause the first. Passage. The host of the rising crosses my floor
unhindered, by the gallery road, van to rear-guard, wagons and stragglers
inclusive; and the stair-lift behind my throne, which I have kept wrapped
these many years against exactly this negotiation, is unwrapped for its
use. In perpetuity. Your reinforcements will follow you up this tier for
years, little general. They pass too. That is what I am selling: floor
forty, open, without a single funeral.

"Clause the second. Tribute, annual, in three parts." She counted them
on the air with one foreclaw, delicately. "Part the first: warmth. Once
in each year, for one night, the host's cook-fires are lit in my hall.
You cannot
know what you own, you surface things, who had a sun. My people have
never once been warm. I will have the warmth of a nation's suppers in my
vault one night a year, and my brood will sleep in it.

"Part the second: a hostage of rank, to dwell in my court as surety
while the host and its trains are upon my floor, and to be surrendered
whole, unwrapped, and unpuppeted — the condition of the goods to be
warranted by me — upon the rear-guard's clearance. Rank, mark you. I do
not warehouse spear-carriers. The pledge is only as dear as the head.

"Part the third." And here the silk voice slowed, and something old
moved in it, and every elf-instinct Rhovan owned sat up. "One true
story, never before told to any ear, to be paid to me annually, at this
court, for so long as the contract runs. I hold four hundred years of
other people's secrets in this web, and I am *bored*, and boredom in a
creature of my displacement is a public danger. Truth, novelty, and
delivery in person. Those are the coins of part the third, and I will
know a false one, so do not try."

Silence under the vault. A hundred cocoon-lanterns glowing softly.

"And your court," Ede said. "The people in your silk. They're not in
these clauses."

"They are my property and my furniture, and they are not on the table."
Flat. Final. A door closing. "You may not like a clause and sign, general.
It is done every day."

Rhovan watched Ede stand under the hanging lanterns with a dozen floors'
captives glowing above her head, and watched her measure it: the tenth
of her banners, the winter coming, the years of reinforcements who would
climb this tier behind them, against every hanging light she would be
walking away from. He had blackmailed this woman up a tower, once, in
another life, and he knew the exact shape of her when she was about to
pay too much of herself. Vega's fists were closed. Wend Threadneedle was
not breathing.

"We will come back for them," Ede said at last — to Wend, to the vault,
to the lanterns; a promise laid down in front of the Matriarch like a
counter-clause. "Every one. This contract buys passage. It does not buy
our forgetting."

"Forgetting was not offered," said Vyx, silkily. "Have we terms?"

"One question first," Ede said. "The hostage. Held how long?"

"Until your rear-guard clears my tier-gate, or until the first freeze
stops the lifts — whichever falls first. A hostage is surety against
traffic, and I do not warehouse surety against a road that cannot move;
the clause sleeps with your trains. A season, as your trains straggle.
The story-clause alone is perpetual." The Matriarch's foreclaw
turned, palm up, a merchant's shrug the size of a wagon. "Well? Who is
of rank, and expendable enough to hang in my hall, and interesting
enough that I do not regret the keeping? It is a narrow list, general. I
have read your court from here and I count perhaps three names."

"Two," said Rhovan, and stepped forward before the fear could finish
its sentence. "The others are load-bearing."

Every head turned.

"Rhovan—"

"Of the Grey Rides," he said to the Matriarch, and heard his own voice
come out level. "Archer, agent of the Dusk Wake, resident with the
rising since
Roothollow." A fraction. As always, a fraction. "Rank you may verify:
the Wake does not send junior men to blackmail generals."

"He is a captain of this council," Ede said, slowly, formally, "and the
rising counts him among its own. His head is dear to us." She looked at
him while she said it, and Rhovan understood that he was being given the
rank that made him worth keeping alive, and that it was true, and that
she had just told him so in front of a monster and an army, and that
this was the most anyone had said of him in forty years.

"An elf of the Wake." Vyx's voice curled with pleasure, silk winding on
a spindle. "Oh, *accepted.* The Wake has been declining my
correspondence for fourscore years. Clause the second, part the second:
satisfied. Which leaves" — the foreclaw came around, unhurried, and
pointed at him the way a clerk points at a ledger-line — "part the
third. The first year's story falls due on signature. It need not be
yours, hostage. It need only be true, and new. Have you one about you?"

He had been an agent long enough to know that some purses only open all
the way once. He thought of Calyne — his handler, his examiner, the
Wake's grey arithmetic in a woman's shape — and of what she would do
when this reached her, and it would reach her; the Wake marked its
secrets the way shepherds mark lambs. Nor would it wait on the lifts.
The Wake's word walked the stays — one runner, one satchel, a hair of
the tower's own thread underfoot and four miles of air under that —
which was how the Wake always knew first, and why it never arrived as
an army. And he thought: the rising needs
this floor, and this queen's goodwill after it, for years.

Then he paid.

"In the first winter after the Theft," Rhovan said, "when the great wood
was in nineteen pieces on nineteen floors and the lamps were going out
in all of them, three lamplighter houses met in the only grove still
lit, and founded a watch, and called it the Dusk Wake — those who stay
waking while the wood sleeps." Ede went very still at his shoulder. None
of this was known outside the Wake; men had been quietly drowned for
less. "The
Wake does not climb to cast the Demon King down, whatever it lets its
hirelings believe. It climbs to make the wood whole. And it keeps, in a
place I will not name, a roll called the Leaf-Ledger: every fragment of
the old forest, every floor that holds one, every lamp that might yet be
relit. It has kept that ledger current for the whole age of the tower.
No one outside the Wake has ever heard its name. You are the first."

The vault was silent. Somewhere above, a cocoon turned slowly on its
thread, glowing.

"Oh," said Matriarch Vyx, with four hundred years of appetite in one
soft syllable, "we are going to get on, hostage. Clause the third:
satisfied, and *handsomely*. Scrivener!" — a thrall jerked upright with
a silk-white book — "Take down the instrument. The parties will sign."

The host passed the Broodthrone in three days, and no army in the
world's history had ever marched so quietly. They went by
the gallery road under the hanging lanterns, thousands of boots on silk
that gave back no sound at all, and the wrapped court watched them pass
with whatever was left behind its hundred pairs of eyes, and every
soldier looked up, and no one spoke. Nix walked the whole road backwards
with the standard, watching the cocoons out of sight. Rhovan heard him
counting, and heard the count stop, and heard him start again, quieter.

*The floor is passed without loss,* Rhovan wrote, on the last night, by
the light of a cocoon he did not look at. *Terms enclosed. The tribute
binds the rising, not the Wake.*

He did not write what he had spent. She would know by spring.

On the third evening the rear-guard's horns sounded from the tier-gate,
thin and far above, and the last fighting company's lamp went up the
unwrapped stair and out of sight. But the clause counted wagons and
stragglers; Berrin's trains would climb this road until the freeze
stopped the lifts, and the tier behind would keep sending its freed up
while the road held. The great web settled — the whole vault breathing
one long settling breath of silk — and Rhovan of the Grey Rides was
alone in the Broodthrone with the Matriarch and her kept court and a
season of nights to be got through one at a time.

Silk sang, far up in the dark, as something vast and patient descended.

"Now, hostage," said Vyx, warmly, clause by clause, "we keep house."
