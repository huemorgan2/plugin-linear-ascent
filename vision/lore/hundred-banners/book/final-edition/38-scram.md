# Chapter 44 — Scram

Vega Coalbrow knew the ninety-third floor for what it was before the
lift-gate finished opening: not a forge. Forges have color. The light
that came through the gate-seams was colorless — not white, not pale,
but *absent,* a glare like the idea of burning with everything burnable
taken out — and it lay along the lead-vaulted galleries in long searing
bars where the shielding had cracked.

This was the heart. Everything the climb had fought — the Yoke that
maddened the floors, the fever that took the beasts, the aether that
welded the Wardens, the warmth in every collar from Roothollow's root to
the gate below — burned out of the core at this floor's center and went
down the tower's bones. Floor sixty-two had shown them the scar of an
older heart, dead an age. This one was alive. It came up through her
boot-soles like the breath of something sleeping.

Ferro of Coreside met them inside the gate. He was old the way the
thralls of the deep Made were old — burned hollow, hairless, his skin
gone the gray of his work — and he had
waited a lifetime to say what he said now, so he said it without
greeting:

"I keep the scram-lore. I know the shutting of the King's heart, the
safe shutting, and I have never told a living soul, because the tower
reads souls down here and I would not spend it early." His eyes, in all
that gray, were terribly alive. "You are the rising. I am spent either
way. Come to the Control Spire and I'll say my lesson."

Between the gate and the Spire lay the floor's whole cruelty — two
days' march of gallery and sluice, dwindling ahead to a colorless
vanishing point — and the host waded it. The forge-crews and the
Forge-Court legion came through in core-lead — two days of stripped
blast-shielding beaten into plate, the only armor that turned the
glare — and behind the vanguard the collar-breakers went along the
coolant sluices freeing the lead-crowned engineers one by one, and
each freed engineer turned in the same motion and dampened the coil
they had been feeding. No one instructed them. It
was the first thing every one of them did with a free hand.

At the Fuel Pens, Vega stopped being an armorer for a while. The pens
were cells off the core-floor where the spent were held — thralls whose
bodies had failed past working, racked like cordwood to be fed to the
engine as fuel. Her crews cut the pens open and carried out the living,
one hundred and forty of them, weightless as bundled straw. Nix walked
the line of litters counting them into the rolls, his slate scratching.
Ulfar stood at the mouth of the pens looking in — at the racks, at the
chute beyond them that ran down toward the coil-light — and Vega watched
her brother's face do a thing it had not done in all the floors since
the Frozen Sea. The mathematics behind it moved. She saw a carried digit
fall.

He said nothing. Giants finish their thoughts before they speak, and
this one, she judged, was long.

The demon came at them in the last gallery, where the sluices met under
the Control Spire and the shielding stood thickest. Halflight: the
core's own colorless flame given a body to guard its heart, taller than
Vega, umbilicaled to the coils behind it by ropes of glare, burning at
half a sun. Arrows went to ash a spear-length out. Core-lead blistered.
It could not be killed, Ferro had told them, only starved — *it cannot
die while it feeds; nothing on this floor can* — and so the battle for
the heart of the tower was not a battle at all but a work-shift: shield-
lines of lead holding the thing in the gallery mouth while the freed
engineers choked the coolant sluices, six of them, one by one, driving
the core up toward the red edge of its own law, where the scram could be
thrown; and the rising paid the shift's wages in burned crews and held.

"Now the Spire," Ferro said, when the sixth sluice groaned shut. "It
must be now. The heart is climbing toward its fury. We shut it from
above or it shuts itself from below, and if it goes from below, this
floor goes with it."

The Control Spire was the one place on the floor the leak could not
reach, and after the galleries its quiet was a blow. Shielded in lead an
arm thick, windowed in leaded glass, it hung above the core-floor like a
pulpit over a congregation of fire. Below, through the glass, the core
itself: coil upon coil of the colorless burning, the stolen aether of a
stolen country, breathing.

The controls were older than any craft Vega knew and simpler than she
had feared. The tower's makers had built for eternity, and eternity
favors levers. Sluice-wheels ranked along one wall; the coil-governors
in their banks; and alone on the high dais, under a hood of lead dusted
by generations of thralls who never once touched it, the scram-bar: one
great lever, counterweighted for a failing hand, that would drop the
dampers between the coils and kill the broadcast in a breath. The Yoke,
the fever, the welding-fire, all of it. One pull.

Ferro said his lesson. Wheel by wheel, turn by turn, the order of the
safe shutting, and the freed engineers moved down the wall as he spoke
and made the Spire ready, and the core's hum rose slowly through the
lead like a question, and at the end of it there was nothing left
between the rising and the quiet but the bar.

"It should be a smith," Ferro said simply. "It wants a strong arm and an
honest one. I've neither left." And he stood aside, and it was the
Coalbrows who went up the dais, sister and brother, and Ulfar Coalbrow
put his hand on the scram-bar and did not pull it.

"Vega," he said, slowly. "Look down at the core and hear me out once,
because you are the only soul in this tower who can check my figures."

She looked at his hand on the bar. "Ulfar —"

"The survey was theory," he said. "The light-table on the ice was
theory. This room is not. From this Spire, sister, it can be *done* —
everything I have argued since the Frozen Sea, done gently, done truly.
Throttle the broadcast to a tenth — the King's voice, the King's
veils, the coils that carry them, and nothing else. The Yoke at a
tenth is a headache, not a madness; the fever at a tenth is a shiver.
And the engines keep the core's full breath — the broadcast never fed
them, and the Strand needs no feeding — and I set the floors down."
His voice never rose. It never had. It was the terrible patient voice
of the only person in her life who had never once lied to her, and
every sentence arrived whole. "Thirty-one years, Vega. One generation
of a lit core, and I lower a hundred floors onto the earth like a cup
onto a table. No fall. No world of the freed left standing in the sky
for good, whole skies above its own stolen ground. And at the end of
it, the last coil damped, the Yoke out forever, and every floor a
*field* — not a monument, not a grave, a country again. That is my
whole crime, sister. I want to inherit the tower warm." His hand
flexed on the bar, and she saw it, the future, standing behind his
eyes: the Forge-Court's dream, the gentle mathematics, the tower not
broken but *kept* —

— and the room made no sound, but the room was listening: Ferro gray and
still, the freed engineers stopped along their wall of wheels, Ede at
the stair-head with her hand nowhere near her sword, a sword being no
answer here, and below the leaded glass the stolen heart of the world
burning, burning, waiting to hear which Coalbrow was right.

Vega did not argue ethics. She had lost that argument on the ice, and
she had watched him count the Fuel Pens, and she knew her brother to the
grain: he could carry any weight except a figure that would not balance.
So she said:

"Whose hands held yours in the quench-trough, the morning you lit your
first fire?"

Ulfar was silent.

"Say it. It's a plain question."

"Mother's," he said.

"And what did Aud Coalbrow make you say, every dawn of your
apprenticeship, before she would let you near the coals — because you
were quick, she said, and quickness forgets? I was small. I stood on the
bellows-crate to watch. Say it now, in this room, with your hand on
that."

And because he was a giant, and giants finish their sentences, he said
it — the forge-oath of a smith-matron dead these forty years, in a lead
pulpit over the burning heart of the stolen world:

"Light no fire I cannot quench." His voice had gone down to the bedrock
of itself. "Keep no fire past its work. And answer for every hour it
burns."

"Now do the sum you came up here to do," Vega said. "Not the floors, not
the engines — you were never wrong about the engines, and I will grieve
your engines with you. The *hours.* Thirty-one years of a lit core is
what —
count it in collar-hours, count it in fever-nights, count it in the
shifts they'd still be standing down there —" she pointed through the
glass, at the sluices, at the pens, at the gray generations of Coreside,
"— because somebody must tend a lit heart, Ulfar. Your survey has a
crew-table in it. I have read it. *Who did you write in the crew-table?*
Count the hours and answer for them, the way she'd stand there and make
us. And if you can answer them all, pull the bar out of my reach and
light your generation, and I'll not stop you." She stood eye to eye with
him, two giants under a hood of lead, and finished it the way their
mother finished everything, gently, with the hammer already falling.
"But you cannot. Nobody in this tower has ever answered for the hours.
That's what the tower *is.*"

The hum of the core came up through the floor of the Spire, patient,
enormous, thirty-one years long.

Her brother stood with his hand on the scram-bar for the time an
ingot takes to lose its color. She watched the mathematics behind his
face to the end
— the crew-table, the carried digit, the figure that would not balance —
and she did not help him, because this was his fire, and their mother's
third clause had a meaning under its meaning that Aud never had to say
aloud: *whoever cannot answer, quenches.*

"The work is done," Ulfar said at last, to the core, in the tone of a
smith closing his forge for the night. "It was done an age ago. It was
never — " and there the sentence failed, the only sentence Vega ever
heard her brother leave unfinished, and Thane Ulfar Coalbrow of the
Forge-Court pulled the scram with his own hand.

The bar went down. The dampers fell between the coils, all of them at
once, with a sound like a mountain swallowing.

And the tower went quiet.

There is no way to write the quiet except by what it took. The hum went
out from under everything — out of the floor, out of the lead, out of
the small bones of Vega's ears, a pressure she had carried since
Roothollow and called silence, having never once stood in the true kind.
Below the leaded glass the colorless glare folded into itself coil by
coil, and the dark that came was not the dark of a lamp put out but of a
*voice* stopping — the Yoke, dying mid-word on a hundred floors at once.
On ninety freed floors and the six unfreed above, every collar went cold
in the same breath; every welded thing lost its fire — far below, in the
eye of the seventieth's own gale, the Storm Queen's crown among them,
breaking; the fever loosened its grip on every beast in the stolen world
in one long unclenching, and no one on floor ninety-three heard any of
it, because what they heard was nothing, enormous, everywhere nothing,
the first nothing in an age and a half.

What no one in the lead country could see, the Girdle-sea saw — the
fishermen would tell it for the rest of their lives, and the host had it
from them after: the last veils dying together, and the grey rungs of
the world going gold at once — every last veil that was his. One dark,
at the fortieth, being nobody's but hers, stood.

In the gallery mouth, Halflight guttered. The coil-fed demon starved
standing, its glare going out of it like water out of cupped hands, and
what remained was a cinder in the shape of a kneeling man. The freed
engineers did not break it; they stood around it in a slow ring, the way
folk look at a hanged tyrant and find him small.

Ferro of Coreside sat down on the dais steps, a man whose one lifelong
purpose had just been spent, wholly, and wept without sound
into his gray hands, and would let no one comfort him, and did not need
them to.

Then Sliver spoke, and Vega learned what else the scram had cost.

*They are going out,* it said, from Ede's palm, and there was a note in
the dry old voice none of them had heard before. *You will want to know
what I am hearing. I will tell you. The Yoke rode the bones of my people
— the dead network, the shard-roads. We told you this on the wind
floors. I did not tell you that the riding kept the bones warm.* A
silence. *The last of the network is going dark. Kel-of-the-sixtieth,
gone. The three sleepers under the ice, gone. The one in the Wyrm's
hoard, who was rude to me for nine hundred years, gone; I had hoped —*
and the voice stopped itself, and started again, level. *There. Quiet.
The carried ones burn on — the census's two hundred and twelve, warm
in pockets and palms the length of this host — but the roads between
us are gone. The choir is what died. Each of us is a lamp now in a
country without roads. I am the last of the shardkind that walks
unbound — and the first, in an age, that burns on nothing but its own
choosing.*

Ede closed her hand around the splinter and held it against her
breastbone, and said nothing, correctly.

Vega stood in the dark Spire and ached, armorer's ache, mender's ache,
to fix it — and let the ache pass through her and go, because the stair
had taught her, one floor down, that not every grief is a broken thing,
and this one asked only to be *kept.* She kept it: the freeing of the
world, bought with the last hearth of the shardkind, and paid, like
everything on this climb, by whoever had the least left.

Her brother stood apart, at the leaded glass, looking down at the dead
coils. After a while he took the Forge-Court survey from its case — the
whole gentle mathematics, the country lowered like a cup — and Vega
braced to watch him burn it. He did not. He laid the scrolls on the dead
controls, squared their edges, and left them there in the dark for
whoever keeps the museums of lost futures, and came away, and his hands,
she saw, had already begun their grieving; they were smith's hands with
no fire that was theirs to tend.

"There will be work," she said to him. It was not comfort. Between
Coalbrows it was scripture.

"There will be work," he agreed, slowly, and finished his sentence,
because he was himself again, or would be: "colder work, and better."

They came down from the Spire into a floor full of freed thralls
standing in the great new silence with their faces turned up, the way
folk stand in the first snow. Above them the throne-tiers waited, six
floors of the King's last strength — deaf now, dark now, fire dead in
its constructs, word dead on its roads, and somewhere at the top of all
that quiet, the throne, and the last door before it.

It was Ferro who said it, at the stair-lift, keeper-fashion, knowing one
thing more than he was asked.

"Ninety-nine has a door that never opened," he said. "And a soldier in
it older than the drums. The core fed everything in the Made, but not
her; nothing feeds her. She just *stands* — the King's champion, the
first taken and the last kept." He looked at Ede, and at the banner on
Nix's small shoulder, and the oldest rumor of the tower's deep places
surfaced at last. "Word among the thralls was always: don't fear the
door. Fear waking what stands in it — for she was somebody's, once,
before she was his. Some company's, in the young world."

Ede went very still. Behind her, on its pole, in the dead heart of the
quiet tower, the Shieldwall banner stirred — though there was, of
course, in that deep place, no wind at all.
