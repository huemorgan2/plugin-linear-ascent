# Chapter 5 — The Ledger of Gates

Rhovan of the Grey Rides told the truth for a living, in fractions, and
he had learned the trade in rooms like this one: a hall too large for
its lamps, full of paper that remembered more than the people did.

The Counting Halls ran away from him in shelf-canyons of ledger-stone
and brass, glittering faintly where the lamplight caught the coin-dust
hanging in the air. Abacuses the size of gates hung frozen mid-count
overhead. Somewhere off in the stacks a ledger wisp was reading numbers
aloud to nobody, a clerk's ghost caught in an unfinished sum, and the
host's children had already learned to walk wide of it. The van had
broken the Warden here three days before Rhovan came up — Brassbone, a
vault-beetle welded round a core of tally-gears, which had counted every
blow it took and answered in kind until Ede stopped feeding it blows and
fed it a lie of numbers instead, and the count choked, and the great
ledger-door of the stair-lift read out its final balance and opened.

Freed, for everyone, forever. That was the canon of the climb, cut into
the Stone at Roothollow under every name: a lift once opened can never
be shut again.

No one had thought to write down that it could be *rented*.

The chain was hung across the stair-lift's arch at the height of a
man's chest, good new iron, and coins had been nailed to the post
beside it in a neat rising column — the tariff, displayed the way a
fair displays prizes. A head-price for a man. A head-and-a-half for a
giant. A price for a mule, a price for a barrel, a price for a bale of
arrows going up to the war. The men who kept it wore good boots and
coin-belts and the gate-companies' grey, and they were not soldiers,
and were not pretending to be. There were nine of them. Rhovan had
counted the same way Nix counted, out of the same instinct, though he
would not have admitted the kinship.

Below the chain the queue stood. That was the part Ede could not look
at and could not stop looking at: muster-folk of Bannerline with their
kit on their backs, come up to join the rising; Ledgerstone clerks with
their salvaged books; a family of Weirsend eel-trappers who had walked
free out of a floor their grandmothers were stolen onto, forty years of
waiting paid off at last — stopped at an open door by men of their own
kind, for coin.

"Read it to me again," Ede said.

The toll-captain — Weck, a round, patient, untroubled man — held up his
warrant so the seals caught the light. He had shown it four times and
would show it forty. "By charter of the gate-companies of Roothollow,"
he said, "and under the seal of Gilder Voss, Tollmaster-General:
maintenance of freed gates, upkeep of stairs, surety of passage. All
lawful, captain. All stamped."

"The lift was freed three days ago. You've maintained nothing but the
chain."

"The chain's the upkeep," said Weck, comfortably. "Anarchy's free,
captain. Order costs."

At Ede's shoulder the splinter burned. *In eighty years,* said
Sliver, dry as coin-dust, *I have watched this tower try every cruelty
it knows. It never once thought of this. He undercut the Demon King
with a toll-chain.*

Serjeant Hale's Cullers stood off to the left in their ash-grey,
watching the Tollmen the way they watched everything, sorting it into
what wanted killing now and what wanted killing later. Hale himself
said only, "Nine men. One order," and let it hang. And that was the
trap in it, Rhovan saw, laid by no one and worse for it: cut the chain
and cut nine men of Roothollow down before the queue they were robbing,
and the rising became a thing that killed Men at open gates — and every
toll-post from here to the Made would buy itself a garrison and call it
defense. Voss did not need to win the argument. He only needed Ede to
end it with iron.

"No one touches them," Ede said. "We do not pay, and we do not cut. Not
today."

"The bread pays," Weck observed, easy as a posted tariff, nodding at
the supply-sledges. "Or the bread waits."

Rhovan watched Ede take that, and bank it, and walk away, and he made
his own tally, in the currency he dealt in. Warrants have ledgers
behind them. Ledgers have rooms. Rooms have locks, and locks have a
whole family of honest little truths they will tell a patient elf in
the dark.

"Where are you going?" Nix asked him at the fire-line, fast, three
questions in one, as ever. "You've got your quiet boots on, you've got
your nothing-face on, where?"

"To do some maintenance," said Rhovan.

The Tollmen had requisitioned a counting-vault off the Shelf-Maze for
their strong-room, which told Rhovan most of what he needed before he
ever touched the door: men who keep books they are proud of leave them
at the post, under the lamp, for showing. Books that get walked into a
giants' vault at night are books with two kinds of truth in them.

He went in at the hour when the paper-fungus drifts — the halls' one
blessing, for his purposes, since the Tollmen's watch spent it indoors
with the shutters fast, having learned what settled on warmth. Tolla
Brasspen, chief reckoner of Ledgerstone, had drawn him the vault's
approach on the back of a cancelled account and asked no questions
aloud, though her face had asked one, and he had paid it a fraction:
"Your halls kept honest count for a thousand years. Someone up here is
keeping the other kind." That had been enough for her. It was true,
too. Rhovan told the truth whenever it would do the work of a lie.

The tally-lock was giants' work, older than the Tollmen, and it opened
not to a key but to a correct reckoning; the Tollmen had not changed
it, because they could not read it. Brasspen could. He turned the sum
she had taught him — so much ore raised, so much owed, so much *paid*,
the mountain's own arithmetic — and the door heard its answer and let
him in as courteously as a clerk.

The toll-ledger lay on a shelf between a strongbox and a wheel of
sealing wax. He did not touch the strongbox. Silver leaves marks on a
man; paper only leaves it on the world.

He read for the time a lamp takes to burn a finger's width, and it was
what he had expected, and it bored him, and he blessed it for boring
him: the skim. Two counts kept side by side, the tariff taken at the
chain and the tariff reported to Voss, and the difference sleeping in
Weck's strongbox. Every toll-post since coin was minted has kept that
second column. It was leverage, it would open the chain, and an hour
earlier it was all he had come for.

Then he turned the back leaves, where the ledger had been sewn onto an
older book — the gate-companies' remittance-book, brought up from
Roothollow, because the Tollmen's chain was new but their *accounts*
were not — and the boredom went out of him like heat out of iron.

The entries in the old book ran level and dull for years. Gate-fees,
carters' bonds, lamp-oil, wages. And then, alone on its page, ruled off
from the entries above and below it as if the clerk himself had not
wanted it touching the honest sums:

*Received of a Roothollow hand, by warrant, the sum entire — carried up
by the posts of the companies, stage by stage, to the high tower's
receiver, and there discharged. No goods. No consignment. Settlement of
account.*

No goods. Money does not climb a tower where money buys nothing —
unless the thing it bought was already sold, and delivered, and the
tower was *settling*. Payment did not come down for the Shieldwall
Company. Payment went *up* — and Rhovan set his fingertip under the
date, and held it there, and made himself read it three times, because
an agent who believes a thing at first reading will believe anything.

He carried, sewn into his coat's lining, the ledger-page that had
started all of this — the page he had laid in front of a sober, retired,
furious woman in Roothollow half a year ago, the page that proved the
Red Warcamp had been given her company's route before her company ever
climbed. He did not need to take it out. He knew its week the way other
men know their name-day.

The same week. The sale below; the settlement above; seven days
between, the time a laden post takes to climb twenty floors.

And at the foot of the entry, where a name should have sat, there was
no name. There was a warrant-mark, stamped in the gate-companies'
fashion: Roothollow's own device, the hollow root under the bar, and
in its quarter the small countersign that meant a private hand — some
one person, of the town, with the standing to move coin under the
companies' seal and the sense not to sign for it.

Someone in Roothollow had signed the tower's receipt for Ede's company.
Someone from home.

Rhovan stood in a strong-room a mile inside a stolen mountain and heard
his own heart, and around him the coin-dust glittered and settled on
the shelves, patient, the way the truth settles on everything given
time. Then he took out his copying-lead and did his work: the skim, the
entry, the date, the mark, each of them twice.

He put the ledger back with its dust squared, and reckoned the door
shut behind him, and walked back through the drifting stacks dividing
what he had learned into shares — because that was his trade, and
because, he told himself, a truth this heavy dropped whole on Ede
Stonhal would break something in her that the climb still needed. The
skim was Weck's, to be spent at the chain tomorrow. The payment and the
week were Ede's; she had earned them at prices he did not like to
think about. The mark, the little countersign, the shape of a single
private hand — that was the Wake's, and went that same night in cipher
down the column, in the quill-work of a bored elf writing home.

Three shares, honestly divided. He would have a long climb to learn
what he learned too late: that a truth divided in three is three lies,
and that he had just written the first letter of the last one.

At the chain, in the morning, Rhovan spent his first share.

He gave Weck the numbers quietly, at the post, in the tone one book-
keeping man uses to another, and watched the comfort leave the toll-
captain's face by weights and measures. He did not threaten. He
recited. The skim, the sum in the strongbox, the tariff Voss believed
and the tariff Voss was owed — and the only word anything like a blade
came at the end, and was one syllable: "Choose."

The chain came down before the lamp above the arch had guttered. Weck
posted it as an amnesty of the gate-companies — *passage free to the
freed, in honor of the rising* — which was, Rhovan thought, the most
Roothollow sentence ever nailed to a post: a robbery, repented in the
grammar of a gift. The queue went up. The eel-trapper family crossed
under the arch, last of a forty-year wait discharged at no charge, and
Ede stood watching with her jaw set wrong.

Rhovan gave her the second share on the stairs, plainly, both facts and
no third: money had gone up the tower through the Tollmen's posts, no
goods against it; and its date sat in the same week as the sale of her
company. He watched it land. She did not rage. She had spent her rage
years ago on cheaper truths. She only said, "Through Roothollow," not
as a question, and looked back down the stair a long breath — down the
shaft of the freed lift, down the column, toward home — and then turned
and climbed, which was the whole of her answer, and the only one the
climb ever accepts.

He did not give her the mark. He fed the fraction to himself the way
he always did: *when it means something. When it is more than a stamp.
When telling her arms her instead of gutting her.* An elf can keep a
truth warm for years. It is the trade's one talent.

They made the next five floors in five weeks, and the war let them be
small weeks, which the rising had learned to distrust and take anyway.

Every floor asked the same walk of them now: up out of a country's
ground at one rim, where the gate-towns kept their farms and markets
close against the light, then the long slow bend of the land down and
inland and back to the light again, and the next stair always standing
at the far edge. The tower had set its two doors a country apart, so
there was no such thing as passing a floor. You crossed it, or you
stayed.

On the Shearing Downs the chalk lay open under the false light like a
page, and shear-mad rams came down the wind at the column and were
cured one by one, shaking their great heads, trotting away ordinary.
The downs were three days wide, bare white country rolling to a white
horizon with the wind never once stopping, the dew-ponds skinned grey
with wool-mould and every fold-wall on the high ground penning
nothing. Ewan Woolgate walked a day of it beside the van with his
crook and his flock-book, counting a flock the Theft had scattered,
because the count was hopeless and the keeping of it was not; and the
night the column lay at Woolgate the shepherds raised the broken
beacon on the Long Barrow, and folk came in off the far combes who
had been hiding since the shearing.
At the Eaves the dark wood stood full of dead signals, the hum of
messages sent an age ago to no one; the edge-elves of Lastlamp came out
to see elves climbing armed, and said almost nothing, and gave them
almost everything — lamp-oil, guides, the wood's edge-paths. Under the
boots the listening-moss gave back scraps of the dead lamp-song, and
from the crown of the Watchpine, strung with old signal-copper, a
scout could look out over gloom lying level to the floor's own
horizon — a night the width of a country, with one watch-fire in it.
Rhovan walked those under his hood. Whatever else the Grey Rides had made of
him, it had begun somewhere like this, and he told no one, which was
itself a fraction.

In the Gear Galleries the machinists of Sprocket Row threw the master
clutch for the first time since the Theft, and the halls' endless
rumble stopped, and giants wept at a silence. The Grinding Pit gave up
its pressed pit-fighters, Red Orcs with chain-scarred fists who looked
at the freed camp with no idea what to do in it; three stayed, grim as
tax, and Ede took them, over Hale's one flat objection: "You'll want
killing men soon enough, captain. You needn't recruit them from the
kennel." Above the Pit lay the Cable Roots, and the column walked that
country four days on lines the color of old blood: a cavern strung
floor to roof with copper grown root-thick, live bundles humming and
spitting blue static into a dark the sparks lit in stutters. Fend
Wiregate, the line-master, one hand scarred white, led them the whole
way along the dead lines out of the Deadline Camp's logbooks, reading
the tangle by the pitch of its static, and nobody strayed an arm's
length, and nobody needed telling twice. The column crossed the
Hollow Lanes behind Cobb Hedgeharbor's
notched staff, hedge-blind wolves striking from the joining lanes and
going cured down the green tunnels, and came up at last into the open
at Hedgeharbor, sixteen floors above the world's floor, with the
mine-tier's last country above them and everyone's boots done for.

The word was waiting for them there, having come up the lanes faster
than baggage ever moves: a giant had arrived at Sprocket Row, alone,
off the freed lifts from below. A woman of the forge-realm, grey with
road-dust, who had asked one question at every landing since
Roothollow — *where is the captain called Stonhal* — and who carried,
wrapped in oil-cloth and iron bands like something that might otherwise
get loose, a sheaf of the tower's own papers.

"Asking for me by name," Ede said.

"By name," said the runner, "and captain — she wouldn't set the papers
down. Not to eat. Not to sleep. The machinists offered her a strong-box
and she looked at them like they'd offered to hold her hands to a
forge."

Ede turned back down the lanes that same evening, and Rhovan went with
her, and found he was cataloguing already, out of habit: a giant, off
the lifts, alone, with documents she would not be parted from and a
question she asked by name. In his trade that shape had a meaning. In
his trade, that shape was a confession, walking uphill to be heard.

He was, for once in his fractional life, entirely right.
