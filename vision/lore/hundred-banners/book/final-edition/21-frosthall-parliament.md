# Chapter 24 — The Frosthall Parliament

The hall-steward of Hearthold kept her songs the way other keepers had
kept weirs and lamps and lid-maps: hidden, mended, and ready. Her name
was Bruna, and she met Ede in the Frozen Yard at dusk, among frost-birch
hung with shields, and before she would speak a word of Wardens or
lifts she walked the general down the row of trees and made her read.

Every shield bore a name in the giants' deep script. Every name was a
thane who had fallen at the endless feast and risen from it collared,
bound to drink and fight and die and drink again at the pleasure of a
song that never stopped. The firelight from the hall doors came out
across the snow and touched the shield-rims gold, and inside, under
that lovely light, the feast roared on as it had roared for years,
tireless as weather.

"You'll hear men tell you the Frosthall is a trap dressed as a
welcome," Bruna said. "It's worse. It is a welcome, trapped. My jarl
was the best of us. He'd give his own high seat to a cold stranger and
stand for the length of the meal. The tower took that open hand and
froze it to a fist, and set him at the head of his own table, feasting
on his own folk." She stopped at the last tree. "So understand me,
general of the rising. You do not come to kill a Warden tonight. You
come to wake a good lord from a bad dream, and I have kept what wakes
him."

Ede had broken eleven Wardens with steel and siege-craft, and one with
mourning. She stood in the snow and listened to a hall-steward lay out
a battle-plan made of a cask and a cup and a handful of old songs, and
she thought of Skarn dying in the Warcamp still believing his collar
was a rank, and she said, "Tell me what you need."

*She is asking you for an army that sings,* Sliver said against her
palm, dry as always, old as always. *Not one climber I have carried was
ever asked for that before. I find I am glad to have lasted.*

They went in at the middle of the night's third course, when the
war-feast was loudest and the ice skald's harp had the whole hall by
the blood. That was the fight, and it was like no fight in the annals
Ede meant one day to write: a wedge of shields down the length of the
longest table in the world, hearth-wolves cured left and right into
dogs that dropped by the fire and slept through the rest of it, the
freed thanes' shields called out name by name from the yard so that
collared warriors faltered mid-swing at the sound of themselves — and
at the center of it Bruna Hearthold and every voice she had drilled,
singing the true songs against the skald's war-song, plain tunes,
hearth tunes, the songs the feast had drowned, while Vega walked
through the wreck of the benches like a tower moving and put her
craft to collar after collar. The rime-shrouded skald bent its whole
frozen art on them and was out-sung in its own hall, and when its
song broke, it drained away into the hearth it had poisoned, and the
harp fell silent for the first time in years, and the silence was
like water on a burn.

Jarl Hrimgar stood alone at the High Seat in his war-plate of rime,
huge even for his folk, the frost-crown welded to his brow, and Ede
came up the dais steps with no sword in her hand. In her right was
the cup. Vega's shears had opened the great collar at his neck; the
Mead of Memory did the rest, and the rest was the worst part to
watch. Memory came back to him the way feeling comes back to a
frozen limb.

He looked a long time down the ruin of his hall, at the burst casks
and the cured hounds and the thanes kneeling over thanes.

"I remember all of it," he said at last. Giants speak slowly, and
finish their sentences, and this was the slowest sentence Ede ever
heard. "That is the cruelty in the craft of it. I was not absent. I
was at the head of my own table the whole while, with my own hands,
in my own hall, and I remember every course of it."

He took the frost-crown from his brow — it came away with a sound
like ice leaving a rock — and he looked at the thing, and he did not
throw it down in rage, which Ede half expected. He set it on the
table the way a man sets down a debt.

"You will want the lift," he said. "It stands behind my seat. It is
yours, and the seat is yours, and the hall. I have fed on my folk
for eleven years. It is a long time before I sit at the head of
anything." He looked at her then, and something came into the ruined
face that had not been there under the crown, and it took her a
moment to know it for the thing Bruna had promised: the open hand.
"But before you climb, general — you and every soul you brought, and
every soul on every floor you've freed who can make the journey. My
hall has served eleven years of the wrong feast. Let it serve a true
one. Stay. Eat at my table. Be welcome."

Behind her, at the foot of the dais, Ede heard a goblin blow out a
breath eleven floors long.

The gloom came off the jarldom the next day, while the hall slept off
its rescue. Nobody marked the hour it finished; there was only Bruna
at dusk, stopped among the frost-birches with a shield in her two
hands, looking up past the eaves at stars — true stars, over a country
where the grey had stood so long it had passed for sky.

They stayed the winter, and the hall became something no floor of
the Ascent had ever seen, and the word for what it became was old
and half-forgotten and had to be dusted before use, like the good
cups: a capital.

It began with the mundane things, the way everything real began.
The host needed quarters, and Hearthold's folk opened the steadings
of the jarldom. The wounded of the Long Stair needed beds, and the
Undercroft's smaller vaults were swept and warmed. The freed floors
below needed to send their tithes and their troubles somewhere, and
suddenly there were couriers on every lift from the Fencerows to
the Frosthall gate, and petitions piling on trestles, and Voss's
Tollmen keeping accounts of grain that no one had asked them to
keep, and keepers arriving in person — keepers, who never left
their floors — because word had gone down the whole freed tower
that the rising was to hold a council of everyone, and everyone,
for the first time since the Theft, meant everyone.

Ede had commanded companies and then columns and then a host. She
had never in her life presided, and she learned in one winter why
kings went grey. The parliament of the banners met in the great
hall from the feast of first-frost onward, and it argued from the
morning horn to the dousing of the lamps, and the arguing was the
point — Rhovan said that to her, one night when she despaired of
it, and he was right in the way that only a man who lies for a
living can be right about truth. The tower had been built so this
noise could never happen. Every voice raised over a grain-ledger
in Hrimgar's hall was a wall of it coming down.

The matters before the parliament were four, and each one nearly
broke it.

The Forge-Court came first, because Ulfar Coalbrow did nothing
second. The tower's engines, he argued — and he argued well, slow
and finished as all his sentences, with the survey-scrolls to hand
— could lower the floors. Gently. Over a generation. The rising
did not have to end at a throne with a sword in it; it could end
with every stolen country set down whole in its own bed, if the
engines were kept, and manned, and mastered. Against him stood his
own sister, who would not have the Yoke's core lit one hour past
victory, and half the keepers, who had heard the word *generation*
before from folk who did not have to live under the floor being
lowered. The quarrel went four days. What came out was a clause,
because that is what parliaments make instead of corpses: the
engines' fate would be decided by a freed world's own council, on
the ground, after the throne — nothing kept, nothing scrapped,
until the world that owned them could vote. Vega signed it with
her jaw set. Ulfar signed it with the look of a man who believes
time is on his side. Ede began, that winter, to understand that a
charter is a truce dressed for church.

The Wake came second. Calyne stood in the well of the hall, grey
and exact, and asked for what the Wake always asked for — the
elf-woods, the scattered fragments of the one great forest, raised
to the head of the line of march — and the hall heard her out with
folded arms, because nobody loves a spymaster, and Ede watched
suspicion do its quiet work and did not altogether fight it in
herself. The woods clause failed.
Calyne took the defeat without a line of her face moving, gathered her
papers, and stayed — which Ede noted, because it was what a loyal
woman would do, and also what a patient traitor would do, and the
two had become impossible to tell apart at any distance. Rhovan
alone went and sat by her afterward. He told the truth in
fractions, that man, but he seemed to know which fraction was
owed where.

The gates came third, and this was the fight Ede had dreaded,
because it was about money, and quarrels about money are the only
ones nobody forgives. The Tollmen had kept lifts and ledgers on
thirty floors since before the rising had a name, sometimes
lawfully, mostly not. The keepers wanted every freed gate open and
unkept forever. Voss wanted his investment back. The debate was
proceeding toward knives when Ede did the thing she had been
turning over since the Cindermouth, and stood, and offered Gilder
Voss the post of quartermaster-general of the rising: every store,
every train, every granary and ferry and toll-house of the freed
tower under his hand, at a fixed honest fee, audited by the
parliament twice a year.

"You are setting a thief to keep the thieves," Voss said, into a
silence you could have carved.

"I am," said Ede. "You'll find there's no better warden for a
lock than the man who has picked it."

He laughed — the first time anyone in that hall had heard him do
it — and haggled the fee for two hours as a point of honor, and
signed. The gates clause passed the same evening: *every lift of
a freed floor stands open to all, for nothing, forever.* The
Tollmen wept no tears; they had, as of that afternoon, a larger
concern to profit by honestly. Berrin, who had provisioned the
rising out of nothing since Roothollow and knew Voss's stores
better than Voss did, offered the new quartermaster-general his
own muster-rolls and manifests entire, the work of two years,
without being asked. "A fuller cellar than mine," he said mildly,
handing over the keys of his office as if they weighed nothing,
"and about time." Half the hall cheered him. He was that kind of
man; he gave a thing away and the room felt richer.

The last matter had no scrolls and no survey, and it was the one
the tower would have understood least of all. A thane of the
Frosthall — new-freed, still raw at the neck — rose to ask why
there were goblins at a table of the peoples, and kobolds behind
them, seeing as such folk had held every gate and fought under
every Warden from the Fencerows up. The hall went bad-quiet. Nix
stood on his bench so as to exist above the table-line and had
got as far as "Well, now, that's a question with a lot of floors
under it—" when Dun Harrow rose from the Shieldwall benches with
the banner in his one hand, and stood, and said nothing at all
for a moment, in the way that made whole camps go still.

"This standard fell at the Kingsfield," Dun said. "A goblin
picked it up. It has come up forty floors since and it has never
once been dropped. The Pressed were the tower's first prisoners
and the rising's first proof. He sits, or I stand for the rest
of this parliament."

Nobody in the gathered world wanted Dun Harrow standing on their
account. The clause passed: a seat for the freed Pressed among
the peoples, in their own right, in their own names — the names,
Ede thought, watching Nix sit down very carefully, that some of
them had chosen for themselves in a winter camp with a goblin
teaching them how.

They signed the charter on the last day of the deep winter, on
the High Seat's table, under the smoke-hole, in the light of the
honest hearth.

It was not a beautiful document. It was four clauses and a
preamble argued down to the bone, copied fair by three clerks,
and the preamble said the only thing the whole tower agreed on:
*that the world was one country before the Theft, and shall be
one country after, and that no floor climbs alone.* Then the
names. Hrimgar rose to put his down first, and stood a moment
over the page, the pen a straw in his great hand, and said —
slowly, the way his folk finish what they begin — "Alone, each
of us was a floor. Stacked, we were his tower. Standing shoulder
to shoulder, for the first time since the Theft, we are a
stair." Nobody wrote it into the charter. Nobody needed to; the
hall would carry it up the rest of the tower for him. He signed
in the deep script, and Vega and Ulfar
after him, brother and sister, one pen. Calyne signed small and
plain. Voss signed with a flourish that used up more ink than
his whole clause. Hobb Fennick, sent up from the first floor of
the world with his counts under his arm, signed for the keepers
in a farmer's careful hand, checking the letters twice, and
after him the keepers of forty floors. Rhovan signed a name that
may or may not have been his. Nix drew, slowly and with his
tongue between his teeth, the character he had settled on for
himself, and under the seat where no one could see, Ede heard
the faint click of a tally-cord being counted. Dun signed
one-handed, steadying the page with the banner's butt. Berrin
signed last but one, quartermaster's neat fair hand, and looked
up at Ede with the smile he had been handing her across a
counter since she was a drunk nobody in a market town, and said,
"Your mother would not have believed one word of this."

Ede signed last. Fifty-eight banners hung from the rafters above
her head, and the wind from the smoke-hole moved them all
together, softly, like one cloth.

*Item,* said Sliver, in the dry old voice it kept for the things
it meant. *Sixty floors ago you would not climb with thirty. You
now hold a parliament of nine thousand. I am given to understand
this is called recovery.*

It did not feel like recovery. It felt, Ede thought, standing
under the banners with the ink drying on the age's strangest
paper, like standing at the exact center of the one thing Vharuk
had built a hundred floors to prevent. Every clause in the
charter was a thing the tower was *for* stopping. Every name on
it was a cell emptied. When word of this paper reaches the
throne, she thought, he will stop sending Wardens to slow us and
start sending something else — because from tonight we are not
climbers to him. We are the thing he fears. We are the
gathering.

She would remember thinking it, later, with the taste of ash in
her mouth. She had been looking a hundred floors up for the
answer, and the answer had been in the room.

But that night there was only the hall, warm to its rafters, and
Hrimgar's folk already laying the long tables for the great
feast of the charter — a true feast, the first the Frosthall had
served in eleven years, set for three nights hence — and Berrin
in the thick of the planning as he always was, cheerful over the
lists with Bruna and the goblin cooks, drawing up the seating in
his neat quartermaster's hand so that no two old quarrels would
share a bench, so that Cullers sat far from keepers and Tollmen
far from everyone, so that every soul in the gathered world
would sit down that night among friends.

"Leave the doors open," the jarl told his stewards, weeping,
happy, his open hand come home. "All of them. Let the light out.
Let anyone come in."
