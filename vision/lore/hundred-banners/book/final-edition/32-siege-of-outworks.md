# Chapter 38 — The Siege of the Outworks

Nix counted the squad in the dark, because the dark was where counting
mattered most.

"Eleven," he said. "Eleven, and the eleventh is asleep. Mag. Mag, you
beautiful idiot, you cannot sleep in a sap-trench, the trench is damper
than you are and twice as likely to fall on us."

"Wasn't sleeping," said Mag, who was a kobold, and had been asleep. "Was
listening to the wall."

That was the trouble. On this floor you listened to the wall, and the wall
listened back.

Three days ago the rising had come up out of the Pale Court's green dark
into a country with no country in it. Nix had stood on the last stolen
grass with the cut-down standard he'd hidden behind at the Kingsfield —
two years and seventy floors ago, when hiding was the whole of his craft —
and looked at the thing above them, and for once had found nothing funny
to say. Every floor of his life had hung in the sky wearing the ragged
shape of the country it was cut from — headlands, bays, the crook of an
old border river drawn out in air. The edge above the Pale Court ran in
one clean unbroken curve, no bay, no crook, no signature: geometry,
perfect as a coin, and Nix, who had grown up under stolen borders,
understood the shape before anyone said it aloud. No people had ever
drawn that line. Below floor eighty-one, every land they'd crossed had
been a place the King stole. From here up it was all a thing the King
built. Slag-fill and rivet. Iron-bramble on the ramparts, grown like
thorn and meaning it. A rampart of riveted plate ran across the whole
world, out of sight both ways, and there was no soil under it, and
nothing grew, and the crows that mobbed its embrasures were the only
honest life in sight.

The First Outwork, the sapper-captain called it. Dara Sappersrest, her
face grey with the fill her people had been made to dig, had said the rest
plainly, the way keepers said things: *the tower is built of us. My
sappers are in that wall. Rivet by rivet. Some of them went in alive.*

So the rising had gone into the ground, because the sappers had left them
a road there. The sap-head ran in under the outwork's footing, shored by
dead men's craft, and at its end sat a charge and a cache of
demolition-lore twenty years in the hoarding. Above it, the
Furnace-Trench ran with trench-gas and slag-light, and the plan — Vega's
plan, and Dara's, argued out over a light-table until the watch had
twice changed — was simple the way a hammer is simple. Speak the
rampart's own seams open — *Unrivet*, a breach made of the wall's own
craft — and send the collar-breakers sweeping through it first, ahead
of any spear, to free whatever the King drove into the gap. Get the
work-gangs clear. Fire the gas. Drop the King's first wall into the
King's own hole.

Nix's part came before the fire. It always did, now.

"Collar-breakers," he said, "on me. Count off."

They counted. Eleven voices, none of them born to the same floor, three
of them wearing collars they had thrown down themselves — Nix had watched
each one do it, and every time it was the finest thing he had ever seen,
and he had seen a wyrm and a storm-queen and Vega eating soup. They went
up the sap and out into the trench-dark below the plate, where the second
wave crouched with rivet-steel shields — the tower's own outwork-plate,
stripped and turned, Vega's forge-crews grinning behind armor with the
King's rivets still in it.

Somewhere above, a word went out that was not a word so much as a craft.
*Unrivet.* Nix felt it in his teeth. All along the rampart's face, seams
that had held for an age let go together, plate shrieking off plate, and
the whole first wall of the Made opened like a badly sewn coat.

Then it was noise, and Nix ran into it.

The outwork imps came scuttling down the breach the way imps fought —
badly, and forever, rivet-fisted, driven onto the shields by something
that was not courage. Nix had been driven like that once. He knew the gait
of it. He went in low, under a swung fist, and got his hand flat on the
imp's collar, and said the word the rising had carried since floor twenty
like a coal carried in a horn.

*Break-collar.*

The collar cracked. Not like iron. Like ice off a rope. The imp sat down
in the slag with its mouth open — they always sat down, that was the first
thing freedom did, it took the legs — and stared at its own fists as if
they had just been given back.

"Up you get," Nix said. "You're one of mine now. What's your name? You've
got one. It's under there somewhere. It'll keep. Shield-line's that way,
hot food's behind it, and nobody — " he caught the imp's chin, made it
look at him, goblin to imp, small thing to small thing — "nobody drives
you. Ever again. Go."

It went. Toward the food, which was correct, and away from the King, which
was the whole war.

Around him his eleven were doing the same, and behind the shields the
second wave was doing it, and the count was running in Nix's head the way
counts did, all on its own. Two. Five. Nine. A gate-hellknight, faceless
in riveted plate, froze with its halberd up while Mag hung off its arm
shrieking the word, and when the collar went the knight knelt in the
breach and would not rise for a long while, and then rose *theirs* — the
heaviest infantry the rising owned, and it had walked in on the other
side. Fourteen. Twenty. Twenty-two.

Not all of them. A hellknight took Brann's spear through the collar and
died before the word could reach it, and Nix stood over the body — the
enemy's body — and hated the arithmetic that made him glad the spear had
been faster. A death that should not have been needed. There was a whole
column of those in his count, and it never stopped running either.

Then the wall itself joined the battle, and the count stopped meaning
anything for a while.

Wall-flesh, the sappers called it, because sappers name things true. A
groaning length of rampart shrugged out of the line of the plate and
reached — reached, with arms of mortared bone and rivet, the dead in the
fill waking wrong and dragging at the living to bring them in. And behind
it came the Warden. Rivetgrim: a walking section of the outwork, wall made
beast, studded with the bones of the crews built into it, and it did not
roar. Walls don't. It came on quiet, at the pace of something that has
never once needed to hurry, and it took a forge-hand into its plate and
sealed the seam before anyone could reach her.

"Clear the trench!" Dara's voice, cracked brass. "All gangs — clear the
trench, down the sap, NOW!"

Nix ran the trench-lip the wrong way, because his squads were still in it.
He counted them out — eight, nine, Mag hauling a freed imp by the wrist,
eleven, and behind them Vega's crews walking backward with the word,
*Unrivet, Unrivet,* peeling the Warden's plate off it a seam at a time so
that it came on stripping itself like a man shedding coats in a fire.

The last sapper cleared the sap-mouth. Dara looked once at the wall her
people were buried in, and lit the charge.

The trench-gas fired with a sound Nix felt in the ground rather than
heard, and the First Outwork of the Made went down into its own footing —
not toppled but *dropped*, all at once, the way a lie drops when the last
man stops telling it. The dust stood over it in a column. When it cleared,
Rivetgrim lay in the ruin as dead plate, and what had been driven out of
it was already gone, drained down and out of any land that had ever been
anyone's — an eviction, the shardminds called that, and for once the word
was gentle enough. The dead in the wall were only dead now. That was all
liberation could give them, and it gave it.

In the quiet after, Nix did the count.

Eleven collar-breakers had gone in. Thirty-one people stood around him at
the muster — his eleven, and twenty who had woken up on the wrong side of
the battle and walked out of it on the right one, sitting in the slag
learning their own names.

Thirty-one. He wrote it on his slate, and under it, because the number
mattered more than any number he had ever counted, he wrote it again.

The Chain Yards never went quiet, and so nobody heard the rising come in.

Floor eighty-two was a din you wore like weather: hammer on link, link on
drum, drum on gantry, and under it all the hiss of red chain going into
black water. The tower forged its chains here — every shackle, every
hoist-line, every drag-chain that bound every floor above and below — and
it forged them with slave-gangs whose own shackles were the finest work on
the floor. Wend Linkside said so himself, and showed them, turning his
ankle to the forge-light with a craftsman's disgusted pride: a
quench-hardened link he had been filing at in the dark for eleven years
with a file he kept hidden in a drain.

"Eleven years," Nix said. "I like you. That's a real number. That's a
number with teeth."

The battle for the yards was fought the way Vega fought now, which was to
say mostly against the machinery. Her forge-crews jammed the trip-hammer
line with its own tongs, and the endless row of tower-driven hammers beat
itself lame; they seized the Great Hoist, and then every chain on the
floor answered the rising — drag-lines, hoists, and the shackles on ten
hundred ankles alike. Wend taught them the word his people had wanted made
flesh for a generation, the word the yards existed to prevent.
*Break-chain.* It snapped any binding at a saying. The slaves struck off
their own shackles with it, and some of them wept, and some of them
laughed, and one old giantess just stood holding her two halves of link,
one in each fist, weighing them, as if the scales of something long
unbalanced had finally come level.

The Warden nearly spoiled all of it. Anchorwrath came off the drums at the
shift-change — every drag-line in the yard welded into one thrashing
anchor-serpent, hung with the bones of the crews forged into it — and it
fought the only way a chain knows, by binding and hauling. It took six of
the shield-line off their feet in one coil and ran them toward the
quench-pits, and the pits were still boiling.

Nix got there first, because he was lightest, and had spent a war being
lightest. He went up the moving chain itself, link over link — do not
think about it, counting is better than thinking, four links, nine links,
fifteen — and jammed Wend's master-file crosswise into the living seam
where link ground on link, and the serpent balked just long enough. Vega's
voice came down the yard like a rockfall, slow and complete as her people's
speech always was: "Break-chain. Unrivet. *Now,* all crews, and do not
stop saying it." The words walked down the serpent's length in a wave, and
Anchorwrath fell out of the air as dead links, a hundred fathoms of it,
ringing on the forge-floor like the world's largest purse being emptied.
The drowned in it were freed to rest. The stair-lift's curtain of
unfinished chain ran slack and swung open.

And out of the wreck of the fight came Nix's favorite arithmetic again,
grown bigger. A quench knight — a steaming tower of pit-hardened plate
that had held the pit-edge against them for half the battle — knelt at the
end with its collar in frost-cracked pieces around its knees, and when it
rose it looked down at Nix from twice Vega's height and made a sound that
its welded mouth had not been built for. It was trying to say a name. Its
own, Nix judged, or someone's it had lost.

"Take your time," Nix said. "We're going the same way. You can walk with
me and say it when it's ready."

It walked with him. It never stopped walking with him, afterward — through
the whole war above, a tireless quenched shadow at his shoulder — and by
evening his squads were calling it the Kettle, and it seemed pleased, so
far as plate can.

He did the count at the yard-gate. He had brought sixty up from the
outwork, counting the twenty new. He marched out with a hundred and forty,
and the difference was not recruits sent up from below. The difference had
been *issued to the enemy* that morning.

The supply-sleds met them at the gate, hauled up the freed lifts from
eighty floors of friendly country, and on the third sled were bread-crates
stamped with Roothollow's mark, the loaves out of a gate-town oven two
floors down that Berrin Or had taught Roothollow's own recipe — that good
dense fair-day bread, sweet as a feast morning. Nix took his loaf and
ate it slowly and thought, as he
always thought, that whatever else the rising had, it had one man who
never once forgot that an army was made of stomachs. He counted the loaves
for the new-freed twice, to be sure there were enough.

There were. There were always enough. Berrin saw to it.

Floor eighty-three was a griddle, and the rising crossed it the way you
cross a griddle: lightly, never stopping — three days and nights of it —
and only where a man who knew the fire told you to put your feet.

The Furnace Roads ran on iron gratings over open furnace-pits, road-lattice
slung across a lake of fire, glowing red from beneath so that the whole
floor was lit like a forge from the wrong side. Cinder-swifts strafed the
causeways. Slag dripped from the gantries. The road-crews who stoked it —
soot-blind, half-cooked, losing one of their own through a weakened grate
every shift — sent their boss to the gate to meet the rising, and Grint
Stokegate looked the war-council up and down and said: "Whoever walks
first walks where I say. The gratings lie. I know which ones."

"That's me," Nix said. "I'm first. I'm always first, it's because I'm
littlest, they pretend it's an honor."

He had walked point before, on the Trapdoor Fields, twenty-nine floors and
a lifetime ago, an army holding its breath behind him. He walked it again
now, reading the glow with Grint at his shoulder — *bright cherry holds,
white lies, orange sings before it gives* — and behind them the host came
over the fire two by two, and behind the host Vega's crews worked the
floor's own mercy. The word for it was *Bank.* Said right, with craft
behind it, it damped any furnace to embers, and the freed road-crews said
it like a prayer they had only just been allowed to finish. Stretch by
stretch the griddle cooled to a road. Stretch by stretch the red light
went out of the world below, and the dark that came up in its place was
just dark — honest, cool, sleepable.

The Warden fought them at the road's end, because it had to; that was the
last cruelty of the collar on the Made's own constructs, that even the
tower's furniture was driven. Cindergrate rose out of the under-grate ash
wearing the road itself as armor — the fused slag and bone of a hundred
fallen crews, a golem of everyone the floor had eaten — and it dropped the
gratings out from under the vanguard's feet, and it belched cinder, and
for a hundred heartbeats it was the worst fight since the wall. They
banked its fires out from under it. They broke it on the cooled plate. The
burned crews in it were freed, and Grint shut the master damper with his
own scalded hands, and the furnace-roads of the Made went cold for the
first time since they were laid.

At the muster on the cooled road, under a gantry-sky with no fire in it,
Ede's officers took the count of the whole host, as they did at every
lift. Nix stood in the lamplight and listened to the tally come in,
company by company, tongue by tongue, and watched the general's face do
the sum.

They had come up into the Made three floors ago with the army the stolen
world had given them. They stood now, at the foot of the fourth floor,
*larger* — larger by every sapper walked out of a wall, every chain-slave
struck loose, every road-crew off the griddle; larger by two hundred of
the enemy's own soldiery, sitting at the cook-fires relearning their
names while the Kettle ladled for them. Every other army in the history
of every world got smaller by fighting. The rising had finally, fully,
become the thing the collar-breakers had promised at the Kingsfield: an
army that grew by advancing, a line of battle that fed on its own
victories, the tower's design run backward.

"They should surrender now," Nix said to the Kettle. "Save everyone the
stairs. I hate the stairs. Have I mentioned the stairs?"

The Kettle rumbled. Overhead, at the road's end, the stair-lift to floor
eighty-four stood welded shut behind a seam of fresh iron — not barred,
not chained. *Welded.* And down through it, riding the dead cold air of
the banked furnaces, came a smell the whole muster fell silent under, one
company at a time, as it reached them. Flux, and arc-smoke, and under
those, unmistakable, scorched flesh.

Grint Stokegate spat on the cold grate and named the floor above them the
way a man names a disease.

"The Welding Halls," he said. "Where soldiers are made. Now you'll see
what the cages below were feeding, all these years."

Nix stood a long time looking up at the welded seam, and did not count
anything, and could not think of one funny thing to say.
