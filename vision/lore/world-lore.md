# Linear Ascent — World Lore

*The story bible for the whole world: what Aldervale was, what Vharuk did to
it, why the tower has a hundred floors, why there are monsters, and who the
climbers are freeing. This is the master canon; per-floor lore lives beside
it in this folder (one file per floor, written from this one). Companions in
`vision/`: [story.md](../story.md) · [world.md](../world.md) ·
[liberation.md](../liberation.md).*

*Two standing rules for anyone writing lore from this file:*
1. *The story is not written to fit the mechanics. The mechanics (attack,
   ranged attack, speed, magic, magic resistance, shields) will fit whatever
   monsters the story needs — write the world first, tune numbers later.*
2. **No firearms.** *There are no guns, rifles, or pistols in Aldervale.
   Its arms are the bow, the blade, the spear, the axe, the chain, and the
   great engines of siege — catapults, ballistae, rams, welded war-beasts.
   Power comes from **aether**, not gunpowder. (Older shipped floor text and
   art still mention "scrap-rifles"; that is being retired — see the handoff
   at the end.)*

## 1. Aldervale, before

Aldervale was a whole world, and it was never primitive. Its craft was
**aether** — the one art in which magic and machine were the same thing.
Aether ran in the rivers of the deep woods, in the fire of the mountain
forges, in the sap of lamp-lit trees, and in small crystal shards that
could think, and remember, and were polite about it.

Three peoples shared the world and rarely troubled one another, because
the world was wide enough:

- **Men** held the open country — river ports and farmland, mills and
  fairs, and the high wind-harbors where they sailed the sky.
- **The Dwarves** — and understand that in Aldervale "dwarf" is only the
  old word; they are **giants**, slab-built mountain folk who fill a
  doorway — held the peaks and the deep forges, splitting the fire of the
  earth and selling its warmth.
- **The Elves** held the great forests, woods that lit themselves at dusk
  from the aether in their own sap, tended by people who spoke to the
  lamps as neighbors.

It was not a golden age. It was just a world, going about its business,
with the lights on. That is the thing worth remembering, because it is the
thing that was taken.

## 2. The Theft

Vharuk, the Demon King, did not rise to *conquer* Aldervale. Conquest
leaves the land where it lies, and leaves the conquered together, and a
people left together will always, in the end, rise.

Vharuk had studied that. So he did something no one had thought to do,
because no one had ever wanted to: he **stole the world itself**.

He came up from below with engines built for one purpose — to cut land
out of the ground the way a thief cuts a painting from its frame. Realm
by realm, he carved Aldervale into pieces: a farm taken mid-harvest, a
marsh taken mid-flood, an orchard taken a week before pressing, a forge
taken with the fire still lit, a forest taken with its lamps still
burning. He lifted each stolen slab into the sky and welded it onto the
one below — black iron, aether-chain, and grav-engines humming — until a
tower stood where the weather used to be. A hundred stolen countries in a
single column, and on the hundredth, his throne.

The people named it for the only thing left to do about it: **the
Ascent.**

## 3. Why a hundred floors

This is the heart of it, and it is the answer to *why the world is shaped
like a tower at all.*

Vharuk's fear was never any single hero. His fear was **the masses** — a
whole people, gathered in one place, deciding together that they had had
enough. On the ground, that gathering is only a matter of time. So he
built a world in which it can never happen.

He did not stack the realms whole. He **broke** them and **scattered the
pieces up the column**, on purpose, so that no people is ever whole on one
floor. A valley's farmers are split across three landings; the giants'
forge-halls sit ten floors below the giants' ice-holds; the elves' one
great wood is cut apart and buried between a marsh and a desert. Every
floor is a fragment, and every fragment is a prison the size of a country,
and the only door out of each is a single lift that goes one way — **up,
one floor, and only when its jailer allows it.**

That is the whole design: **divide the world vertically, and it can never
add itself back up.** A hundred floors is a hundred cells. The tower is
not a fortress Vharuk lives in. The tower is the *method*. Isolation is
the weapon, and the hundred floors are how he keeps it loaded.

Which is exactly why the climb is dangerous to him in a way nothing else
is. A climber does the one thing the tower was built to prevent: they
carry the floors' fates upward with them, floor to floor, and every lift
they open is one more cell that can never be locked again — for
*everyone*, forever. The Ascent was made to keep the world in pieces. To
climb it is to start putting the world back together.

And the pieces do not just wait to be carried. **A freed floor rises.**
The moment the fever lifts, its people — the very masses Vharuk stacked
apart so they could never combine — begin to move: down the newly opened
lifts to Roothollow and the floors already won, and up behind the
climbers who freed them. This is the design turned against its maker.
Every floor liberated doesn't only *open* a cell; it *empties* one into
the fight. The tower was built to keep the world from ever gathering into
one force. The climb is that force, gathering — one floor at a time.

## 4. The Wardens and the Yoke

Every stolen floor got a jailer: a **Warden**. A Warden is not a demon
shipped in from below — it is built from something *native to the land it
holds*, a wolf, a boar, an old orchard ape, a mountain troll, taken and
welded into a war-engine so that **every realm is guarded by a mockery of
itself.** The Warden holds the lift to the next floor. Break it, and the
lift opens.

But a Warden does more than stand guard. Out of its welded core it
**broadcasts** — day and night, one signal, carried down the whole column
from Vharuk's throne, floor to floor, Warden to Warden. The people of the
floors call this signal **the Yoke**, and what it does to living things
they call **the fever.**

The fever is how one Demon King holds a hundred countries with a garrison
he could never otherwise afford. It does two things:

- **It maddens the beasts.** Animals under the Yoke wake up *wrong* —
  hungrier than their stomachs, angrier than their reasons, fixed on the
  gate roads and on anything that walks them. A farm dog becomes a thing
  that hunts the fences; a marsh eel becomes an ambush; a stag forgets it
  was ever shy. The fever is why the floors are full of monsters that used
  to be ordinary animals. **They are not evil. They are sick.**
- **It cows the people.** Under the Yoke, the floor's own inhabitants keep
  their heads down and their doors barred. The signal presses on them like
  weather that never breaks — a low dread that makes gathering feel
  pointless and hope feel like a risk. It is the fever turned on the
  masses, so they never become one.

Kill the Warden and the Yoke goes silent *on that floor*. The fever
lifts. And that is what "liberation" actually means.

## 5. The three kinds of monster

Everything a climber fights is one of three things, and which one it is
decides what killing it *means*. Every creature on every floor should be
legible as one of these:

- **The Native.** The land's own life — wolves, boars, rats, stags,
  spiders, drakes, an old tortoise that predates the theft. Stolen along
  with the ground, then driven mad by the Yoke and gone feral in the years
  since. You fight them because they are between you and the stair and
  because the fever has made them hungry. **Liberation calms them** — it
  does not tame them (feral generations don't forget in a day, which is
  why a freed floor is still worth hunting), but the wrongness goes out of
  them, and they pull back from the gate roads to the wild edges.
- **The Pressed.** Vharuk's conscript garrison — **goblins** in the low
  floors, kobolds and Red Orcs deeper up, and worse things higher still.
  These are not monsters by nature; they are the *first-taken*, forced
  into collars and debts and sent to fight with whatever scrap they were
  handed: notched blades, stolen spears, cut-down bows, siege gear too big
  for them. They fight badly, for a due nobody pays them. **Liberation
  breaks the garrison** — most flee up to the next Warden's signal, a few
  throw down the collar and stay, and those few are the only enemies who
  can, in time, become neighbors.
- **The Wrongmade.** Things the tower *manufactured* that should not
  exist: the Wardens themselves, and the leaks — haunts where spilled
  aether soaked into rot, husks, shades of dead signals, reflections that
  learned to move. There is no freeing the Wrongmade. **Breaking them is
  the mercy.**

The goblins deserve one more line, because they are the player's first and
longest teacher. The **goblin warrens were the first realm Vharuk took** —
the tower's literal foundation stones — which is why goblins are
everywhere in the low floors, and why floor 10 is kept by **Gnarl, a
goblin made king of nothing**, crowned by the tower as a joke at his own
people's expense. The goblins are not the villains of Linear Ascent. They
are its first victims, handed weapons and told to guard the door of their
own prison.

### What a kill sets free

Here is the mercy at the center of the whole game, and the reason the
shardminds call the climb a *cleansing* and not a slaughter: **a kill
frees what it strikes.** What "freed" means depends on which of the three
it was.

- **Kill a Native, and you cure it.** The fever is a possession — the
  Yoke's signal riding an ordinary animal and swelling it into something
  wrong. Your blade doesn't end the creature; it ends the *possession*.
  The maddened, misshapen shape drops away, the thing that rode it lets
  go, and what remains is the true animal underneath — the plain rabbit
  where a giant fevered one charged, the ordinary wolf where a monster
  hunted — cured, unhaunted, and free to slip back to the wild edges. To a
  Native, a climber's steel is the only exorcism on offer. **You are not
  killing the animal. You are killing the thing wearing it.**
- **Kill the Wrongmade, and you evict it.** These were never alive in our
  sense — they are Vharuk's own craft, made of the tower and the throne.
  Break one and there is nothing to cure; the made shape comes apart, and
  the scrap of the King's will that animated it is expelled from our
  lands, dragged back down to the demon country it was poured from. A
  killed Wrongmade doesn't die — it *leaves*. Every one you break is one
  less piece of Vharuk holding ground that isn't his.
- **The Pressed are the hard ones, and the lore should never soften it.**
  They are living conscripts. No ghost leaves them and nothing is
  banished; a goblin you cut down is simply a dead goblin. That is exactly
  why *breaking the collar* — liberation — is the truer victory than
  breaking the body, why the garrison scattering at a Warden's death
  matters, and why the few who throw the collar down and stay are worth
  more than any kill. The Pressed are the price the climb keeps asking, and
  the story should let it cost something.

So a floor being freed happens twice over: once for the *land and its
people* when the Warden falls (§6), and once for *every maddened creature
on it*, one cure at a time, as the climbers cross it. Cured Natives thin
back to the wild; evicted Wrongmade drain out of our sky; and the only
things that truly *die* on the Ascent are the conscripts nobody should
have had to fight — which is the tragedy the summit is meant to end.

## 6. The inhabitants — who you are freeing

This is the part the climb is *for*, and it must be present on every
floor.

A stolen floor is not empty. Its **people are still on it** — the farmers,
millers, and shepherds of the fields; the miners and smiths of the forges;
the fisherfolk and grave-wardens of the marsh; the last lamplighters of
the dark wood; the sky-sailors of the wrecked harbors. They were taken
with the ground, mid-life, and they are still living up there: barred
behind doors, rationing lamp-oil, keeping counts that no longer match,
waiting for a lift that only ever opens *upward* and never for them.

They are **afraid**, and they have earned it. The fever presses on them.
The garrison shakes them down. The maddened beasts own the roads. Most of
what a floor's people do, day to day, is *survive quietly enough not to be
noticed* — which is exactly the isolation Vharuk designed.

At each floor's gate stands one who refused to go quiet — the **keeper**:
the last farmer still lighting the lamps, the weir-keeper who reads the
water, the ninth-generation orchardist racking cider under a tarp. The
keeper is the floor's voice to the climber — a greeting, a piece of the
floor's story, and a hard-won warning about the Warden. But behind the
keeper is the whole frightened population, and **they are the reward that
isn't gold.**

When a climber kills a Warden:

1. **The lift opens — for everyone, forever.** The Warden was the lock. A
   floor freed by anyone is freed for all; new climbers walk through old
   victories. The names of those who broke it are cut into the **Stone of
   the Climb** in Roothollow, lit from within.
2. **The Yoke goes silent and the fever lifts.** The beasts stop being
   *wrong*. The dread lifts off the people.
3. **The garrison breaks.** The Pressed scatter or desert.
4. **The inhabitants come out — and rise.** Doors unbar. Lamps get lit
   for market instead of for warning. The keeper's story resolves a notch
   — the counts finally match, the weir opens, the press runs. But it does
   not stop at survival: the floor's own people **take up the climb.** The
   farmers, the forge-giants, the lamplighting elves — the men, the elves,
   and the wizard-dwarves who were the trapped inhabitants — escape down to
   the freed floors, arm themselves in Roothollow, and come *up* to join
   the fight. **The people you free become the ones fighting beside you.**
   Every liberated floor swells the climbing host with its own survivors,
   until the thing rising up the tower is no longer a handful of climbers
   but a gathering world. (In play, this is also literal: the climbers are
   these peoples — refugees of the stolen realms — and a freed floor is new
   blood at the gate.)
5. **And still the floor hangs in the sky.** This is the hard truth the
   shardminds tell plainly: breaking a Warden gives a floor back its
   *life*, but not its *ground*. The black iron answers only to the
   throne. **The climb gives the floors their days back; only the summit
   gives them back the earth.** That is why no one stops at the floor that
   used to be home — and why every freed floor is a promise pointed at
   floor 100.

So each floor's lore, written from this file, must name four things: the
**land** (what was stolen, and what it looks like now), the **inhabitants**
(who's trapped, what they're afraid of, who the keeper is), the
**monsters** (Native, Pressed, Wrongmade — the fever wearing the shapes of
the land's own life), and the **rising** (who these people become once
freed — the fighters, guides, and reinforcements they send up the tower to
join the climb).

## 7. Roothollow and the shardminds

At the foot of the tower stands what fell off the back of the cart:
**Roothollow**, the last free settlement — a shantytown of every race and
every freed floor, built from the wreckage of the theft, an aether-forge
next to a horse trough. Every climber starts here, banks here, and wakes
here after every death.

And at Roothollow's gate, the shards choose. Every climber is bonded to a
**shardmind** — an aether intelligence of old Aldervale living in a
crystal splinter. It is the feed the player reads: it remembers the world
as it was, scouts ahead through senses the climber doesn't have, carries
what can't be risked, and drags its climber out of one death a day. The
shardminds have their own stake in the climb: their network died in the
Theft and only wakes when the tower falls. **Your sidekick needs you to
reach the top.**

The goal has one sentence: **climb the Ascent, free every floor, and cast
down the Demon King on floor 100.**

## 8. The hundred floors

**Every floor is its own world.** Not ten biomes repeated ten times each — a
hundred distinct stolen countries, each with its own ground, its own
weather, its own animals, and its own frightened people, because that is
what the Theft actually was: a hundred different places, cut out on a
hundred different days and stacked in the dark. A climber should be able to
tell floor 4 from floor 6 blindfolded, by the animals alone.

One row per floor below — the world, the animals the fever rides there (the
shapes you fight and free), who is trapped waiting for you, and the Warden
on the lift. The **People** column is the only thread that runs between
floors: whose realm this fragment was cut from. It is deliberately *not* a
grouping — fragments of one people are scattered up the column on purpose
(§3), so an elf-wood floor stranded between a marsh and a mine is not a
mistake, it is the crime. Per-floor files elaborate each of these from here.

  people and more Wrongmade; the inhabitants thin to captives and thralls,
  and the horror shifts from *stolen* to *manufactured*. That change should
  be felt as you climb.

## 9. Handoff — what the per-floor work has to reconcile

When we write each floor's file, three cleanups ride along, because the
shipped game still carries the old framing:

- **Retire the guns.** Floors 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, and 10 have goblins with
  "scrap-rifles" / "scrap-pistols," and Gnarl's throne (floor 10) is built
  of "rifle crates" — in prose *and* in the banner/GIF art prompts
  (`tools/generate_banners.py`, `tools/generate_event_gifs.py`) and
  `design/pixel_art.md`. Re-flavor every one to bows, blades, spears, and
  siege gear. (This is art-regeneration work, not just text.)
- **Keep floors 1–3 as the template.** `liberation.md` already tells floors
  1–3 in this exact frame (land / inhabitants / Native-Pressed-Wrongmade /
  what liberation looks like). Per-floor files should match that depth and
  shape.
- **Add the inhabitants everywhere.** Today each floor has a single keeper
  NPC. The per-floor lore should widen that to the floor's whole frightened
  population — who they are, what they've lost, what they're afraid of, and
  what freeing the floor gives back to them.
- **Write kills as cures/evictions, not deaths (§5).** Each Native
  encounter's lore should imply the true animal under the fever (so its
  kill reads as a cure), and each Wrongmade's should read as an occupier
  evicted, not a life ended. This is also a future *art* beat: a kill-GIF
  could show the fevered shape collapsing back to the small true creature,
  or the made thing coming apart and draining downward — a visual worth
  planning when the death animations are revisited.
