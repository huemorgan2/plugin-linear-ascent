# World construction — how the Ascent is actually built

*CANON (v2 adopted, 2026-08-04, plan 011). The dials sections remain the
living iteration surface; world-lore.md and the book's style-guide carry
the condensed rules that bind all writing.*

*v2 rulings (yours): the tower is NOT weakened by Warden-deaths — it is
freed by them. Wardens keep the light out, not the tower up; killing one
lifts its floor's veil, and killing the demon frees the whole ladder. And
the trays are lenses — land deepest in the middle (mining country),
shallower at the rim (living country), so people cluster at the edge
where the sun sometimes reaches and the clouds rain.*

---

## 1. The picture in one paragraph

The Ascent is not a building. It is a hundred stolen countries stacked on
a loom of magic thread. Each floor is a tray of land roughly a hundred
miles across and a few miles of sky deep, resting on a woven sheet of
strand — a material that cannot break because it is a bound oath, not a
metal. Around each tray's rim stand needle-thin pillars, cross-braced
with triangles of the same thread; between one floor and the next runs a
single climbing spine that from ten miles away looks like spider silk.
The sides are open. Clouds drift out of one country's rim and past
another's. From the plains below, the tower is a ladder of paper-thin
lands fading up into the blue, and no one has ever seen the top.

---

## 2. The Strand (the infinitely strong material)

**What it is.** Not mined, not forged — *bound*. The Strand is will made
solid: an oath of holding, spun and pulled. It has no grain, no rust, no
temperature you can feel. Black iron appears only where the Strand meets
ordinary matter — the fittings, the anchors, the gate arches — because
ordinary matter needs something it can be bolted to.

**The oath is unconditional.** The Strand does not need tending. The
binding was spoken once, whole, at the cutting — Vharuk's masterwork,
the one honest thing he ever made — and it holds whether he lives or
not. The tower CANNOT fall. It was told to stand, and it obeys, and it
will still be standing when everyone in this story is a name on a
stone. This matters at the end: the rising does not bring the tower
down; it takes the tower away from him. A hundred countries do not
want their prison demolished — they are standing on it. They want the
keys. (The book already believes this: "And then? The tower stands.
Dead iron, root to crown" — chapter 18. And the scram kills the
broadcast, not the standing.)

**What the Wardens actually hold.** Not the structure — the light.
Each Warden tends its floor's veil (§5): the manufactured gloom that
keeps a country dim, wet, and fit for the fever's creatures, slowly
suffocating everything that isn't theirs. Kill the Warden and the
floor does not shake. It *brightens*. From the Girdle-sea a freed
floor is visible the hour it is freed — one hairline of the tower
turning from grey to gold. That is how Roothollow learns the war is
being won.

**Why nothing grows on it.** The Strand refuses claim. Roots find no
purchase, rust finds no iron, nails find no bite, moss slides off. It
cannot be owned, drilled, welded to, or carved. This is Vharuk's nature
in material form: everything his, nothing shared.

**The loophole (see §7).** You cannot bind TO the Strand — but you can
bind AROUND it. A collar that grips by geometry rather than adhesion, a
basket woven closed about a cable, a clamp that owes its hold to
friction and its own shape. Everything people have ever built on the
strings is built on this loophole, and the loophole is the theme: the
freed learn to live around what cannot be owned.

> Dials: the material's name ("the Strand" is a placeholder — other
> candidates: the Weft, godthread, the Chain-oath). Whether the Strand
> gives any sign at all when its maker dies (I propose one, once: on
> the day the scram falls, every thread in the tower rings one low
> note, and never speaks again).

## 3. The bones (engineering with infinite strength)

Infinite strength does not mean you can skip structure — it means every
member can be absurdly thin. The load still has to travel somewhere.

- **The weft.** Each floor's deck is not a slab but a woven lattice of
  Strand, loomed tight as sailcloth, with a country's worth of rock,
  soil, rivers, and mountain roots laid over it. This is why a floor
  seen edge-on looks paper-thin: it *is* thin. A country resting on a
  sheet of cloth that cannot tear.
- **The rim pillars.** Around each tray's edge, a colonnade of pillars
  no thicker than a keep's tower, carrying each floor's weight down in
  compression, floor to floor, into the bedrock of the drowned world.
  From afar they read as a shimmer of hairlines, not a wall.
- **The stays.** Triangles of Strand-cable webbing the pillars and the
  trays — tension bracing against wind and sway, like a mast's
  shrouds. The triangle is the honest shape of the tower: it appears
  in Warden sigils, gate arches, and the freed's superstitions.
- **The spine.** Between each floor and the next, ONE climbing tower —
  the stair-lift. Four miles of helical stair and counterweighted
  lift-cages wound around a master cable. From outside: a thread with
  a bead of light crawling up it. And the spines are OFFSET — floor
  12's spine rises at the east rim, floor 13's at the southwest — so
  no single bore runs the height of the tower (Vharuk's security),
  and every army must cross each country to reach the next stair.
  *This quietly explains the whole shape of the game and the book's
  logistics: land at one rim, march a week, climb at the far rim.*

**How you actually climb (the walkthrough).** There is no single tower
you ride from bottom to top. Each gap has its own stair-lift: it stands
on the lower country's soil, runs the miles up, and bolts into the
underside of the floor above — you come out through a gate arch in
that country's GROUND, like coming up out of a cellar into a new land.
So every floor has two doors:
  - the **down-gate** — the arch where people arrive from the floor
    below (a hole in this country's ground);
  - the **up-gate** — the tower standing on this country's soil that
    leads to the floor above.
They are deliberately not above one another. The climb through the
whole Ascent zigzags: ride up, come out at one rim, cross the country
— days or weeks — reach the far rim, ride again. Gate-towns, tolls,
markets, and the book's entire supply-line logic grow at the two doors.

**Why not one central spine?** If every gap's tower stood at the
center, stacked, the exit of one and the entrance of the next would be
a hundred paces apart: no one would ever need to cross a country, one
garrison per center would control the whole tower, and the rim-folk
would never see a climber. The OFFSET is what forces the journey. But
placement is a per-floor dial, and a minority of floors should use the
cruel setting:
  - **Rim gates (the norm).** Both doors in the Rimlands — light,
    towns, trade. The crossing runs rim to rim, skirting or daring the
    Deep Middle.
  - **Deep-middle gates (the terror floors).** The up-gate stands in
    the country's dark heart — on an island in the central sea, at the
    bottom of the mine-lands — so the only way off the floor is a
    journey INTO it. Vharuk chose this for the realms he wanted no one
    to leave.

> Dials: pillar count per floor (dozens? hundreds?); whether any floor
> has lost pillars (a tilted country — a floor that dips at one coast,
> its lakes gathered at the low rim); whether the spine's stair is
> inside the cable or wound around it; WHICH floors get deep-middle
> up-gates (I propose ten to fifteen, the dark floors and two
> surprises).

## 4. The trays (shape — and your sun problem, solved honestly)

You are right that flat disks leak: at dawn and dusk the sun rakes
level, and any open slot lights end to end. In v2 the work is shared
three ways: the outline is a country, the tray is a lens, and the
darkness is a Warden's daily labor (§5) — which means light is
something a floor can WIN BACK, not something the architecture denies
forever. Three decisions, taken together:

**a) The trays are country-shaped, not round.** Each floor was CUT from
the world, and it keeps the outline of the realm that was stolen —
coastlines, promontories, the crook of an old border river. The tower
is not a stack of coins; it is a cairn of countries, each silhouette
different, none aligned. The eldest can stand at a rim and recognize
their homeland's shape in the shadow the floor above casts at noon.
Vharuk cut along borders — he weaponized the borders themselves.

**b) Every tray is a lens, not a plate.** The land is bent shallowly
toward the middle. At the center the fill runs deepest — miles of
rock, ore, and old dark to sink mines into. Toward the rim it thins,
but never past building-and-farming depth. Note what the lens does and
does not govern: it shapes the FILL, not the scenery on top. A
mountain range in a floor's middle is MORE believable now, not less —
the middle is where the fill is deep enough to hold mountain roots.
The existing hundred landscapes survive; what moves is the people.
Two worlds per floor fall out of this:
  - **The Rimlands — where people belong.** Light comes in off the
    open slot, the clouds that ride in over the rim drop their rain
    there first, and twice a day the Kindling burns along the edge.
    Farms, gate-towns, the stair-lift arches: all of it clusters in a
    band a few miles deep around each country's coastline of air.
  - **The Deep Middle — where people go and come back changed.** The
    interior bends down and away from the light. Mine country, wild
    country, fever country: the veil is thickest there, the monsters
    den there, and the best ore in the tower is at the bottom of it.
    Every floor's economy is the same brave errand — rim-folk going
    inward and down, and trying to come out again.

**c) Light lives at the rim.** Even with no veil at all, a
hundred-mile country lit from its open sides is brightest at its
edges — a silver day in the Rimlands, an honest dusk inland, true
dark over the Deep Middle. The veil (§5) presses that whole gradient
down into gloom; killing the Warden lifts it back up. And the
Kindling holds regardless: at true sunrise and sunset a gold blade
rakes level through the slot and burns along the Rimlands for a
minute or two. People gather for it. Some floors built religions on
it; some Wardens scheduled work details to keep crowds from it. A
minute of real gold a day for eighty years — that is a wound you can
build a rising on.

**d) The stagger makes exceptions, and exceptions are stories.** Because
the outlines differ and nothing aligns, some floors have:
  - **Sunskirts** — an outer ring or a single promontory that sticks
    out past the floor above into full true daylight. Real sun, real
    stars, real seasons on one peninsula of an otherwise Yoked land.
    Naturally forbidden. Naturally where smugglers, lovers, and the
    Wake go.
  - **Undershadows** — where a wide floor above overhangs a narrow one
    below, a permanent dusk country. Some of our existing dark floors
    (fungal, lantern-lit, pale-court) now have a *reason*.

> Dials: how deep the Deep Middle runs on the deepest floors (I
> propose up to two miles of fill at center on the mining floors);
> how wide the Rimlands band is per floor (two miles on the cruel
> floors, ten on the kind ones); which specific floors get Sunskirts.

## 5. The Veil (what a Warden is for), and the varying heights

**The Veil.** The tower does not need Wardens to stand (§2). Vharuk
needs them for something meaner. Each Warden tends its floor's veil —
a manufactured lid of cloud, smoke, and false light that keeps the
country dim. Under the veil the slot-light dies inland, the Rimlands'
silver day fails, and the floor is paid back with the Yoke's
counterfeit: a grey, sourceless daylight with no sun in it and no
honest night behind it. The gloom is not weather; it is policy —
  - it suits the fever's creatures, which den and hunt in the dark;
  - it starves what it does not suit — thin harvests, pale trees, the
    slow suffocation already written into every floor file's "Under
    Yoke" flora notes (this doc finally gives those a mechanism);
  - and it cannot cross the Shear. The rim gales tear any cloud that
    reaches the slot — which is why the Kindling survives on even the
    darkest floor, why farming stays possible in the Rimlands, and
    why the one place a Warden's writ frays is the edge of its own
    country.

**What breaking a Warden does.** The floor does not tremble. The
veil, untended, unravels in about a day — and the country gets its
light back: the silver rim-day reaching inland, real weather, real
nights, real stars in the slot. Liberation is VISIBLE from the
Girdle-sea, floor by floor. And when the throne falls and the
scram-bar kills the broadcast, the last veils die together — the
whole ladder of lands turning gold at once. The tower is not
destroyed at the end; it is freed, root to crown, and people keep
living on it — which is exactly what the trials chapter, the
gazetteer, and the epilogue already show.

**Heights vary (the sky-sentence).** The gap between floors is not
uniform. Alongside the veil, each country got a sky-sentence:

- **Low-vaulted floors** (a mile of air or less): the lid presses. Mist
  worlds, whisper worlds, floors where weather is only fog and drizzle
  because there is no room for storms. Good for the Waste floors and
  the drowned commons — oppression you can feel on your neck.
- **Standard floors** (three to five miles): room for real weather —
  cloud decks, inland rains off the cold lakes, proper winds.
- **High-vaulted floors** (six to eight miles): cathedral skies. The
  boss floors and the proud realms — Vharuk liked his trophies lit
  and airy. Thunderheads, snow that falls for a day, the underside of
  the next floor a faint slate ceiling you can genuinely forget.

**The lid.** On a standard floor the next floor's underside is visible
— a smooth, dark heaven the Yoke's false light paints over. People
stopped seeing it the way you stop seeing your own nose. On high floors
it fades to a rumor; on low floors it is a fact you live under. Children
raised on deep-bowl floors do not believe in stars.

> Dials: the exact spread of heights; whether height correlates with
> tier (I propose NO — a low-vaulted floor at 71 is scarier than any
> pattern); which floors are the cathedral skies.

## 6. Air, water, weather (the jar canon)

- **Weather in a jar.** Each shelf's air is bound the way everything
  else is: part of the floor's binding, a held breath per country. The
  binding preserves the climate the land was stolen with — a tundra
  floor stays tundra at any altitude, a monsoon floor keeps its rains.
  That is why biomes ignore height, and why the eldest say the weather
  is the one thing he could not improve on, only imprison.
- **The Shear.** Where wind squeezes through the open slot between two
  floors it accelerates — rim gales that can lift a wagon. This is why
  nobody simply flies out of a floor, why birds hug the interior, and
  why the spines are engineered like storm-anchors. The rim is loud.
  Ten steps inland from any lip, the Shear dies to a whisper — the
  slot's own violence is the prison's cheapest guard.
- **Water runs inward.** The lens bends every river toward the Deep
  Middle: rain falls hardest on the Rimlands (the clouds ride in over
  the rim and drop their load first), gathers inland, and pools over
  the deepest fill as each floor's central sea — a dark lake above
  the mines, home to the worst of the fever's swimmers, holy and
  feared in equal measure. Floors drain through weft-sluices bored at
  the bottom of their central seas: one country's overflow falls as
  the UNDER-RAIN of the next — rain that arrives out of the roof of
  the sky. Some floors bless it; some tax the plunge-pools; every
  floor knows the rule of the chain: foul your sea and you poison
  your neighbor below.
- **The Long Rain and the Girdle-sea.** What the sluices spill past
  the last tray falls the whole way — the Long Rain — and feeds a
  ring of grey water around the tower's foot. Roothollow is a port on
  it. The drowned old world is under it. Fishermen there pull up roof
  tiles, church bells, and coins of realms that no longer have names.

> Dials: whether the Yoke's false day keeps true-sun time (I propose
> YES — the tower steals even the clock, so bells agree across floors);
> whether any floor's binding is failing enough to leak air (a thin-sky
> floor where fires burn low and heads ache — dread without a monster).

## 7. Life on the strings (the Ringworld chapter, but ours)

Nothing grows ON the Strand. Everything grows AROUND it.

- **Knot-gardens.** Dust settles in the cable junctions; birds nest at
  the knots; wind-blown soil packs into the saddles where triangles
  meet. After eighty years some stay-junctions carry gardens the size
  of farmyards — soil held in a net of bird lime, rot, and rope, with
  crooked apple trees growing sideways out of a knot four miles above
  a country. Nobody planted them. The tower grew its own hedgerows in
  its armpits, and the Strand never felt a thing.
- **Bead-towns.** The loophole made architecture: friction-collars of
  timber and woven cord, clamped AROUND pillars and stays, gripping by
  shape alone. Houses hung like beads on a wire, whole streets in a
  vertical string, ladders between them, cisterns catching the Long
  Rain. Built by rim-folk who worked the pillars, escaped debtors,
  and the Made floors' freed — people the trays had no room or law
  for. A bead-town owns nothing under it and nothing over it. They
  are the freest places in the tower and the easiest to destroy: cut
  one rope. Wardens burned unauthorized collars for decades; the
  surviving towns are the ones that learned to be invisible from
  above.
- **The stay-roads.** Where stays run from one floor's rim toward
  another's, rope-runners cross between floors WITHOUT the spine —
  half-smuggler, half-saint, walking a hair of oath-metal over four
  miles of air with the Shear plucking at them. Capacity: a person, a
  message, a satchel of medicine. NOT an army — the book's spine
  logistics stay intact. But the Wake knows the stay-roads, and now
  the reader knows how the Wake always knew things first.
- **Upward farming.** Some floors anchored to the pillars at their own
  rims and built UP: terrace-collars every fifty feet, hanging vines,
  drop-spindle granaries, windmills bolted around (never to) the
  pillar shafts, riding the Shear for power. From the tray they look
  like ivy on a needle. The engineering is the loophole again — the
  pillar carries a country and never notices the village gripping it.

> Dials: how legal any of this is post-rising (I propose: the trials
> chapter's world inherits the question — who OWNS a bead-town?);
> whether one bead-town matters enough to name and use in the book;
> whether stay-roads should appear in the game as travel/quest seeds.

## 8. What you see (the money shots)

- **From inside a floor:** a country. Horizons, weather, a visible dark
  heaven behind the veil's false light. At the rim: the Rimlands'
  farms running to the edge of the air, the Shear howling in the slot,
  and twice a day, the Kindling — a blade of real gold along the rim.
  Inland: the land bending slowly down toward the dark of the Deep
  Middle and its still, black central sea.
- **From a Sunskirt:** the one photograph of freedom — true blue sky
  overhead, the next country's underside a dark coastline hanging in
  that sky, and the plunge of open air below your feet.
- **From the Girdle-sea / Roothollow:** the tower does not read as a
  wall anymore. It reads as a ladder of thin lands — each floor a
  hairline of coast and cloud, pillars a silver shimmer at the edges,
  one thread of a spine catching the light, the stack fading up into
  the blue like a ladder sinking into deep water. Clouds slide OUT of
  floors, drag their shadows across the disks below, and dissolve.
  You never see the top. Not because it is far — because the air
  itself gives up before you do.
- **At night, from outside:** the trays go dark (cities are dust at a
  hundred miles), but the spines glow faintly along their length, and
  bead-towns show as strings of orange sparks in the black — the
  tower's only honest stars.
- **During the rising, from outside:** the war is legible from the
  Girdle-sea. A veiled floor is a grey hairline; a freed floor is a
  gold one. Floor after floor, the ladder changes color from below —
  and on the day the scram-bar falls, the last grey rungs turn all at
  once. Roothollow does not need a courier to know it is over.

## 9. What this changes in what we already have

Honest list, so we can decide with open eyes:

1. **The "black iron cliff-face" exterior canon dies.** world-lore's
   "measure of the thing," the style-guide scale canon, and roughly a
   dozen chapter passages describe the tower from outside as an iron
   wall filling the sky. That was our fix for masonry-tower images; it
   is now wrong twice over. Replace with the ladder-of-lands imagery.
   In the same breath, world-lore's "breaking the Wardens weakens the
   tower" sentence dies too — replaced by the veil mechanism. The
   BOOK needs almost nothing here: chapter 18 already says "the tower
   stands, dead iron, root to crown," and 38's scram already kills
   the broadcast, not the standing.
2. **The "ceiling too far to see" line inverts.** New canon: the lid is
   visible and ignored (standard floors), oppressive (low floors), a
   rumor (high floors). Several chapters lean on the old version.
3. **Plates:** front-tower, front-roothollow, plate-01, plate-02,
   plate-30, plate-41, plate-19a sell the iron wall and need
   regeneration. 38a's enclosed "iron hall between floors" becomes an
   open shelf crossing — arguably a better chapter and plate.
4. **New floor-lore hooks, free:** every floor file can gain a few
   lines — its sky height, its veil's weight, how wide its Rimlands
   run, what its Deep Middle hides (ore, central sea, dens), and any
   Sunskirt / Undershadow / bead-town / stay-road it owns. The
   existing "Under Yoke" flora notes finally get their mechanism, and
   every "When it falls" section gets its image: the veil lifting.
5. **The game gains systems for free:** the Kindling as a daily
   event, Sunskirts as forbidden zones, stay-roads as smuggler
   travel, under-rain plunge-pool tolls, bead-town quests,
   deep-middle mining runs, and liberation that visibly brightens a
   floor the player freed.
6. **Nothing about the plot breaks.** The spine choke-point, the
   march-across-each-floor structure, the eighty-year Theft, the
   scram, and all keystones survive untouched — and the veil actually
   IMPROVES the liberation theme: freeing a floor now has a face. The
   only organ replaced is one lore sentence about Wardens holding the
   tower up. Everything else is skin.

## 10. Three closing rulings (small, load-bearing)

**a) What the scram kills, and what survives.** The engines can never
be woken again (ch. 18) — so the ledger must balance:
  - SURVIVES: the Strand and every pillar, stay, and weft (the oath is
    unconditional); each floor's bound air and stolen climate (same
    oath); the stair-lifts (counterweighted machinery — muscle, water,
    and iron, never Yoke-work — a lift is a well-wheel, not a spell).
  - DIES: the veils (light returns); the Yoke's false daylight (real
    light means real nights — hence the epilogue's honest lanterns);
    the fever-broadcast; the welding-fire; the shard-choir's captor.
  One sentence of this belongs in the book's appendix on the Yoke, so
  no reader ever wonders whether the freed won a tomb.

**b) The countries can never be put back — but they can heal.** The
same oath that keeps the tower standing keeps it standing FOREVER.
The pits where the stolen realms once lay are scar-lakes now; the
Girdle-sea is eighty years of rain that never found its old rivers.
Nobody goes home, because home is miles of sky above where home used
to be. The freed keep the tower not by choice but by physics — the
rising could only ever win the keys, never the ground back. BUT the
ground they keep is not a tomb: lift the veil, kill the curse, and a
country starts to mend. True light reaches inland, the pale trees
put out honest green, the harvests come back heavy, the fever's dens
empty year by year. The floors were never dead lands — they were
lands held under a thumb. Eighty years of gloom against a country's
whole deep history: the country wins, given light. The epilogue's
truth is both halves said together, quietly, by someone old: we are
never going down again — and up here, for the first time in eighty
years, things GROW.

**c) The Made floors are round.** Floors 81-100 are Vharuk's own
construction, not stolen — so they are the only trays in the cairn
without a country's ragged outline. Perfect circles. Climbing out of
the last stolen land, you know you have crossed into the Made because
the coastline stops being a coastline and becomes geometry. No border
was ever drawn by a people here; the shape itself is the warning.

## 11. The per-floor card (the iteration interface)

When we fold this into canon, every floor gets four new lines in its
lore file. Propose we fix the vocabulary now:

```
Sky:     low / standard / cathedral   (the sky-sentence, in miles)
Veil:    heavy / thin / torn / lifted   (the Warden's habit — and the
         reward for breaking it)
Land:    how wide the Rimlands run; what the Deep Middle hides
         (ore, central sea, dens)
Gates:   where the down-gate and up-gate stand (rim / inland /
         deep-middle) — and so the shape of the crossing
Strings: any Sunskirt, Undershadow, bead-town, stay-road, knot-garden
```

One more rule the cards enforce: **towns live at the rim by default.**
Any settlement written inland is an exception and owes the reader one
sentence of why — a mining town that lives by lamplight, an
Undershadow people who never knew better, a shrine on the central
sea. Same law we already applied to out-of-place monsters.

Four words per floor and the whole tower becomes navigable — for the
writers, for the game, and for the reader who never sees this doc.
